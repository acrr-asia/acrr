The [third entry](/blog/arguments-and-their-structure) took arguments apart into premises and conclusions and separated deductive validity from inductive strength. It treated validity as a property one checks argument by argument. This entry steps back and asks what kind of discipline studies that property, what its basic objects are and why a handful of patterns can settle the validity of arguments about anything at all.

The entry opens with what logic is and what it is not (Section 1), then introduces its basic unit, the statement (Section 2), and the simplest ways of building statements from other statements (Section 3). Section 4 defines the relations that logic studies between statements: consistency, entailment and equivalence, and shows that they are one idea seen from different sides. Section 5 explains why validity depends on logical form, and Section 6 uses consistency checking to solve a classic kind of puzzle, by hand and with a short Python 3 program. No symbols are needed beyond the few listed on the [series page](/series/critical-thinking); the next three entries make them precise.

## 1. What logic is

### 1.1 Correct reasoning, not the psychology of reasoning

In the broad sense, logic is the study of the principles of good reasoning, and in that sense it overlaps almost entirely with critical thinking. In the narrower sense used in this and the next three entries, **deductive logic** studies which conclusions follow necessarily from which premises, and which sets of statements can be true together.

Logic is a *normative* discipline: it says how one ought to reason if one wants conclusions that cannot be false when the premises are true. It is not a description of how people do reason. That second question belongs to the psychology of reasoning, an empirical science that measures what people actually infer, how often they err and how their performance depends on age, training or the way a problem is worded. A well-known result of that science is Wason's selection task, in which most participants fail to choose the observations that could falsify a simple conditional rule [Wason 1968]. The finding matters to logic teachers, but it is not a law of logic: a rule of logic would stay correct even if nobody followed it, in the same way that an arithmetic identity stays correct when people make mistakes in mental arithmetic.

The distinction has a practical consequence. "Most engineers would conclude X from these data" is a claim about people and is settled by surveying them. "X follows from these data" is a claim about the data and X, and is settled by logic.

### 1.2 Topic neutrality

Consider three arguments.

1. If the build passes all the tests, it is deployed. The build passes all the tests. So the build is deployed.
2. If the supply voltage drops below 3.0 V, the controller resets. The supply voltage drops below 3.0 V. So the controller resets.
3. If $n$ is divisible by 6, then $n$ is even. $n$ is divisible by 6. So $n$ is even.

They concern software, electronics and arithmetic, yet each is valid for the same reason: each has the shape "if $P$ then $Q$; $P$; therefore $Q$", the pattern called *modus ponens* in the third entry. Nothing about tests, voltages or numbers is needed to see that the conclusion follows.

This is **topic neutrality**: the principles of logic apply to reasoning about any subject matter. The laws of thermodynamics hold for physical systems and the theorems of number theory hold for integers, but a valid pattern of inference is valid whatever its statements are about. That generality is what makes logic useful as a tool for every other discipline, including the engineering ones.

### 1.3 Necessity

Logic also differs from the empirical sciences in the kind of truth it deals with. A statement is **contingent** if it could have been true and could have been false, depending on how the world is. "The speed of sound in dry air at 20 °C is about 343 m/s" is contingent: it is established by measurement, and a world with different physics could have made it false. A **logical truth** is a statement that is true in every possible situation because of its form alone, such as "either the sensor is faulty or it is not faulty" or "if the valve is open, then the valve is open". No measurement could refute it, and none is needed to establish it.

The same contrast applies to arguments. A valid argument guarantees its conclusion in every possible situation in which the premises are true, not merely in the situations that happen to occur. That is why logical knowledge is acquired by reasoning rather than by observation, and why an empirical test can show that a premise is false but never that a valid inference is invalid.

### 1.4 Formal and informal logic

**Formal logic** studies reasoning with artificial languages whose symbols and rules of inference are defined exactly, so that whether a step is allowed can be checked mechanically. Sentential logic (the sixth entry) and predicate logic (the seventh) are the two basic formal systems. **Informal logic** studies reasoning as it occurs in ordinary language: in reports, debates, reviews and specifications. It deals with ambiguity, unstated premises and fallacies, and the term is often used as a near synonym for critical thinking.

The two support each other. Formal logic supplies exact definitions and reliable tests; informal logic decides how a real argument should be translated before those tests can be applied. Formal logic is also the basis of several engineering tools: Boolean algebra in digital circuit design, satisfiability (SAT) solvers in hardware verification and scheduling, and formal methods that prove properties of programs and protocols.

## 2. Statements: what can be true or false

### 2.1 Declarative sentences

Logic studies the relations between things that can be true or false. These are called **statements** (or propositions): declarative sentences that make a claim. "The tank is full", "every packet carries a checksum" and "$2 + 2 = 5$" are statements; the last is false, but it is still a statement because it has a truth value.

Other kinds of sentence do not make claims. A question ("Is the tank full?") asks for information, and a command ("Close the valve.") asks for an action. Neither is true or false, so neither can be a premise or a conclusion as it stands. Fragments such as headlines, slogans and log tags ("Disk full", "Retry limit") are not statements either until they are expanded into complete sentences, and expanding them is often where an ambiguity comes to light: "Disk full" might mean "the disk is full" or "the disk will be full soon".

Some sentences need care. Requirements written with "shall" ("The system shall respond within 100 ms") are norms rather than descriptions: they say what the system must do, not what it does. To reason about whether a design meets them, one converts them into statements about the system ("the system responds within 100 ms in every run") whose truth can then be checked.

### 2.2 Sentences and statements

The same statement can be made with different sentences ("the test failed" and "the test did not pass"), and the same sentence can make different statements in different contexts ("the latest build is broken" makes a different claim every time a new build appears). Logic evaluates what is claimed, so before an argument is analyzed its sentences should be made specific enough that each has a definite truth value. The second entry, on [meaning and clarity](/blog/meaning-clarity-and-definitions), treats the tools for this.

### 2.3 Simple and compound statements

A **simple** (atomic) statement contains no other statement as a part: "the pump is running". A **compound** statement is built from other statements by words such as "not", "and", "or" and "if ... then": "the pump is running and the valve is closed". The words that build compound statements are the **logical connectives**. They matter because the truth value of a compound statement depends, for the connectives logic studies, only on the truth values of its parts. Section 3 introduces the main connectives informally; the sixth entry defines them exactly with truth tables.

## 3. Building statements from statements

### 3.1 Negation

The **negation** of a statement $P$, written $\neg P$, is the statement that is true exactly when $P$ is false. "It is not the case that the pump is running" is the negation of "the pump is running". Two facts about negation follow from the definition: a statement and its negation can never both be true, and they can never both be false. In every possible situation exactly one of them holds.

That second fact makes negation easy to get wrong in practice. The negation of "all tests passed" is not "all tests failed" but "at least one test did not pass". The negation of "the valve is fully open" is not "the valve is fully closed", because a half-open valve makes both of those false. Two statements that cannot both be true but can both be false are **contraries**; two statements that always have opposite truth values are **contradictories**. Only the contradictory of a statement is its negation, and a careless denial often states a contrary instead. In a debugging discussion, "it is not true that the cache always helps" is a much weaker claim than "the cache never helps", and the evidence needed for each is different.

### 3.2 Conjunction

The **conjunction** $P \land Q$ ("$P$ and $Q$") is true when both parts are true and false otherwise. English expresses conjunction with many words besides "and": "but", "although", "yet" and "whereas" all assert both parts, while adding a note of contrast that logic ignores. "The firmware is old but stable" makes the same logical claim as "the firmware is old and stable".

### 3.3 Disjunction, inclusive and exclusive

A **disjunction** says that at least one of two statements holds: "the fault is in the sensor or in the cable". English "or" has two readings. On the **inclusive** reading, $P \lor Q$ is true when $P$ is true, when $Q$ is true or when both are; it is false only when both are false. On the **exclusive** reading, "$P$ or $Q$" is true when exactly one part is true, and false when both are.

Logic takes the inclusive reading as the basic one and writes the exclusive reading as a compound: $(P \lor Q) \land \neg(P \land Q)$, "$P$ or $Q$, but not both". A fault in the sensor does not rule out a second fault in the cable, so a technician who reads the diagnosis as exclusive and stops after finding the first fault has made a logical error, not merely a practical one. When the reading matters, write "or both" or "but not both" explicitly. In digital electronics the two readings are two different gates, OR and XOR, and nobody confuses them; ordinary language deserves the same care.

### 3.4 Conditionals

A **conditional** "if $P$ then $Q$", written $P \to Q$, links an **antecedent** $P$ to a **consequent** $Q$. Its exact meaning is the subject of a separate discussion in the [sixth entry](/blog/sentential-logic). For now it is enough that a conditional is false when the antecedent is true and the consequent false, and that it does not assert either part on its own: "if the battery is flat, the robot stops" says nothing about whether the battery is in fact flat.

## 4. Logical relations between statements

### 4.1 Consistency

A set of statements is **consistent** if it is possible for all of them to be true at the same time, and **inconsistent** if it is not. "Possible" here means logically possible: the question is whether some conceivable situation makes every member true, not whether such a situation is likely or actual.

Some examples make the definition concrete.

- "The controller samples at 1 kHz" and "the controller runs on a 32-bit processor" are consistent: both can be true, and probably are for many controllers.
- "The controller samples at 1 kHz" and "the controller samples at 2 kHz" (referring to one controller, one sampling rate, at one time) are inconsistent.
- "Every request is answered within 50 ms", "request 17 was answered in 80 ms" and "request 17 is a request" are inconsistent as a set, although any two of them are consistent.

Several properties follow from the definition. A set of true statements is always consistent, because the actual world is a situation that makes them all true. A set of false statements may be consistent or not: "the Moon is made of copper" and "the Moon is a good conductor" are both false and consistent. An inconsistent set need not contain any false statement *by itself*; the trouble may lie only in a combination. And a set is inconsistent as a whole as soon as any part of it is, however many other members are harmless.

Consistency is a minimum standard for a body of beliefs or a specification. A requirements document that is inconsistent cannot be met by any design, however clever, and the inconsistency is usually cheaper to find on paper than in a failed acceptance test. Consistency is not, however, a sign of truth: a well-crafted false story can be perfectly consistent.

### 4.2 Inconsistent or self-defeating

Some claims are odd without being inconsistent. Suppose a logging library writes the line "logging is currently disabled" to its log file. The statement is consistent: there are possible situations in which logging is disabled. What is impossible is for the library to make the statement *truly* by that means, because the act of writing it shows that logging is enabled. Such a claim is **self-defeating**: its falsity follows from the way it is asserted, not from its content.

The difference matters when evaluating an argument. A premise that is self-defeating in context is false and should be rejected, but it does not make the set of premises inconsistent in the logical sense, and a test for consistency will not detect it. Sweeping claims about truth are a common source of self-defeat in ordinary debate: "no statement can be known to be true" is a statement offered as known to be true.

### 4.3 Entailment

A statement or set of statements $X$ **entails** a statement $Y$ if it is impossible for $X$ to be true and $Y$ false. "The latency of every request in the log is below 10 ms" entails "the latency of the first request in the log is below 10 ms", but not the other way round.

Two consequences are used constantly. First, if $X$ entails $Y$ and $Y$ turns out to be false, then $X$ is false: a model that entails a prediction is refuted, at least in part, when the prediction fails. Second, if $X$ entails $Y$ and $X$ turns out to be false, nothing follows about $Y$: a refuted model may still have made some true predictions.

Entailment also orders claims by strength. If $X$ entails $Y$ but $Y$ does not entail $X$, then $X$ is the **stronger** claim and $Y$ the weaker. "Every request completes within 10 ms" is stronger than "99% of requests complete within 10 ms", which is stronger than "some request completes within 10 ms". A stronger claim says more about the world and is more useful when true, but it is also exposed to more ways of being false. Choosing the strength of a claim is part of stating it honestly: a test report that has measured a sample should not state the universal claim.

### 4.4 Logical equivalence

Two statements are **logically equivalent** if each entails the other, so that they have the same truth value in every possible situation. "Not every test passed" is equivalent to "some test did not pass". "No cheap component is reliable" is equivalent to "no reliable component is cheap", since both say that nothing is both cheap and reliable.

Equivalence is often assumed where it does not hold. "All reliable components are expensive" is *not* equivalent to "all expensive components are reliable": the first allows expensive components that fail, and the second allows cheap ones that do not. The [next entry](/blog/categorical-logic-and-venn-diagrams) gives a picture that makes such differences visible at a glance.

### 4.5 One idea seen from several sides

Validity, consistency, entailment, equivalence and logical truth are all defined in terms of what is possible, and each can be expressed through the others. Writing $\Gamma$ for a set of premises and $C$ for a conclusion:

| Relation | Definition | In terms of consistency |
|---|---|---|
| $\Gamma$ entails $C$ (the argument from $\Gamma$ to $C$ is valid) | No possible situation makes every member of $\Gamma$ true and $C$ false | $\Gamma$ together with $\neg C$ is inconsistent |
| $P$ and $Q$ are equivalent | They agree in truth value in every possible situation | $\{P, \neg Q\}$ and $\{\neg P, Q\}$ are both inconsistent |
| $P$ is a logical truth | $P$ is true in every possible situation | $\{\neg P\}$ is inconsistent |
| $P$ is a contradiction | $P$ is false in every possible situation | $\{P\}$ is inconsistent |
| $\Gamma$ is consistent | Some possible situation makes every member true | (definition) |

The first row is the most useful. To test whether an argument is valid, add the negation of the conclusion to the premises and ask whether the result could all be true. If it could, the situation that makes it so is a **counterexample**: true premises with a false conclusion, which shows the argument invalid. If it could not, the argument is valid. Proof by contradiction (*reductio ad absurdum*) and the refutation procedures used by automated theorem provers both rest on this equivalence.

The table also explains a result that surprises most readers the first time they meet it. If the premises $\Gamma$ are inconsistent by themselves, then $\Gamma$ with $\neg C$ added is inconsistent for every $C$, so an inconsistent set of premises entails every statement. The argument is valid but can never be sound, since its premises cannot all be true; the third entry discussed why validity alone does not make an argument good.

## 5. Validity depends on form

### 5.1 Logical form

The three arguments in Section 1.2 share a pattern. Replacing each simple statement with a letter, consistently, gives

$$
P \to Q, \quad P, \quad \text{therefore } Q.
$$

This pattern is their **logical form**: the structure that remains when the content-bearing parts are replaced by placeholders and only the logical words ("if ... then", "not", "and", "or", "all", "some") are kept. An argument form is **valid** if every argument with that form whose premises are all true also has a true conclusion; equivalently, no instance of it has true premises and a false conclusion. A valid form guarantees the validity of all of its instances at once, whatever their topic, which is the source of the topic neutrality described in Section 1.2.

### 5.2 Refuting a form with an instance

A form is shown to be invalid by a single instance with true premises and a false conclusion. Consider

$$
P \to Q, \quad Q, \quad \text{therefore } P,
$$

the pattern called affirming the consequent in the third entry. An instance: "if $n$ is divisible by 6, then $n$ is even; 4 is even; therefore 4 is divisible by 6". The premises are true and the conclusion is false, so the form is invalid, and any argument that relies on it alone, such as "if the bearing is worn, the motor vibrates; the motor vibrates; so the bearing is worn", is invalid too, even if its conclusion happens to be true. A vibrating motor is consistent with an unbalanced rotor, a loose mounting or a resonance, and the counterexample for the arithmetic version shows why the shape of the reasoning cannot rule those out.

The method is powerful because it transfers a judgment from a familiar case, where the truth values are obvious, to an unfamiliar one, where they are not. Its one requirement is that the substitution be faithful: the counterexample must have exactly the same logical form as the original.

### 5.3 What counts as form depends on the logic

Which words count as logical, and therefore which features of an argument belong to its form, depends on the logical system. Consider

> All stainless steels contain chromium. This alloy is a stainless steel. So this alloy contains chromium.

In sentential logic, which looks inside a statement no further than its connectives, the three statements have nothing in common and the form is "$P$, $Q$, therefore $R$", which is invalid. Yet the argument is plainly valid. Its validity depends on the words "all" and "is a", which sentential logic cannot see. Categorical logic (the [fifth entry](/blog/categorical-logic-and-venn-diagrams)) and predicate logic (the [seventh](/blog/predicate-logic)) analyze statements at that finer grain and confirm the argument valid.

The lesson is asymmetric. If an argument has a valid form in some correct logical system, it is valid. If it fails to have a valid form in one particular system, it may still be valid in a richer one, so "I cannot find a valid sentential form" is not a proof of invalidity.

### 5.4 Limits of formal analysis

Form is not everything. Formal validity says nothing about whether the premises are true, so a valid argument from a false premise can lead to a false conclusion. The translation from ordinary language into logical form can itself go wrong: a word used in two senses (an equivocation) can make an invalid argument look formally valid, and English connectives carry shades of meaning ("but", "unless", "only if") that a translation must handle with care. Some good arguments are not deductive at all; their strength is a matter of probability, the subject of the [eighth](/blog/scientific-reasoning-and-causation) and [ninth](/blog/probability-and-statistical-reasoning) entries. Formal tools answer one question exactly: whether the conclusion follows. The other questions remain.

## 6. Consistency at work: knights and knaves

### 6.1 A puzzle solved by cases

Puzzles about an island whose inhabitants are either knights, who always tell the truth, or knaves, who always lie, were popularized by Raymond Smullyan [Smullyan 1978]. They are exercises in exactly the reasoning of Section 4: each hypothesis about who is what must be consistent with what everyone says.

Here is one. Two inhabitants, A and B, speak.

- A says: "B is a knave."
- B says: "A and I are of the same kind."

The method is to suppose each possibility in turn and test it for consistency.

1. Suppose A is a knight. Then A's statement is true, so B is a knave. B's statement must then be false, and it is: A is a knight and B a knave, which are different kinds. Everything is consistent.
2. Suppose A is a knave. Then A's statement is false, so B is a knight. B's statement must then be true, so A and B are of the same kind. But A is a knave and B a knight. The supposition leads to an inconsistency and is rejected.

Only the first case survives, so A is a knight and B is a knave. Case 2 is a small proof by contradiction in the sense of Section 4.5: the puzzle's statements together with "A is a knave" form an inconsistent set, so they entail "A is a knight".

### 6.2 Exhaustive search

Checking every case is mechanical, so a computer can do it. Each inhabitant is a knight (`True`) or a knave (`False`), and a statement made by an inhabitant is true exactly when that inhabitant is a knight. The program below enumerates the four possibilities and prints those consistent with both statements.

```python
from itertools import product

# True means knight (always truthful), False means knave (always lies).
for a, b in product([True, False], repeat=2):
    says_a = not b          # A: "B is a knave."
    says_b = (a == b)       # B: "A and I are of the same kind."
    # A speaker's statement is true exactly when the speaker is a knight.
    if says_a == a and says_b == b:
        print(f"A is a {'knight' if a else 'knave'}, B is a {'knight' if b else 'knave'}")
```

Run with Python 3.13, it prints a single line:

```text
A is a knight, B is a knave
```

One surviving assignment means the solution is unique. Two or more would mean the statements do not settle the question, and none would mean the story itself is inconsistent. The program is a four-row truth table in disguise; the sixth entry builds truth tables systematically. The same enumerate-and-test idea, with clever pruning in place of brute force, is what SAT solvers do when they check whether a hardware design can ever reach a bad state. With $n$ two-valued unknowns there are $2^n$ cases, so brute force stops being practical long before $n$ reaches 100; the pruning is what makes industrial problems tractable.

## 7. Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Which of the following are statements? (a) "Is the server up?" (b) "The server is up." (c) "Restart the server." (d) "Every server in the rack was restarted last night."

*Answer.* (b) and (d) are statements; (a) is a question and (c) a command. (d) is a statement even if nobody knows whether it is true.

**Exercise 2.** Give the negation of each statement in plain English. (a) "All the sensors are calibrated." (b) "Some reading is out of range." (c) "The motor is hot and the fan is off."

*Answer.* (a) "At least one sensor is not calibrated." (b) "No reading is out of range." (c) "The motor is not hot or the fan is not off (or both)." The last uses the inclusive "or": the conjunction is false as soon as either part is false.

**Exercise 3.** Are the following sets consistent? (a) "The file is larger than 2 GB." "The file is smaller than 1 GB." (b) "Every alarm was logged." "No alarm was acknowledged." (c) "The robot is in the kitchen." "Every room the robot is in has a charging dock." "The kitchen has no charging dock."

*Answer.* (a) Inconsistent, for the same file at the same time. (b) Consistent: alarms can be logged and never acknowledged. (c) Inconsistent: the first two entail that the kitchen has a charging dock, which the third denies.

**Exercise 4.** Statement $X$ entails statement $Y$. For each case, say what can be concluded. (a) $Y$ is false. (b) $Y$ is true. (c) $X$ is false.

*Answer.* (a) $X$ is false. (b) Nothing about $X$. (c) Nothing about $Y$.

**Exercise 5.** Are "every safe design is simple" and "every design that is not simple is not safe" logically equivalent? Are "every safe design is simple" and "every simple design is safe"?

*Answer.* The first pair is equivalent: both say that no design is safe without being simple. The second pair is not: the first allows simple designs that are unsafe, and the second allows safe designs that are complex.

**Exercise 6.** Show that the following argument form is invalid by giving an instance with true premises and a false conclusion: $P \to Q$, $\neg P$, therefore $\neg Q$.

*Answer.* One instance: "If $n$ is divisible by 4, then $n$ is even. 6 is not divisible by 4. Therefore 6 is not even." The premises are true and the conclusion false. (The form is called denying the antecedent.)

**Exercise 7.** On the island of Section 6, A says "B is a knight" and B says "A is a knave". Who is what?

*Answer.* No assignment works. If A is a knight, B is a knight, so B's claim that A is a knave is true, a contradiction. If A is a knave, B is a knave, so B's claim that A is a knave must be false, another contradiction. The two statements cannot both have been made on such an island: the story is inconsistent. (Changing the two `says_` lines of the program in Section 6.2 to `says_a = b` and `says_b = not a` confirms that it prints nothing.)

**Exercise 8.** A colleague argues: "Logic cannot tell us anything about the real world, because logical truths are true in every possible situation." What is right and what is wrong in this?

*Answer.* It is right that a logical truth such as "the valve is open or it is not" carries no information about which situation is actual. It is wrong to conclude that logic tells us nothing: entailment tells us which further claims about the world we are committed to by claims we already accept, and inconsistency tells us that some of our claims about the world must be false.

## 8. Summary

| Concept | What it means |
|---|---|
| Deductive logic | The study of which conclusions follow necessarily from which premises; normative, not a description of how people reason. |
| Topic neutrality | Valid patterns of inference apply whatever the statements are about. |
| Logical truth | A statement true in every possible situation by its form alone, unlike a contingent statement. |
| Statement | A declarative sentence that is true or false; questions and commands are not statements. |
| Negation | The contradictory of a statement, true exactly when it is false; not the same as a contrary. |
| Inclusive disjunction | "$P$ or $Q$ or both", the default reading of $P \lor Q$ in logic. |
| Consistency | Possible for all members of a set to be true together. |
| Entailment | Impossible for $X$ to be true and $Y$ false; orders claims by strength. |
| Equivalence | Mutual entailment: the same truth value in every possible situation. |
| Validity and consistency | An argument is valid exactly when its premises together with the negation of its conclusion are inconsistent. |
| Logical form | The structure left when content is replaced by placeholders; one counterexample instance refutes a form. |

These ideas give the next three entries their purpose: each builds a logical system that makes form precise and turns the question "is this consistent?" into a procedure. The [next entry](/blog/categorical-logic-and-venn-diagrams) starts with the oldest and most visual of them, categorical logic, in which statements about classes ("all", "no", "some") are drawn as overlapping circles and the validity of a syllogism can be read from a picture.
