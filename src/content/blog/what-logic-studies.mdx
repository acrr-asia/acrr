The [third entry](/blog/arguments-and-their-structure) took arguments apart into premises and conclusions and separated deductive validity from inductive strength. It treated validity as a property one checks argument by argument. This entry steps back and asks what kind of discipline studies that property, what its basic objects are and why a handful of patterns can settle the validity of arguments about anything at all.

The entry opens with what logic is and what it is not (Section 1), then introduces its basic unit, the statement (Section 2), and the simplest ways of building statements from other statements (Section 3). Section 4 defines the relations between statements that logic studies (consistency, entailment and equivalence) and shows that they are one idea seen from different sides. Section 5 explains why validity depends on logical form, and Section 6 uses consistency checking to solve a classic kind of puzzle, by hand and with a short Python 3 program. No symbols are needed beyond the few listed on the [series page](/series/critical-thinking); the sixth and seventh entries make them precise.

## 1. What logic is

### 1.1 Correct reasoning, not the psychology of reasoning

This entry and the next three are about **deductive logic**: the discipline that decides which conclusions are guaranteed by which premises, and which collections of statements could all hold at once. (The word "logic" is also used loosely for good reasoning of every kind, which is the territory of the whole series.)

A comparison with software helps. A language's type rules define which programs are well typed; a study of programmers could record which type errors they make most often. The first is a specification and the second a set of observations, and no observation of programmers changes the specification. Logic stands to reasoning as the type rules stand to programs: it is *normative*, defining when a conclusion is guaranteed by its premises. The observational counterpart is the psychology of reasoning, an empirical science. Its best-known experiment, Wason's selection task, asks people which cards they must turn over to test a rule of the form "if a card has $P$ on one side, it has $Q$ on the other"; most turn over the $P$ card, which is right, and the $Q$ card, which cannot refute the rule, and skip the not-$Q$ card, which can (Wason, 1968). Later studies found that performance changes with the content and wording of the rule, a fact about people that leaves the logic of the task untouched.

"Most engineers would conclude $X$ from these data" is therefore a claim about people, settled by surveying them; "$X$ follows from these data" is a claim about the data and $X$, settled by logic.

### 1.2 Topic neutrality

Consider three arguments.

1. If the build passes all the tests, it is deployed. The build passes all the tests. So the build is deployed.
2. If the supply voltage drops below 3.0 V, the controller resets. The supply voltage drops below 3.0 V. So the controller resets.
3. If $n$ is divisible by 6, then $n$ is even. $n$ is divisible by 6. So $n$ is even.

They concern software, electronics and arithmetic, yet each is valid for the same reason: each has the shape "if $P$ then $Q$; $P$; therefore $Q$", the pattern called *modus ponens* in the third entry. Nothing about tests, voltages or numbers is needed to see that the conclusion follows.

A program that checks these arguments would need no dictionary. It could replace each simple statement with an opaque token, compare the tokens and reach the same verdict for all three. That independence from subject matter is called **topic neutrality**, and it is why one logic serves every technical field. A reviewer who knows the valid patterns can check an argument about firmware without being a firmware expert, although judging whether its premises are true still needs one.

### 1.3 Necessity

Imagine checking two statements about a production batch. "Every reading in batch 7 is within tolerance" is checked by opening the data: it is **contingent**, true or false depending on what the readings turned out to be. "If every reading in batch 7 is within tolerance, then the first reading in batch 7 is within tolerance" needs no data at all. Whatever the readings are, the statement holds, because its structure leaves no way for it to fail. A statement of that kind, true in every possible situation because of its form, is a **logical truth**. No measurement could refute it, and none is needed to establish it.

The same contrast applies to arguments. A valid argument guarantees its conclusion in every possible situation in which the premises are true, not merely in those that happen to occur. That is why an empirical test can show that a premise is false but never that a valid inference is invalid.

### 1.4 Formal and informal logic

Logic can be practiced at two levels of precision. **Formal logic** writes arguments in a notation with fixed rules, so that a machine could check each step; sentential logic (the sixth entry) and predicate logic (the seventh) are its two basic systems. **Informal logic** works on arguments as they appear in design reviews, incident reports and debates. There the hard part is deciding what an argument says: resolving ambiguity, supplying unstated premises and spotting fallacies.

The two support each other: formal logic supplies exact tests, and informal logic decides how a real argument should be translated before they can be applied. Formal logic also underlies engineering tools such as Boolean algebra in circuit design, satisfiability (SAT) solvers in hardware verification and formal methods that prove properties of programs.

## 2. Statements: what can be true or false

### 2.1 Declarative sentences

Premises and conclusions have to be things that can be true or false. These are called **statements** (or propositions): what a declarative sentence claims, which is either true or false. "The tank is full", "every packet carries a checksum" and the equation $2 + 2 = 5$ all make statements; the equation is false, which does not stop it from being one.

A useful test is whether "true" or "false" can sensibly be said in reply. "Is the tank full?" and "Close the valve" fail it, so neither can serve as a premise as it stands. Terse text in a dashboard or log ("Disk full") often passes only after it is expanded, and the expansion is where ambiguity shows: is the disk full now, or predicted to fill?

Requirements written with "shall" ("the system shall respond within 100 ms") are norms rather than descriptions. To check a design against them, one restates them as claims about the system ("the system responds within 100 ms in every run").

### 2.2 Sentences and statements

The same statement can be made with different sentences ("the test failed", "the test did not pass"), and the same sentence can make different statements in different contexts ("the latest build is broken" changes its claim with every new build). Logic evaluates what is claimed, so an argument's sentences should first be made specific enough to have definite truth values, the task of the [second entry](/blog/meaning-clarity-and-definitions).

### 2.3 Simple and compound statements

A **simple** (atomic) statement contains no other statement as a part: "the pump is running". A **compound** statement is built from other statements by words such as "not", "and", "or" and "if ... then": "the pump is running and the valve is closed". The words that build compound statements are the **logical connectives**. They matter because the truth value of a compound statement depends, for the connectives logic studies, only on the truth values of its parts. Section 3 introduces the main connectives informally; the sixth entry defines them exactly with truth tables.

## 3. Building statements from statements

### 3.1 Negation

The **negation** of a statement $P$, written $\neg P$, is the statement whose truth value is the opposite of $P$'s in every possible situation; "it is not the case that the pump is running" negates "the pump is running".

Because the truth values must be opposite *everywhere*, a denial that leaves some situation where both claims fail is not a negation. The negation of "all tests passed" is not "all tests failed" but "at least one test did not pass". The negation of "the valve is fully open" is not "the valve is fully closed", because a half-open valve makes both of those false. Two statements that cannot both be true but can both be false are **contraries**; two statements that always have opposite truth values are **contradictories**. Only the contradictory of a statement is its negation, and a careless denial often states a contrary instead. In a debugging discussion, "it is not true that the cache always helps" is a much weaker claim than "the cache never helps", and the evidence needed for each is different.

### 3.2 Conjunction

The **conjunction** $P \land Q$ ("$P$ and $Q$") is true when both parts are true and false otherwise. "But", "although" and "yet" also assert both parts, adding a note of contrast that logic ignores: "the firmware is old but stable" makes the same logical claim as "the firmware is old and stable".

### 3.3 Disjunction, inclusive and exclusive

A **disjunction** says that at least one of two statements holds: "the fault is in the sensor or in the cable". English "or" has two readings. On the **inclusive** reading, $P \lor Q$ fails only in the situation where $P$ and $Q$ both fail; one true part, or two, makes it true. On the **exclusive** reading, "$P$ or $Q$" is true when exactly one part is true, and false when both are.

Logic takes the inclusive reading as the basic one and writes the exclusive reading as a compound: $(P \lor Q) \land \neg(P \land Q)$, "$P$ or $Q$, but not both". A fault in the sensor does not rule out a second fault in the cable, so a technician who reads the diagnosis as exclusive and stops after finding the first fault has made a logical error, not merely a practical one. When the reading matters, write "or both" or "but not both" explicitly. In digital electronics the two readings are different gates, OR and exclusive OR (XOR), and nobody confuses them; ordinary language deserves the same care.

### 3.4 Conditionals

A **conditional** "if $P$ then $Q$", written $P \to Q$, links an **antecedent** $P$ to a **consequent** $Q$. Its exact meaning is settled in the [sixth entry](/blog/sentential-logic). For now it is enough that a conditional is false when the antecedent is true and the consequent false. It does not assert either part on its own: "if the battery is flat, the robot stops" says nothing about whether the battery is in fact flat.

## 4. Logical relations between statements

### 4.1 Consistency

A set of statements is **consistent** if some possible situation makes every one of them true, and **inconsistent** if no situation does. "Possible" here means logically possible: the situation must be describable without contradiction, not likely or actual.

Some examples make the definition concrete.

- "The controller samples at 1 kHz" and "the controller runs on a 32-bit processor" are consistent: both can be true, and probably are for many controllers.
- "The controller samples at 1 kHz" and "the controller samples at 2 kHz" (referring to one controller, one sampling rate, at one time) are inconsistent.
- "Every request is answered within 50 ms", "request 17 was answered in 80 ms" and "request 17 is a request" are inconsistent as a set, although any two of them are consistent.

The third example shows why reviewing a specification line by line is not enough: each requirement can look sensible alone, and the clash appears only when they are read together. A requirements document that contains such a clash cannot be met by any design, and the clash is cheaper to find on paper than in a failed acceptance test.

Running the definition backward gives a diagnostic. If a set is inconsistent, at least one member must be false, since otherwise the actual world would be a situation making all of them true. Consistency, though, is no evidence of truth: a design document for a product that was never built can be free of contradictions and still describe nothing real. Consistency is a minimum standard, not a sufficient one.

### 4.2 Inconsistent or self-defeating

Suppose a logging library writes the line "logging is currently disabled" to its log file. Nothing in the content of the line is contradictory: an operator who says the same sentence on a day when logging is switched off describes the system correctly. The trouble is that the act of producing it supplies evidence against it, because only an enabled logger can have written it. Such a claim is **self-defeating** in context: the circumstances of its assertion, not its content, show that it is false.

The distinction matters for analysis. A consistency check looks only at content, so it passes the log line; a reader has to notice the circumstances. When a premise of an argument turns out to be self-defeating in this way, the argument is unsound, even though no contradiction can be derived from its premises.

### 4.3 Entailment

A statement or set of statements $X$ **entails** a statement $Y$ if it is impossible for $X$ to be true and $Y$ false. "The latency of every request in the log is below 10 ms" entails "the latency of the first request in the log is below 10 ms", but not the other way round.

Entailment carries truth forward and falsity backward, and nothing else. A simulation model that entails a predicted settling time is put in doubt when the measured settling time differs: the false consequence reflects back on the model. The reverse transfer fails. A model shown wrong in one respect may still predict the settling time correctly, so the falsity of $X$ says nothing about $Y$.

Entailment also orders claims by strength: if $X$ entails $Y$ but not conversely, $X$ is the **stronger** claim. Take a controller board on which the motor driver is one of several circuits. "The fault is in the power stage of the motor driver" entails "the fault is in the motor driver", which entails "the fault is on the controller board". Each step down the chain is safer and less useful: the first claim tells a technician which component to replace, the last only which board to pull. Stating a diagnosis at the right strength means choosing the strongest claim the evidence actually supports.

### 4.4 Logical equivalence

Two statements are **logically equivalent** if each entails the other, so that they have the same truth value in every possible situation. "Not every test passed" is equivalent to "some test did not pass". "No unsigned image is deployed" is equivalent to "no deployed image is unsigned": both rule out exactly the same thing, a deployed unsigned image.

Equivalence is often assumed where it does not hold. "All deployed images are signed" is *not* equivalent to "all signed images are deployed": the first allows signed images waiting in a registry, and the second allows unsigned images in production. The [next entry](/blog/categorical-logic-and-venn-diagrams) gives a picture that makes such differences visible at a glance.

### 4.5 One idea seen from several sides

Validity, consistency, entailment, equivalence and logical truth are all defined in terms of what is possible, and each can be expressed through the others. Writing $\Gamma$ for a set of premises and $C$ for a conclusion:

| Relation | Definition | In terms of consistency |
|---|---|---|
| $\Gamma$ entails $C$ (the argument from $\Gamma$ to $C$ is valid) | No possible situation makes every member of $\Gamma$ true and $C$ false | $\Gamma$ together with $\neg C$ is inconsistent |
| $P$ and $Q$ are equivalent | They agree in truth value in every possible situation | $\{P, \neg Q\}$ and $\{\neg P, Q\}$ are both inconsistent |
| $P$ is a logical truth | $P$ is true in every possible situation | $\{\neg P\}$ is inconsistent |
| $P$ is a contradiction | $P$ is false in every possible situation | $\{P\}$ is inconsistent |
| $\Gamma$ is consistent | Some possible situation makes every member true | (definition) |

The first row is the most useful. To test whether an argument is valid, add the negation of the conclusion to the premises and ask whether the result could all be true. If it could, the situation that makes it so is a **counterexample**: true premises with a false conclusion, which shows the argument invalid. If it could not, the argument is valid. Proof by contradiction (*reductio ad absurdum*) and the refutation procedures used by automated theorem provers both rest on this equivalence.

The table also explains a result that surprises most readers the first time they meet it. If the premises $\Gamma$ are inconsistent by themselves, then $\Gamma$ with $\neg C$ added is inconsistent for every $C$, so an inconsistent set of premises entails every statement. The argument is valid but can never be sound, since its premises cannot all be true; the third entry discussed why validity alone does not make an argument good.

## 5. Validity depends on form

### 5.1 Logical form

The three arguments in Section 1.2 share a pattern. Replacing each simple statement with a letter, consistently, gives

$$
P \to Q, \quad P, \quad \text{therefore } Q.
$$

This pattern is their **logical form**: the structure that remains when the content-bearing parts are replaced by placeholders and only the logical words ("if ... then", "not", "and", "or", "all", "some") are kept. An argument form is **valid** if every argument with that form whose premises are all true also has a true conclusion; equivalently, no instance of it has true premises and a false conclusion. A valid form guarantees the validity of all of its instances at once, whatever their topic, which is the source of the topic neutrality described in Section 1.2.

### 5.2 Refuting a form with an instance

A form is shown to be invalid by a single instance with true premises and a false conclusion. Consider

$$
P \to Q, \quad Q, \quad \text{therefore } P,
$$

the pattern called affirming the consequent in the third entry. An instance: "if 12 is divisible by 8, then 12 is divisible by 4; 12 is divisible by 4; therefore 12 is divisible by 8". The first premise is true because every multiple of 8 is a multiple of 4, the second is true and the conclusion is false, so the form is invalid. Any argument that relies on it alone is invalid too, even when its conclusion is true. "If the bearing is worn, the motor vibrates; the motor vibrates; so the bearing is worn" ignores that an unbalanced rotor or a loose mounting also makes a motor vibrate.

The method is powerful because it transfers a judgment from a familiar case, where the truth values are obvious, to an unfamiliar one, where they are not. Its one requirement is that the substitution be faithful: the counterexample must have exactly the same logical form as the original.

### 5.3 What counts as form depends on the logic

Which words count as logical, and therefore which features of an argument belong to its form, depends on the logical system. Consider

> All stainless steels contain chromium. This alloy is a stainless steel. So this alloy contains chromium.

In sentential logic, which looks inside a statement no further than its connectives, the three statements have nothing in common and the form is "$P$, $Q$, therefore $R$", which is invalid. Yet the argument is plainly valid. Its validity depends on the words "all" and "is a", which sentential logic cannot see. Categorical logic (the [fifth entry](/blog/categorical-logic-and-venn-diagrams)) and predicate logic (the [seventh](/blog/predicate-logic)) analyze statements at that finer grain and confirm the argument valid.

The lesson is asymmetric. If an argument has a valid form in some correct logical system, it is valid. If it fails to have a valid form in one particular system, it may still be valid in a richer one, so "I cannot find a valid sentential form" is not a proof of invalidity.

### 5.4 Limits of formal analysis

Form is not everything. Formal validity says nothing about whether the premises are true. The translation into logical form can itself go wrong: a word used in two senses (an equivocation) can make an invalid argument look formally valid, and connectives such as "unless" and "only if" need care. Some good arguments are not deductive at all; their strength is a matter of probability, the subject of the [eighth](/blog/scientific-reasoning-and-causation) and [ninth](/blog/probability-and-statistical-reasoning) entries. Formal tools answer one question exactly: whether the conclusion follows.

## 6. Consistency at work: knights and knaves

### 6.1 A puzzle solved by cases

Puzzles about an island whose inhabitants are either knights, who always tell the truth, or knaves, who always lie, were popularized by Raymond Smullyan (Smullyan, 1978). They are exercises in exactly the reasoning of Section 4: each hypothesis about who is what must be consistent with what everyone says.

Here is one. Two inhabitants, A and B, speak.

- A says: "B is a knave."
- B says: "A and I are of the same kind."

The method is to suppose each possibility in turn and test it for consistency.

1. Suppose A is a knight. Then A's statement is true, so B is a knave. B's statement must then be false, and it is: A is a knight and B a knave, which are different kinds. Everything is consistent.
2. Suppose A is a knave. Then A's statement is false, so B is a knight. B's statement must then be true, so A and B are of the same kind. But A is a knave and B a knight. The supposition leads to an inconsistency and is rejected.

Only the first case survives, so A is a knight and B is a knave. Case 2 is a small proof by contradiction in the sense of Section 4.5: the puzzle's statements together with "A is a knave" form an inconsistent set, so they entail "A is a knight".

### 6.2 Exhaustive search

Checking every case is mechanical, so a computer can do it. Each inhabitant is a knight (`True`) or a knave (`False`), and a statement made by an inhabitant is true exactly when that inhabitant is a knight. The program below enumerates the four possibilities and prints those consistent with both statements.

```python
from itertools import product

# True means knight (always truthful), False means knave (always lies).
for a, b in product([True, False], repeat=2):
    says_a = not b          # A: "B is a knave."
    says_b = (a == b)       # B: "A and I are of the same kind."
    # A speaker's statement is true exactly when the speaker is a knight.
    if says_a == a and says_b == b:
        print(f"A is a {'knight' if a else 'knave'}, B is a {'knight' if b else 'knave'}")
```

Run with Python 3.13, it prints a single line:

```text
A is a knight, B is a knave
```

One surviving assignment means the solution is unique; two or more would mean the statements do not settle the question, and none would mean the story itself is inconsistent. The program is a four-row truth table in disguise, of the kind the sixth entry builds systematically. With $n$ two-valued unknowns there are $2^n$ cases, so brute force soon becomes impractical; SAT solvers apply the same enumerate-and-test idea with pruning that skips most of the cases.

## 7. Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Which of these sentences make statements? (a) "Is the server up?" (b) "The server is up." (c) "Restart the server." (d) "Every server in the rack was restarted last night."

*Answer.* (b) and (d) are statements; (a) is a question and (c) a command. (d) is a statement even if nobody knows whether it is true.

**Exercise 2.** Give the negation of each statement in plain English. (a) "All the sensors are calibrated." (b) "Some reading is out of range." (c) "The motor is hot and the fan is off."

*Answer.* (a) "At least one sensor is not calibrated." (b) "No reading is out of range." (c) "The motor is not hot or the fan is not off (or both)." The last uses the inclusive "or": the conjunction is false as soon as either part is false.

**Exercise 3.** Are the following sets consistent? (a) "The file is larger than 2 GB." "The file is smaller than 1 GB." (b) "Every alarm was logged." "No alarm was acknowledged." (c) "The robot is in the kitchen." "Every room the robot is in has a charging dock." "The kitchen has no charging dock."

*Answer.* (a) Inconsistent, for the same file at the same time. (b) Consistent: alarms can be logged and never acknowledged. (c) Inconsistent: the first two entail that the kitchen has a charging dock, which the third denies.

**Exercise 4.** Statement $X$ entails statement $Y$. For each case, say what can be concluded. (a) $Y$ is false. (b) $Y$ is true. (c) $X$ is false.

*Answer.* (a) $X$ is false. (b) Nothing about $X$. (c) Nothing about $Y$.

**Exercise 5.** Are "every safe design is simple" and "every design that is not simple is not safe" logically equivalent? Are "every safe design is simple" and "every simple design is safe"?

*Answer.* The first pair is equivalent: both say that no design is safe without being simple. The second pair is not: the first allows simple designs that are unsafe, and the second allows safe designs that are complex.

**Exercise 6.** Show that the following argument form is invalid by giving an instance with true premises and a false conclusion: $P \to Q$, $\neg P$, therefore $\neg Q$.

*Answer.* One instance: "If 12 is divisible by 8, then 12 is divisible by 4. 12 is not divisible by 8. Therefore 12 is not divisible by 4." The premises are true and the conclusion false. (The form is called denying the antecedent.)

**Exercise 7.** On the island of Section 6, A says "B is a knight" and B says "A is a knave". Who is what?

*Answer.* No assignment works. If A is a knight, B is a knight, so B's claim that A is a knave is true, a contradiction. If A is a knave, B is a knave, so B's claim that A is a knave must be false, another contradiction. The two statements cannot both have been made on such an island: the story is inconsistent. (Changing the two `says_` lines of the program in Section 6.2 to `says_a = b` and `says_b = not a` confirms that it prints nothing.)

## 8. Summary

| Concept | What it means |
|---|---|
| Deductive logic | The study of which conclusions follow necessarily from which premises; normative, not a description of how people reason. |
| Topic neutrality | Valid patterns of inference apply whatever the statements are about. |
| Logical truth | A statement true in every possible situation by its form alone, unlike a contingent statement. |
| Statement | What a declarative sentence claims, true or false; questions and commands make no statement. |
| Negation | The contradictory of a statement, true exactly when it is false; not the same as a contrary. |
| Inclusive disjunction | "$P$ or $Q$ or both", the default reading of $P \lor Q$ in logic. |
| Consistency | Possible for all members of a set to be true together. |
| Entailment | Impossible for $X$ to be true and $Y$ false; orders claims by strength. |
| Equivalence | Mutual entailment: the same truth value in every possible situation. |
| Validity and consistency | An argument is valid exactly when its premises together with the negation of its conclusion are inconsistent. |
| Logical form | The structure left when content is replaced by placeholders; one counterexample instance refutes a form. |

These ideas give the next three entries their purpose: each builds a logical system that makes form precise and turns the question "is this consistent?" into a procedure. The [next entry](/blog/categorical-logic-and-venn-diagrams) starts with the oldest and most visual of them, categorical logic, in which statements about classes ("all", "no", "some") are drawn as overlapping circles and the validity of a syllogism can be read from a picture.
