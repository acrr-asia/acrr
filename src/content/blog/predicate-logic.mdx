The [sixth entry](/blog/sentential-logic) built sentential logic: a language of whole sentences joined by connectives, with truth tables that decide validity and a natural-deduction system that proves exactly the valid forms. It ended on that system's blind spot. An argument such as "every sensor that passed calibration is in service; S7 passed calibration; so S7 is in service" is valid because of what happens inside its sentences, and sentential logic treats each sentence as an unanalyzed letter.

**Predicate logic**, also called first-order logic, opens sentences up. It splits them into names for objects, predicates for properties and relations, and the quantifiers "every" and "some". This entry introduces the language, shows how to formalize statements with one and several quantifiers, and defines truth in an interpretation. An interpretation that makes given sentences true is a model of them. It then shows how to refute an argument by exhibiting a counter-model, adds four quantifier rules to the natural-deduction system of the previous entry and closes with the metatheory: the system is sound and complete, but, unlike sentential logic, predicate logic has no general decision procedure.

The entry assumes the connectives, truth tables and derivation rules of the [sixth entry](/blog/sentential-logic), and refers to its sections rather than repeating them. Categorical statements of the [fifth entry](/blog/categorical-logic-and-venn-diagrams) reappear in Section 2.2 as a special case.

## 1. Names, predicates and variables

### 1.1 Singular terms and predicates

A **singular term** picks out one particular object: "S7", "the Mars rover Curiosity", "the number 12". In the formal language, singular terms are **names**, written as lowercase letters $a$, $b$, $c$ and so on. A **predicate** expresses a property or a relation. A **one-place predicate** applies to one object ("is a sensor", "is faulty") and a **two-place predicate** relates two ("depends on", "is less than"); predicates with three or more places ("sends ... to ... at ...") work the same way. Predicates are written as uppercase letters followed by the names they apply to:

| English | Formula | Translation key |
|---|---|---|
| S7 is faulty. | $Fs$ | $s$: S7; $F$: is faulty |
| The planner depends on the map server. | $Dpm$ | $p$: the planner; $m$: the map server; $Dxy$: $x$ depends on $y$ |
| The map server does not depend on the planner. | $\neg Dmp$ | as above |

An **atomic formula** is a predicate followed by the right number of names, and atomic formulas combine with the connectives exactly as sentence letters did. The order of the names matters for relations: $Dpm$ and $Dmp$ are different claims, and one can be true while the other is false.

### 1.2 Variables and open formulas

A **variable** ($x$, $y$, $z$) is a placeholder that does not name any particular object. Putting a variable where a name would go gives an **open formula** such as $Fx$ or $Dxm$. An open formula is neither true nor false as it stands, in the same way that the condition `x > 3` in a program has no truth value until `x` is bound to a value. It describes a condition that some objects may satisfy and others not. Quantifiers turn open formulas back into sentences.

## 2. Quantifiers

### 2.1 Every and some

The **universal quantifier** $\forall x$ reads "for every $x$". The formula $\forall x\, Fx$ is true when every object under discussion satisfies $Fx$. The **existential quantifier** $\exists x$ reads "there is at least one $x$ such that". The formula $\exists x\, Fx$ is true when at least one object satisfies $Fx$. The objects under discussion form the **domain**, which must be specified (Section 5.1): "everything is working" is true or false depending on whether the domain is the components of one robot or every machine in a factory.

### 2.2 The four categorical forms

The categorical statements of the [fifth entry](/blog/categorical-logic-and-venn-diagrams) have standard translations, with $F$ and $G$ for the two terms:

| Form | English | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| A | All $F$ are $G$. | $\forall x\,(Fx \to Gx)$ |
| E | No $F$ are $G$. | $\forall x\,(Fx \to \neg Gx)$ |
| I | Some $F$ are $G$. | $\exists x\,(Fx \land Gx)$ |
| O | Some $F$ are not $G$. | $\exists x\,(Fx \land \neg Gx)$ |

Two errors are common enough to deserve a warning. "All sensors are calibrated" is not $\forall x\,(Sx \land Cx)$, which says that everything in the domain is a calibrated sensor. And "some sensors are calibrated" is not $\exists x\,(Sx \to Cx)$, which is true as soon as the domain contains one object that is not a sensor, because a conditional with a false antecedent is true (sixth entry, Section 6.1). The rule of thumb: the universal quantifier goes with $\to$, the existential quantifier with $\land$.

The translation of the A form also fixes the modern answer to the question of existential import raised in the fifth entry. If nothing in the domain is $F$, every instance $Fx \to Gx$ has a false antecedent, so $\forall x\,(Fx \to Gx)$ is true. "All faulty sensors have been replaced" is true when there are no faulty sensors, and it does not entail that some faulty sensor has been replaced.

### 2.3 Negation and quantifiers

"Not every test passed" says the same as "some test did not pass". In general, $\neg\forall x\,\varphi$ is equivalent to $\exists x\,\neg\varphi$, and $\neg\exists x\,\varphi$ is equivalent to $\forall x\,\neg\varphi$. These **quantifier negation** laws let a negation move inward past a quantifier, flipping it. They are the quantifier counterparts of De Morgan's laws (sixth entry, Section 3.2); over a finite domain with objects $a$ and $b$, $\forall x\,Fx$ amounts to $Fa \land Fb$ and $\exists x\,Fx$ to $Fa \lor Fb$, and the two pairs of laws coincide.

## 3. Well-formed formulas

### 3.1 Formation rules

The language of predicate logic extends that of sentential logic. Its **terms** are names and variables. Its well-formed formulas are defined recursively:

1. A predicate of $n$ places followed by $n$ terms is a WFF (an atomic formula).
2. If $\varphi$ is a WFF, so is $\neg\varphi$; if $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are WFFs, so are $(\varphi \land \psi)$, $(\varphi \lor \psi)$, $(\varphi \to \psi)$ and $(\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi)$.
3. If $\varphi$ is a WFF and $x$ is a variable, then $\forall x\,\varphi$ and $\exists x\,\varphi$ are WFFs.

Brackets follow the conventions of the sixth entry. A quantifier has the shortest possible scope, the formula immediately after it, so $\forall x\,Fx \to Gx$ is the conditional $(\forall x\,Fx) \to Gx$, whereas $\forall x\,(Fx \to Gx)$ quantifies the whole conditional.

### 3.2 Free and bound variables

An occurrence of a variable is **bound** when it lies within the scope of a quantifier on that variable, and **free** otherwise. In $\forall x\,(Fx \to Gy) \land Hx$, the $x$ in $Fx$ is bound, $y$ is free, and the $x$ in $Hx$ is free because it lies outside the bracket that the quantifier governs. A formula with no free variables is a **sentence**, and only sentences have a truth value in an interpretation. The distinction mirrors bound and free identifiers in a program: in a function `f(x) = x + y`, the parameter `x` is bound by the definition and `y` must be supplied from outside.

## 4. Multiple quantifiers and relations

### 4.1 Order matters

With two-place predicates a sentence can contain several quantifiers, and their order changes the meaning. Take the domain of the engineers on a team and let $Rxy$ mean "$x$ reviews code written by $y$":

| Formula | Reading |
|---|---|
| $\forall y\,\exists x\,Rxy$ | Everyone's code is reviewed by someone (possibly a different reviewer for each author). |
| $\exists x\,\forall y\,Rxy$ | Someone reviews everyone's code (one reviewer for all). |
| $\forall x\,\exists y\,Rxy$ | Everyone reviews someone's code. |
| $\exists y\,\forall x\,Rxy$ | Someone's code is reviewed by everyone. |

The second sentence entails the first: if one person reviews every author's code, then every author has a reviewer. The converse fails, and Section 6.2 proves it with a two-person counter-model. Confusing $\forall\exists$ with $\exists\forall$ is the formal shape of a common real error. "Every request is handled by some server" is a load-balancing claim; "some server handles every request" is a single-point-of-failure claim.

Mathematics supplies another example. Over the natural numbers, with $Lxy$ for $x < y$, $\forall x\,\exists y\,Lxy$ (every number has a larger one) is true, and $\exists y\,\forall x\,Lxy$ (some number is larger than every number, itself included) is false.

### 4.2 A formalization procedure

Long sentences become manageable when they are translated from the outside in, one quantifier at a time:

1. Fix the domain and a translation key for every name and predicate.
2. Find the main logical structure of the sentence: is it a universal claim, an existential claim or a connective joining smaller claims?
3. Translate the outermost layer, writing the remaining parts in a mix of English and symbols, and repeat on each part.
4. Read the result back and check it against cases where you know the answer.

Take, over a domain of software modules and tests, the sentence "every module that depends on a deprecated module fails some test", with $Mx$: $x$ is a module, $Ex$: $x$ is deprecated, $Tx$: $x$ is a test, $Dxy$: $x$ depends on $y$, and $Fxy$: $x$ fails test $y$. The outer layer is an A form:

$$
\forall x\,(x \text{ is a module that depends on a deprecated module} \to x \text{ fails some test})
$$

The antecedent becomes $Mx \land \exists y\,(My \land Ey \land Dxy)$ and the consequent $\exists z\,(Tz \land Fxz)$, giving

$$
\forall x\,\bigl((Mx \land \exists y\,(My \land Ey \land Dxy)) \to \exists z\,(Tz \land Fxz)\bigr)
$$

(The triple conjunction is an abbreviation; either grouping gives an equivalent formula.) A reading-back check: a module that depends on nothing deprecated makes the antecedent false, so the sentence says nothing about it, which matches the English.

## 5. Interpretations and models

### 5.1 What an interpretation fixes

In sentential logic, a row of a truth table fixed the meaning of every sentence letter. In predicate logic the corresponding object is an **interpretation**, which specifies:

- a nonempty set of objects, the **domain**;
- for each name, the object in the domain that it refers to;
- for each one-place predicate, its **extension**: the set of objects it is true of; for each two-place predicate, the set of ordered pairs it is true of; and so on for more places.

Truth in an interpretation is then defined from the bottom up. An atomic sentence $Fa$ is true when the object named by $a$ is in the extension of $F$, and $Rab$ is true when the pair (object of $a$, object of $b$) is in the extension of $R$. The connectives keep their truth tables. A sentence $\forall x\,\varphi(x)$ is true when $\varphi$ holds of every object in the domain, and $\exists x\,\varphi(x)$ is true when $\varphi$ holds of at least one. An interpretation that makes a set of sentences true is a **model** of that set.

### 5.2 A worked evaluation

Take the domain $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and interpret $Lxy$ as $x < y$, so its extension is the set of pairs $(1, 2)$, $(1, 3)$ and $(2, 3)$.

- $\forall x\,\exists y\,Lxy$ is false: for $x = 3$, no $y$ in the domain is larger.
- $\exists x\,\forall y\,\neg Lyx$ is true: $x = 1$ works, since no object is less than 1.
- $\forall x\,\forall y\,(Lxy \to \neg Lyx)$ is true: no two objects are each less than the other.

The first sentence becomes true if the domain is changed to all natural numbers. Truth in predicate logic is always truth in an interpretation, and a change of domain or extension can change the verdict.

### 5.3 Validity, entailment and consistency

The definitions of the sixth entry carry over with "interpretation" in place of "row". A sentence is **valid** (logically true) when it is true in every interpretation. A set of sentences $\Gamma$ **entails** $\varphi$, written $\Gamma \vDash \varphi$, when every interpretation that makes all of $\Gamma$ true also makes $\varphi$ true. A set is **consistent** when it has at least one model.

The sentential definitions still apply to formulas that have sentential form. $Fa \lor \neg Fa$ and $\forall x\,Fx \lor \neg\forall x\,Fx$ are both instances of $P \lor \neg P$ and are valid for that reason alone. But many valid sentences, such as $\forall x\,Fx \to Fa$ or $\exists x\,\forall y\,Rxy \to \forall y\,\exists x\,Rxy$, are not instances of any tautology; their validity depends on the quantifiers. A truth table cannot test them, because there are infinitely many interpretations: domains of every size, including infinite ones, with every possible extension.

## 6. Showing invalidity with a counter-model

### 6.1 The method

A **counter-model** to an argument is an interpretation in which every premise is true and the conclusion is false. One counter-model proves the argument form invalid, exactly as one counterexample row did in sentential logic. Finding one is a design problem rather than a calculation:

1. Read the conclusion and ask what would make it false.
2. Choose a small domain, often two or three objects, and assign extensions that make the conclusion false.
3. Adjust the extensions until every premise is true, adding objects if needed.
4. Check each premise and the conclusion against the final interpretation.

### 6.2 Two worked counter-models

First, a monadic argument (one with only one-place predicates): "some $F$ are $G$; some $G$ are $H$; so some $F$ are $H$". In symbols, $\exists x\,(Fx \land Gx)$ and $\exists x\,(Gx \land Hx)$, therefore $\exists x\,(Fx \land Hx)$. An engineering instance would be: some field units run old firmware; some overheated units run old firmware; so some field units have overheated. The overheated units may all be on the test bench. The conclusion is false if nothing is both $F$ and $H$, and the premises need a $G$ that is $F$ and a $G$ that is $H$, so two objects suffice. Figure 1(a) shows the interpretation: domain $\{1, 2\}$, $F$ = $\{1\}$, $G$ = $\{1, 2\}$, $H$ = $\{2\}$. Object 1 makes the first premise true, object 2 the second, and no object makes the conclusion true. The form is invalid.

Second, the quantifier order of Section 4.1: $\forall y\,\exists x\,Rxy$, therefore $\exists x\,\forall y\,Rxy$. Take two engineers who review each other's code and not their own: domain $\{1, 2\}$ and $R$ = $\{(1, 2), (2, 1)\}$, shown in Figure 1(b). Every author has a reviewer, so the premise is true. No one reviews both authors, because neither reviews their own code, so the conclusion is false. The form is invalid.

### 6.3 Checking a finite counter-model, and its limits

Over a finite domain whose objects all have names, quantifiers reduce to connectives: with domain $\{a, b\}$, $\forall x\,\varphi(x)$ becomes $\varphi(a) \land \varphi(b)$ and $\exists x\,\varphi(x)$ becomes $\varphi(a) \lor \varphi(b)$. Expanding the premises and conclusion this way turns a candidate counter-model into a sentential problem, which the short truth-table method of the sixth entry (Section 5.2) can check.

Small domains are usually enough, but not always. Consider three sentences about a relation $R$: no object bears $R$ to itself, $\forall x\,\neg Rxx$; $R$ is transitive, $\forall x\,\forall y\,\forall z\,((Rxy \land Ryz) \to Rxz)$; and every object bears $R$ to something, $\forall x\,\exists y\,Rxy$. The natural numbers with $R$ as "less than" are a model. No finite domain is: following $R$ from any object must eventually revisit an object in a finite domain, and transitivity then makes that object bear $R$ to itself, contradicting the first sentence. So the negation of the conjunction of these three sentences is true in every finite interpretation, yet it is not valid. No search through finite domains, however long, would reveal that. Counter-model search is a reliable way to show invalidity when it succeeds, and silent when it fails.

## 7. Derivations with quantifiers

### 7.1 The four quantifier rules

The natural-deduction system for predicate logic keeps every rule of the sixth entry (Section 7.2), now applied to predicate-logic formulas, and adds an introduction and an elimination rule for each quantifier. In stating them, $\varphi(x)$ is a formula in which $x$ may be free, and $\varphi(a)$ is the result of replacing every free occurrence of $x$ in it by the name $a$.

| Rule | From | Write | Restriction |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\forall$E | $\forall x\,\varphi(x)$ | $\varphi(a)$ for any name $a$ | none |
| $\exists$I | $\varphi(a)$ | $\exists x\,\varphi(x)$, replacing one or more occurrences of $a$ by $x$ | none |
| $\forall$I | $\varphi(a)$ | $\forall x\,\varphi(x)$, replacing every occurrence of $a$ by $x$ | $a$ occurs in no assumption that $\varphi(a)$ depends on |
| $\exists$E | $\exists x\,\varphi(x)$, an assumption $\varphi(a)$ and a later line $\psi$ | $\psi$ | $a$ occurs neither in $\exists x\,\varphi(x)$, nor in $\psi$, nor in any assumption that $\psi$ depends on other than $\varphi(a)$ |

Dependencies follow the pattern of the sixth entry. The new line rests on the dependencies of the line it came from; for $\exists$E it rests on those of the existential line together with those of $\psi$, minus the assumption $\varphi(a)$, which $\exists$E discharges.

The two elimination-and-introduction pairs encode familiar steps of informal proof. $\forall$E is **instantiation**: what holds of everything holds of S7. $\exists$I is **generalization from an instance**: S7 is faulty, so something is faulty. $\forall$I is the step a mathematician makes after "let $n$ be an arbitrary integer": a result proved about $a$ without assuming anything special about $a$ holds of everything. $\exists$E is the step after "let $c$ be such an object": knowing that something is $F$, reason about a fresh name for it, and keep only conclusions that do not mention that name.

### 7.2 Why the restrictions matter

Each restriction blocks an invalid inference. Without the restriction on $\forall$I, one could assume $Fa$ and immediately write $\forall x\,Fx$, concluding from "this unit failed" that every unit failed. The restriction rejects the step because $a$ occurs in the assumption $Fa$ that the line depends on: $a$ was not arbitrary.

Without the restrictions on $\exists$E, the following would be a derivation of $\exists x\,(Fx \land Gx)$ from $\exists x\,Fx$ and $\exists x\,Gx$: assume $Fa$ for the first existential, assume $Ga$ for the second, conjoin them and generalize. The error is reusing $a$: the object that is $F$ and the object that is $G$ need not be the same, and the counter-model is a domain $\{1, 2\}$ with $F$ = $\{1\}$ and $G$ = $\{2\}$. The rule forbids the $\exists$E step that discharges $Ga$, because the conclusion at that point still depends on the assumption $Fa$, which contains the same name.

### 7.3 Worked derivations

The calibration argument from the opening becomes $\forall x\,(Cx \to Sx), Cs \vdash Ss$, with $Cx$: $x$ passed calibration, $Sx$: $x$ is in service and $s$: S7. Instantiation does the work:

| Line | Formula | Justification | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\forall x\,(Cx \to Sx)$ | A | 1 |
| 2 | $Cs$ | A | 2 |
| 3 | $Cs \to Ss$ | 1 $\forall$E | 1 |
| 4 | $Ss$ | 3, 2 $\to$E | 1, 2 |

Chaining two universal conditionals, the predicate counterpart of the hypothetical syllogism, needs $\forall$I. The derivation reasons about an arbitrary $a$:

| Line | Formula | Justification | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\forall x\,(Fx \to Gx)$ | A | 1 |
| 2 | $\forall x\,(Gx \to Hx)$ | A | 2 |
| 3 | $Fa \to Ga$ | 1 $\forall$E | 1 |
| 4 | $Ga \to Ha$ | 2 $\forall$E | 2 |
| 5 | $Fa$ | A | 5 |
| 6 | $Ga$ | 3, 5 $\to$E | 1, 5 |
| 7 | $Ha$ | 4, 6 $\to$E | 1, 2, 5 |
| 8 | $Fa \to Ha$ | 5, 7 $\to$I | 1, 2 |
| 9 | $\forall x\,(Fx \to Hx)$ | 8 $\forall$I | 1, 2 |

The step at line 9 is legal because line 8 depends only on lines 1 and 2, and neither mentions $a$. Had line 8 still depended on the assumption $Fa$ at line 5, the generalization would be blocked.

The valid direction of the quantifier-order example, $\exists x\,\forall y\,Rxy \vdash \forall y\,\exists x\,Rxy$, uses all four rules:

| Line | Formula | Justification | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\exists x\,\forall y\,Rxy$ | A | 1 |
| 2 | $\forall y\,Ray$ | A | 2 |
| 3 | $Rab$ | 2 $\forall$E | 2 |
| 4 | $\exists x\,Rxb$ | 3 $\exists$I | 2 |
| 5 | $\forall y\,\exists x\,Rxy$ | 4 $\forall$I | 2 |
| 6 | $\forall y\,\exists x\,Rxy$ | 1, 2, 5 $\exists$E | 1 |

Line 2 names the object that line 1 says exists. Line 5 is legal because $b$ does not occur in line 2, the only assumption line 4 depends on. Line 6 is legal because $a$ occurs neither in line 1, nor in line 5, nor in any assumption of line 5 other than line 2. Any attempt to derive the converse runs into one of the restrictions, as it must: the counter-model of Section 6.2 shows the converse invalid, and the system is sound (Section 8.1).

The derivation strategies of the sixth entry still apply, with two additions. Apply $\exists$E early, with a fresh name, so that later $\forall$E steps can instantiate universal premises to that same name. Apply $\forall$I late, after every assumption that mentions the arbitrary name has been discharged.

## 8. Metatheory

### 8.1 Soundness and completeness

The definitions of the sixth entry (Section 8.1) carry over. The quantifier rules preserve the property that each line is entailed by its dependencies, which is where the restrictions earn their place, so the system is **sound**: if $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, then $\Gamma \vDash \varphi$. It is also **complete**: if $\Gamma \vDash \varphi$, then $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$. Completeness for first-order logic was proved by K. Gödel in 1929, in his doctoral dissertation, and published in 1930. Its proof is beyond this entry; it constructs a model for any set of sentences from which no contradiction can be derived.

Together the two results give a division of labor. To show that an argument form is valid, give a derivation. To show that it is invalid, give a counter-model. Soundness guarantees that a derivation never contradicts a counter-model, and completeness guarantees that for every form one of the two exists.

### 8.2 No decision procedure

Completeness does not provide a way to decide validity. A. Church and A. M. Turing showed independently, in papers published in 1936 and 1937, that no algorithm decides, for every sentence of first-order logic, whether it is valid. The difficulty is the asymmetry of Section 6.3. Every valid sentence has a derivation, so a program that lists all possible derivations in order of length will eventually find one for any valid sentence; validity is therefore **semi-decidable**. But for an invalid sentence the search never ends, and no program can always tell in advance that it should stop. Some restricted fragments are decidable; one is monadic predicate logic, in which every predicate has one place, so the categorical arguments of the fifth entry can be decided mechanically. The undecidability enters with relations such as $R$ in Section 6.3.

For engineers this result sets realistic expectations of automated reasoning tools. Theorem provers and model finders for first-order logic can settle a great many practical problems, but none can be guaranteed to terminate on every input, and a timeout is not a verdict.

### 8.3 Completeness is not incompleteness

Gödel's name is also attached to a different and more famous theorem, which is often confused with the first. The **incompleteness theorem** of 1931 concerns theories, not logic. It says, roughly, that any consistent theory whose axioms can be listed by an algorithm and which is strong enough to prove some elementary facts of arithmetic contains a sentence of its own language that it can neither prove nor refute. The completeness theorem says that the rules of logic prove every sentence true in all models. The incompleteness theorem says that the axioms of arithmetic, used with those same rules, cannot settle every arithmetical question. There is no contradiction: an undecided sentence is true in some models of the axioms and false in others, so it is not a logical consequence of them.

### 8.4 What predicate logic still leaves out

First-order logic is the standard formal language of mathematics and of much of computer science, but it has limits as a model of reasoning. It quantifies over objects, not properties, so "every property of S7 is shared by S8" needs a stronger, second-order language. It treats "every" and "some" as all-or-nothing, so "most tests pass" and "the sensor usually reads high" are outside its reach. And like sentential logic it has no place for causation, time, necessity or probability unless they are added as extra predicates. The entries that follow turn to arguments where those features are central.

## 9. Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Over a domain of builds and tests, formalize with $Bx$: $x$ is a build, $Tx$: $x$ is a test, $Sx$: $x$ is signed, $Dx$: $x$ is deployed and $Fxy$: $x$ fails on $y$. (a) No unsigned build is deployed. (b) Some test fails on every build. (c) Every build fails some test.

*Answer.* (a) $\forall x\,((Bx \land \neg Sx) \to \neg Dx)$. (b) $\exists x\,(Tx \land \forall y\,(By \to Fxy))$. (c) $\forall y\,(By \to \exists x\,(Tx \land Fxy))$, since "build $y$ fails test $x$" is the same claim as "test $x$ fails on build $y$". Sentence (b) entails (c) but not conversely.

**Exercise 2.** In $\exists x\,(Fx \land \forall y\,Rxy) \lor Gy$, which variable occurrences are free? Is the formula a sentence?

*Answer.* Inside the bracket, every $x$ is bound by $\exists x$ and the $y$ in $Rxy$ is bound by $\forall y$. The final $y$, in $Gy$, lies outside both scopes and is free, so the formula is not a sentence.

**Exercise 3.** Take the domain $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and interpret $Dxy$ as "$x$ divides $y$". Evaluate (a) $\forall x\,Dxx$, (b) $\exists x\,\forall y\,Dxy$, (c) $\forall x\,\exists y\,(Dxy \land \neg Dyx)$.

*Answer.* (a) True: every number divides itself. (b) True: 1 divides every number in the domain. (c) False: for $x = 3$, the only $y$ in the domain that 3 divides is 3 itself, and 3 also divides 3.

**Exercise 4.** Show that $\forall x\,(Fx \lor Gx)$ does not entail $\forall x\,Fx \lor \forall x\,Gx$.

*Answer.* Domain $\{1, 2\}$ with $F$ = $\{1\}$ and $G$ = $\{2\}$. Every object is $F$ or $G$, so the premise is true; object 2 is not $F$ and object 1 is not $G$, so both disjuncts of the conclusion are false. For example, every request goes to the primary or the backup server, but neither server receives every request.

**Exercise 5.** Derive $\exists x\,\neg Fx \vdash \neg\forall x\,Fx$.

*Answer.*

| Line | Formula | Justification | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\exists x\,\neg Fx$ | A | 1 |
| 2 | $\neg Fa$ | A | 2 |
| 3 | $\forall x\,Fx$ | A | 3 |
| 4 | $Fa$ | 3 $\forall$E | 3 |
| 5 | $Fa \land \neg Fa$ | 4, 2 $\land$I | 2, 3 |
| 6 | $\neg\forall x\,Fx$ | 3, 5 $\neg$I | 2 |
| 7 | $\neg\forall x\,Fx$ | 1, 2, 6 $\exists$E | 1 |

The $\exists$E step at line 7 is legal because $a$ occurs neither in line 1 nor in line 6, and line 6 depends on no assumption other than line 2.

**Exercise 6.** A colleague's model finder searched every interpretation with up to 50 objects and found no counter-model to an argument. Has the argument been shown valid?

*Answer.* No. Section 6.3 gives sentences that are true in every finite interpretation without being valid, so the absence of small counter-models does not establish validity. A derivation would, by soundness.

## 10. Summary

| Topic | Main result |
|---|---|
| Language | Names, predicates of one or more places, variables, connectives and the quantifiers $\forall$ and $\exists$; sentences are formulas with no free variables. |
| Categorical forms | "All $F$ are $G$" is $\forall x\,(Fx \to Gx)$; "some $F$ are $G$" is $\exists x\,(Fx \land Gx)$; universal claims carry no existential import. |
| Multiple quantifiers | Order matters: $\exists x\,\forall y\,Rxy$ entails $\forall y\,\exists x\,Rxy$, not conversely. |
| Semantics | An interpretation fixes a nonempty domain and the reference of names and extensions of predicates; validity is truth in every interpretation. |
| Invalidity | One counter-model proves an argument form invalid; small domains often suffice, but some invalid forms have only infinite counter-models. |
| Derivations | The sentential rules plus $\forall$E, $\exists$I, $\forall$I and $\exists$E; the restrictions on $\forall$I and $\exists$E keep the names arbitrary. |
| Metatheory | Sound and complete (Gödel, 1930), but undecidable (Church, 1936; Turing, 1937); monadic predicate logic is decidable. |

This entry completes the short course in formal logic that began with the [fourth entry](/blog/what-logic-studies). Deductive logic says what must be true if the premises are; it does not say whether the premises are true or how to find out. The [next entry](/blog/scientific-reasoning-and-causation) turns from deduction to evidence: how scientific hypotheses are tested, and how causes are told apart from correlations.
