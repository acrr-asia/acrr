The [fourth entry](/blog/what-logic-studies) argued that validity depends on logical form and that what counts as form depends on the logic used. Its example, "all stainless steels contain chromium; this alloy is a stainless steel; so it contains chromium", is valid, yet sentential logic cannot show it, because its validity rests on the words "all" and "is a". This entry introduces the oldest logic that looks inside statements at that level: **categorical logic**, the logic of statements about classes, which goes back to Aristotle's *Prior Analytics*.

Section 1 defines classes and the four standard forms of categorical statement, and shows how to translate ordinary sentences into them. Section 2 draws those forms as Venn diagrams with two circles and uses the pictures to compare statements. Section 3 extends the diagrams to three circles, and Section 4 turns them into a test for the validity of syllogisms, with a valid and an invalid worked example. Section 5 gives the traditional rules as a cross-check, and Section 6 states what the method cannot do. The diagrams follow the modern (Boolean) reading of categorical statements, explained in Section 2.4.

## 1. Classes and categorical statements

### 1.1 Classes

A **class** (or set) is a collection of things, its **members**. Two classes with exactly the same members are the same class, however they are described: "failed test runs" and "runs that returned a nonzero exit code" name one class if a run fails exactly when its exit code is nonzero. Everything not in a class $S$ makes up its **complement**, written "non-$S$". A class with no members, such as the class of perpetual-motion machines, is **empty**, and Section 2.4 shows why empty classes need care.

Categorical statements relate two classes. In "every failed run was logged", the **subject term** names the class of failed runs and the **predicate term** names the class of logged runs; the statement says that the first class lies wholly inside the second.

### 1.2 The four standard forms

Every categorical statement can be put in one of four standard forms, traditionally labeled with the letters A, E, I and O. Write $S$ for the subject class and $P$ for the predicate class.

| Form | Standard wording | What it says about the classes | Quantity and quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | All $S$ are $P$ | Nothing is in $S$ outside $P$ | Universal affirmative |
| E | No $S$ are $P$ | Nothing is in both $S$ and $P$ | Universal negative |
| I | Some $S$ are $P$ | At least one thing is in both $S$ and $P$ | Particular affirmative |
| O | Some $S$ are not $P$ | At least one thing is in $S$ outside $P$ | Particular negative |

Two conventions need stating. In logic, "some" means *at least one*, possibly all: "some sensors failed" is true if one sensor failed and also if every sensor failed. And "some $S$ are $P$" does not imply "some $S$ are not $P$", although in conversation it often suggests it. A test engineer who reports "some units passed" when all of them passed has said something true but misleading, a gap between logic and conversational suggestion that the [second entry](/blog/meaning-clarity-and-definitions) discusses.

### 1.3 Translating ordinary language

Real sentences rarely arrive in standard form, and the translation is where most mistakes are made. The table lists common patterns.

| Ordinary wording | Standard form |
|---|---|
| Every $S$ is $P$; any $S$ is $P$; if something is an $S$, it is a $P$ | All $S$ are $P$ |
| Only $P$ are $S$; none but $P$ are $S$ | All $S$ are $P$ |
| Nothing that is $S$ is $P$; there are no $S$ that are $P$ | No $S$ are $P$ |
| There is an $S$ that is $P$; at least one $S$ is $P$ | Some $S$ are $P$ |
| Not all $S$ are $P$; not every $S$ is $P$ | Some $S$ are not $P$ |
| Not a single $S$ is $P$ | No $S$ are $P$ |

The word "only" reverses the direction. "Only certified technicians may sign the release" says that everyone who may sign is a certified technician (All signers are certified technicians); it does not say that every certified technician may sign. Reading it the wrong way round turns a restriction into an entitlement.

Some sentences are ambiguous and must be clarified before they can be translated. "All the outputs are not valid" may mean "no output is valid" (E) or "not all outputs are valid" (O); only the context, or the author, can say which. A sentence without a quantifier, such as "resistors dissipate heat" or "engineers were at the meeting", is usually universal in the first case and particular in the second. Statements about a single individual ("this alloy is a stainless steel") can be handled by treating the individual as a class with one member, but Section 6 explains why that device has limits.

## 2. Venn diagrams for two classes

### 2.1 Circles and regions

John Venn introduced the diagrams that bear his name in 1880 as a way to represent the content of logical statements (Venn, 1880). A rectangle stands for everything under discussion (the **universe of discourse**) and each class is a circle inside it. Two overlapping circles $S$ and $P$ divide the rectangle into four regions: things in $S$ but not $P$, things in both, things in $P$ but not $S$ and things in neither. The circles are always drawn overlapping, whatever the statement says; it is the markings, not the geometry, that carry information.

### 2.2 Shading, marks and blanks

Two kinds of marking are used, and a blank region means something different from both.

- **Shading** a region says that the region is empty: nothing is in it.
- An **X** in a region says that the region has at least one member; one X is compatible with a single member or with thousands.
- A **blank** region carries no information: it might be empty or not.

Each of the four standard forms then becomes a single marking, as Figure 1 shows. The A form shades the part of $S$ outside $P$; the E form shades the overlap; the I form puts an X in the overlap; the O form puts an X in the part of $S$ outside $P$.

<Figure caption="Figure 1. The four standard forms on two-circle diagrams. Shading means the region is empty; an X means it has at least one member. The universal forms (A, E) only shade, and the particular forms (I, O) only mark existence.">

</Figure>

The confusion of a blank region with an empty one is the most common error in reading these diagrams. The A diagram leaves the overlap blank: "all $S$ are $P$" does not say that anything is an $S$, so it does not put an X anywhere.

### 2.3 Comparing statements with diagrams

Because each statement is a marking, two statements can be compared by comparing their diagrams.

**Contradictories.** The A form shades exactly the region in which the O form puts an X, so the two can never both be true or both be false. A region is either empty or not. The same holds for E and I, on the overlap. Each is the negation of the other in the sense of the fourth entry (Section 3.1). To refute "all pull requests were reviewed" one needs a single unreviewed pull request, not a demonstration that none were reviewed.

**Conversion.** Swapping subject and predicate gives the **converse**. The E and I diagrams are symmetric (they mark the overlap, which belongs equally to both circles), so "no $S$ are $P$" is equivalent to "no $P$ are $S$" and "some $S$ are $P$" to "some $P$ are $S$". The A and O diagrams are not symmetric. "All $S$ are $P$" shades the left crescent; its converse "all $P$ are $S$" shades the right one. They are different statements, which is the diagrammatic form of the non-equivalence noted in the fourth entry (Section 4.4).

**Contraposition.** Replacing $S$ by non-$P$ and $P$ by non-$S$ gives the **contrapositive**. "All $S$ are $P$" and "all non-$P$ are non-$S$" both say that nothing is in $S$ and outside $P$, so they shade the same region and are equivalent: if every certified part is traceable, then every untraceable part is uncertified. Contraposition is also valid for O, but not for E or I.

### 2.4 Existential import

Does "all $S$ are $P$" imply that there are some $S$? In the traditional logic taught from Aristotle to the nineteenth century, universal statements were usually read as presupposing that their subject class has members, and on that reading "all $S$ are $P$" implies "some $S$ are $P$". The modern **Boolean** reading, named after George Boole's algebra of classes and followed by the diagrams in this entry, does not make that assumption: an A or E statement only says that a region is empty.

The modern reading fits technical use better. "All requests larger than 10 MB are rejected" is a true description of a server's policy even on a day when no such request arrives; it would be odd to call the policy statement false because nobody tested it. On the Boolean reading, "all $S$ are $P$" is even true *vacuously* when $S$ is empty: "all perpetual-motion machines violate the laws of thermodynamics" is true, while "some perpetual-motion machine violates the laws of thermodynamics" is false, because there are none.

The choice matters for validity. Some inferences that were valid in traditional logic become invalid in Boolean logic unless an assumption of existence is stated as an extra premise. Section 4.6 shows a case.

## 3. Diagrams with three classes

### 3.1 Eight regions

Reasoning that involves three classes needs three overlapping circles. Labeled $S$, $P$ and $M$ (the reason for these letters appears in Section 4.1), they divide the universe into eight regions, one for every combination of being inside or outside each circle. Figure 2 numbers them; the rest of the entry refers to regions by these numbers.

<Figure caption="Figure 2. The eight regions of a three-circle diagram. Every combination of inside and outside the three circles has exactly one region, including region 8, outside all three.">
  <CtVennRegions />
</Figure>

### 3.2 Shading with three circles

A universal statement about two of the classes now shades *two* regions, because the region it empties is cut in two by the third circle. "All $S$ are $M$" says that nothing is in $S$ outside $M$, which is regions 1 and 2 together, so both are shaded. "No $S$ are $P$" empties the overlap of $S$ and $P$, which is regions 2 and 5. The general method: find the region the statement empties on a two-circle diagram, then shade every part of it in the three-circle diagram.

### 3.3 Marks that straddle a line

A particular statement is harder. "Some $S$ are $M$" says that something is in the overlap of $S$ and $M$, which is regions 4 and 5 together. If nothing else is known, the statement does not say which of the two contains the member, so the X is placed **on the line** that separates them. An X on a line means "at least one of the regions it touches has a member", and no more.

Two rules follow, and the diagram test depends on both.

1. **Draw universal statements first.** Shading can settle where an X must go. If region 4 is already shaded, "some $S$ are $M$" can only be satisfied in region 5, and the X goes there.
2. **Treat a straddling X as uncertain.** An X on the line between regions 4 and 5 does not show that region 5 has a member. If any part of the X lies in a region that is later shaded, that part is eliminated and the X effectively moves to the remaining region.

## 4. Testing syllogisms

### 4.1 Categorical syllogisms

A **categorical syllogism** is an argument with two premises and a conclusion, all categorical statements, that uses exactly three terms, each appearing in two of the three statements. The conclusion's predicate is the **major term** ($P$), its subject the **minor term** ($S$), and the term that appears in both premises but not in the conclusion is the **middle term** ($M$). The middle term is what links the two premises; in "all stainless steels contain chromium; all 316L alloys are stainless steels; so all 316L alloys contain chromium", it is "stainless steels".

Since each of the three statements can take any of the four forms, and the terms can be arranged in four ways in the premises, there are $4^3 \times 4 = 256$ syllogistic forms. Checking all of them under the Boolean reading, by enumerating which of the eight regions are empty, finds 15 valid forms, plus 9 more that are valid only when the classes are assumed to have members; textbooks list them by name (Hurley, 2015). The Venn diagram test decides any of them in a minute, without memorizing the list.

### 4.2 The procedure

The test asks whether the premises, once drawn, already contain the information the conclusion asserts.

1. Draw three overlapping circles and label them $S$, $P$ and $M$.
2. Draw the universal premises first (shading), then the particular premises (an X, on a line if the premise does not decide between two regions).
3. Do *not* draw the conclusion. Instead, identify the region the conclusion is about: for an A or E conclusion, the region it says is empty; for an I or O conclusion, the region it says has a member.
4. Inspect the diagram. A universal conclusion is supported if that whole region is already shaded. A particular conclusion is supported if an X lies wholly inside that region, counting an X whose other parts lie only in shaded regions.
5. If the conclusion is supported, the syllogism is valid; otherwise it is invalid, and the unmarked part of the diagram describes a counterexample.

The test works because a diagram of the premises represents every situation in which the premises are true. If the conclusion's information is already in the picture, no such situation makes the conclusion false.

### 4.3 First example: chaining universals

Consider a release process. All code that passes review is merged into the main branch; all code merged into the main branch ships in the next build. Therefore all code that passes review ships in the next build. With $S$ for reviewed code, $M$ for merged code and $P$ for code in the next build, the form is: All $M$ are $P$; all $S$ are $M$; therefore all $S$ are $P$.

The first premise shades the parts of $M$ outside $P$, regions 4 and 7. The second shades the parts of $S$ outside $M$, regions 1 and 2. The conclusion is about the part of $S$ outside $P$, which is regions 1 and 4. Both are shaded, so the conclusion is already in the diagram, and the syllogism is valid. (Whether the premises are *true* of a real release process is a separate question, as the [third entry](/blog/arguments-and-their-structure) stressed.)

### 4.4 A valid syllogism with a particular premise

A purchasing engineer reasons as follows. Every component rated for 125 °C is approved for the engine bay. Some parts in this order are rated for 125 °C. So some parts in this order are approved for the engine bay. Let $S$ be the parts in the order, $M$ the components rated for 125 °C and $P$ the components approved for the engine bay: All $M$ are $P$; some $S$ are $M$; therefore some $S$ are $P$.

The universal premise goes first: it shades the parts of $M$ outside $P$, regions 4 and 7. The particular premise says that something is in the overlap of $S$ and $M$, regions 4 and 5. Region 4 is already shaded, so the X goes in region 5. The conclusion needs a member in the overlap of $S$ and $P$, regions 2 and 5, outlined in Figure 3. The X in region 5 provides it, so the syllogism is valid.

<Figure caption="Figure 3. A valid syllogism: all M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are P. Region numbers follow Figure 2. Shading from the first premise forces the X of the second premise into region 5, inside the dashed overlap of S and P that the conclusion is about.">

</Figure>

If the particular premise had been drawn first, its X would have straddled regions 4 and 5, and the shading of region 4 would then have eliminated half of it. The result is the same, but drawing universals first avoids the extra step.

### 4.5 An invalid syllogism

A recruiter reasons as follows. Every certified pilot has passed a medical examination. Some of today's applicants have passed a medical examination. So some of today's applicants are certified pilots. With $S$ for today's applicants, $M$ for people who have passed the medical examination and $P$ for certified pilots: All $P$ are $M$; some $S$ are $M$; therefore some $S$ are $P$.

The universal premise shades the parts of $P$ outside $M$, regions 2 and 3. The particular premise puts an X in the overlap of $S$ and $M$, regions 4 and 5, and neither is shaded, so the X goes on the line between them (Figure 4). The conclusion needs a member in regions 2 or 5. Region 2 is shaded and the X might lie entirely in region 4, so the premises do not guarantee the conclusion: the syllogism is invalid.

<Figure caption="Figure 4. An invalid syllogism: all P are M; some S are M; therefore some S are P. The X of the second premise straddles regions 4 and 5. It may lie in region 4, outside P, so the dashed overlap of S and P need not have a member.">
  <CtVennInvalid />
</Figure>

The diagram also describes a counterexample: a situation in which region 4 has a member and region 5 is empty. In words, some applicants have passed the medical examination without being pilots, and none of the applicants is a pilot. That situation is easy to imagine, which confirms the verdict. The pattern is common in practice: "every compromised host shows this log entry; some of our hosts show it; so some of our hosts are compromised" fails for the same reason.

### 4.6 When existence must be assumed

Consider: all $M$ are $P$; all $M$ are $S$; therefore some $S$ are $P$. The first premise shades regions 4 and 7, the second regions 6 and 7. The conclusion needs an X in regions 2 or 5, and the diagram has no X at all, because both premises are universal. On the Boolean reading the syllogism is invalid.

The reason is an empty middle class. Read $M$ as "perpetual-motion machines", $P$ as "devices that violate thermodynamics" and $S$ as "devices that would make their inventor rich". Both premises are vacuously true, and the conclusion, which asserts that some actual device is in both $S$ and $P$, is false. If an extra premise states that $M$ has members ("there is at least one $M$"), an X goes in the only unshaded part of $M$, region 5, and the argument becomes valid. This is the precise sense in which traditional logic, which assumed existence, accepted forms that modern logic rejects: the disagreement is about an unstated premise, not about the rules of inference.

## 5. The traditional rules as a cross-check

Before diagrams, syllogisms were checked with a short list of rules, and the rules remain a useful cross-check. They rely on the idea of **distribution**: a term is distributed in a statement when the statement says something about every member of the class it names. The A form distributes its subject; the E form distributes both terms; the I form distributes neither; the O form distributes its predicate (it says that a certain member of $S$ is excluded from the whole of $P$).

Under the Boolean reading, a categorical syllogism is valid if and only if it satisfies all five of the following rules (Hurley, 2015).

| Rule | Name of the fallacy when broken |
|---|---|
| 1. The middle term is distributed at least once. | Undistributed middle |
| 2. A term distributed in the conclusion is distributed in its premise. | Illicit major or illicit minor |
| 3. The premises are not both negative. | Exclusive premises |
| 4. A negative premise requires a negative conclusion, and a negative conclusion requires a negative premise. | Drawing an affirmative conclusion from a negative premise (or the converse) |
| 5. If both premises are universal, the conclusion is not particular. | Existential fallacy |

The examples above agree with the rules. The syllogism of Section 4.5 breaks Rule 1: its middle term, "passed the medical examination", is the predicate of an A statement and of an I statement, and is distributed in neither. The syllogism of Section 4.6 breaks Rule 5. The rules are quicker for an experienced reader, but the diagram has an advantage they lack: when an argument is invalid, it shows the counterexample.

## 6. Limits of Venn diagrams

The method is reliable within a narrow domain, and the domain is worth stating exactly.

- **Number of classes.** Three circles give all eight regions, but four circles cannot be drawn so that all 16 combinations appear; four suitably placed ellipses can. Beyond four or five classes, diagrams become hard to draw and easy to misread.
- **Individuals.** A diagram describes classes, not named things. "This alloy is a stainless steel" can be forced into a class of one member, but statements that mix several individuals and classes quickly become clumsy.
- **Compound statements.** "Either the fault is in the sensor or the controller is misconfigured" relates whole statements, not classes, and has no natural marking. Sentential logic, the subject of the [sixth entry](/blog/sentential-logic), handles it directly.
- **Relations.** "Every task depends on some other task" involves a relation between members of one class. No marking of circles expresses it, and arguments that turn on relations, such as "every module imports a library; every library is tested; so every module imports something tested", are beyond the method.
- **Quantities other than all, none and some.** "Most", "at least two" and "exactly one" cannot be expressed with shading and a single X.

The last three limits are why logic did not stop with syllogisms. Predicate logic, the subject of the [seventh entry](/blog/predicate-logic), replaces the four forms with quantifiers, variables and relations, and contains categorical logic as a special case: "all $S$ are $P$" becomes $\forall x\,(Sx \to Px)$.

## 7. Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Put each sentence in standard form and name its form. (a) "Only signed images are deployed." (b) "Not every alarm is critical." (c) "There is no safe dose of this toxin."

*Answer.* (a) All deployed images are signed images (A). (b) Some alarms are not critical (O). (c) No dose of this toxin is a safe dose (E).

**Exercise 2.** Which of these pairs are equivalent? (a) "No valid inputs are rejected" and "no rejected inputs are valid". (b) "All tested builds are stable" and "all stable builds are tested". (c) "All tested builds are stable" and "all unstable builds are untested".

*Answer.* (a) Equivalent: both shade the overlap (conversion of E). (b) Not equivalent: they shade different crescents (conversion of A). (c) Equivalent: contraposition of A.

**Exercise 3.** Test each syllogism with a Venn diagram, using the region numbers of Figure 2. (a) No $M$ are $P$; all $S$ are $M$; therefore no $S$ are $P$. (b) No $M$ are $P$; no $S$ are $M$; therefore no $S$ are $P$. (c) Some $M$ are not $P$; all $M$ are $S$; therefore some $S$ are not $P$. (d) All $S$ are $M$; some $M$ are $P$; therefore some $S$ are $P$.

*Answer.* (a) Valid. The premises shade regions 5 and 6, then 1 and 2; the conclusion's region, 2 and 5, is fully shaded. (b) Invalid. The premises shade 5 and 6, then 4 and 5; region 2 stays blank, so the overlap of $S$ and $P$ may have members (and Rule 3 is broken). (c) Valid. The universal premise shades 6 and 7; the X for "some $M$ are not $P$" belongs in regions 4 or 7, and region 7 is shaded, so it goes in region 4, which lies in $S$ outside $P$, as the conclusion requires. (d) Invalid. The universal premise shades 1 and 2; the X for "some $M$ are $P$" straddles regions 5 and 6, and may lie in region 6, outside $S$ (undistributed middle).

**Exercise 4.** Give a concrete counterexample for Exercise 3(d), with real classes.

*Answer.* Let $S$ be squares, $M$ rectangles and $P$ shapes with two sides of different lengths. "All squares are rectangles" and "some rectangles have two sides of different lengths" are true, and "some squares have two sides of different lengths" is false.

**Exercise 5.** A specification says "all emergency stops cut motor power" and "all emergency stops are latched in hardware". A reviewer concludes "some hardware-latched mechanisms cut motor power". Is the inference valid? What would make it valid?

*Answer.* With $M$ = emergency stops, it is the form of Section 4.6: invalid on the Boolean reading, because both premises are universal (Rule 5). It becomes valid when the premise "there is at least one emergency stop" is added, which is presumably true of the machine but should be stated.

## 8. Summary

| Idea | Main point |
|---|---|
| Categorical statement | Relates two classes in one of four forms: A (all), E (no), I (some), O (some not). |
| Translation | "Only $P$ are $S$" means all $S$ are $P$; "not all" is an O statement; ambiguous sentences must be clarified first. |
| Venn diagram markings | Shading means empty, an X means at least one member, a blank means no information. |
| Comparing statements | A and O, E and I are contradictories; E and I convert; A and O contrapose. |
| Existential import | The Boolean reading does not assume that classes have members; universal statements about empty classes are vacuously true. |
| Three-circle diagrams | Eight regions; universal premises are drawn first, and an undecided X goes on a line. |
| Syllogism test | Valid when the premise diagram already shows the conclusion; otherwise the blank regions describe a counterexample. |
| Valid forms | 15 under the Boolean reading, plus 9 that need an existence premise. |
| Limits | No individuals, compound statements, relations or numerical quantifiers; at most a few classes. |

Categorical logic analyzes statements down to their classes but treats each statement as a whole when it comes to "and", "or", "not" and "if". The [next entry](/blog/sentential-logic) takes the opposite cut: it ignores the inside of simple statements and studies exactly those connectives, with truth tables to decide validity and natural-deduction proofs to derive conclusions step by step.
