Every entry in this series so far has met at least one way of reasoning badly. The [fourth entry](/blog/what-logic-studies) showed that an argument can have true premises and a true conclusion and still be invalid. The [fifth](/blog/categorical-logic-and-venn-diagrams) and [sixth](/blog/sentential-logic) gave tests that expose invalid syllogisms and invalid sentential forms. The [eighth](/blog/scientific-reasoning-and-causation) warned against reading causation into correlation, and the [ninth](/blog/probability-and-statistical-reasoning) showed how ignoring a base rate turns a good test into a misleading one. Those errors were met one at a time, each beside the method that detects it. This entry gathers them into one map and adds the informal fallacies and cognitive biases that the formal methods do not catch.

The entry defines a fallacy, reviews the formal fallacies, works through four families of informal fallacy and turns to the cognitive biases that make these errors tempting. It ends with the limits of fallacy-spotting and with procedures that reduce bias. One short listing uses Python 3 with no libraries.

## 1. What a fallacy is

### 1.1 Mistakes of reasoning, not mistakes of fact

Someone who records a supply voltage as 3.3 V when it is in fact 3.1 V has made a mistake of fact: the belief is false, but the reasoning that produced it may have been fine (perhaps the meter was miscalibrated). Someone who concludes that a regulator is faulty because "faulty regulators cause low voltage, and the voltage is low" has made a mistake of reasoning: even if every premise is true, the conclusion does not follow, because many other causes produce low voltage.

A **fallacy** is a mistake of reasoning of a recognizable, recurring type. Many textbooks restrict the term to arguments whose premises fail to support their conclusions. This entry casts the net slightly wider. The question "Why did the new library make the build slower?" (Section 3.3) infers nothing, yet it slips an unestablished claim past the listener; the review comment that condemns nitpicking while nitpicking (Section 3.1) is a single assertion, yet it undercuts itself. Both are failures of reasoning that recur, which is what earns a pattern a name.

### 1.2 A map of the territory

Classifications of fallacies differ from book to book, and none is complete. The grouping used here, which follows the topic plan of the *Critical Thinking Web* of Lau and Chan, separates formal fallacies from four families of informal fallacy:

- **Formal fallacies** fail the question "is there a counterexample with the same form?" The defect is in the structure alone.
- **Fallacies of inconsistency** fail the question "could all of these claims be true together, and can this claim be asserted without undercutting itself?"
- **Fallacies of presumption** fail the question "would the people in this discussion grant every assumption, including the ones buried in the wording?"
- **Fallacies of relevance** fail the question "if this reason were false, would the case for the conclusion change at all?" They also include leaving out a reason that would change it.
- **Fallacies of insufficient evidence** pass the relevance question but fail "is the support strong enough to carry this conclusion?"

The families overlap. A hasty generalization from one striking case is a fallacy of insufficient evidence, and the case is usually striking because of a bias toward vivid examples. The point of the map is not to file every error in exactly one drawer but to have a short list of questions to ask of any argument. Is the form valid? Are the claims consistent? Are the assumptions acceptable? Are the reasons relevant, and are they strong enough?

### 1.3 Fallacies and biases

A **cognitive bias** is a systematic tendency of human judgment to depart from a standard of correct reasoning in a predictable direction. A fallacy is a defect in a piece of reasoning, which can be written down and inspected; a bias is a tendency of the reasoner, which may produce no explicit argument at all. A person anchored by an irrelevant number (Section 6.2) has drawn no inference; their estimate has been pulled without their noticing. Biases explain why fallacies feel convincing.

## 2. Formal fallacies

### 2.1 Fallacies of the conditional

The two classic formal fallacies concern a conditional $P \to Q$. **Affirming the consequent** infers $P$ from $P \to Q$ and $Q$; **denying the antecedent** infers $\neg Q$ from $P \to Q$ and $\neg P$. Both look like their valid neighbors, modus ponens and modus tollens, which is why they are tempting.

| Form | Premises | Conclusion | Valid? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Modus ponens | $P \to Q$, $P$ | $Q$ | Yes |
| Modus tollens | $P \to Q$, $\neg Q$ | $\neg P$ | Yes |
| Affirming the consequent | $P \to Q$, $Q$ | $P$ | No |
| Denying the antecedent | $P \to Q$, $\neg P$ | $\neg Q$ | No |

The truth-table test of the [sixth entry](/blog/sentential-logic) finds a counterexample to both invalid forms in one row: $P$ false and $Q$ true, where every premise is true and the conclusion false. In words: "If the build server is down, the nightly job fails. The nightly job failed. So the build server is down." The job can fail for many other reasons; that is the counterexample row.

Affirming the consequent has a respectable cousin. Confirming a hypothesis $H$ by a successful prediction $E$ has the shape "if $H$ then $E$; $E$; so $H$", which the [eighth entry](/blog/scientific-reasoning-and-causation) (Section 2.3) noted is confirmation, not proof. As an inductive argument it can be strong, but only to the extent that $E$ would have been unlikely if $H$ were false, which is the comparison of likelihoods behind Bayes' theorem in the [ninth entry](/blog/probability-and-statistical-reasoning). A prediction that would have come true anyway confirms nothing.

### 2.2 Syllogistic fallacies

The [fifth entry](/blog/categorical-logic-and-venn-diagrams) tested categorical syllogisms with Venn diagrams. The errors that test catches have traditional names:

- The **undistributed middle**: the middle term is never distributed, so the two premises may talk about different parts of it. "All memory leaks increase resident memory. All caches increase resident memory. So all caches are memory leaks." Both premises place their subject inside the class of things that increase resident memory, and nothing connects the two subjects to each other.
- **Illicit major** and **illicit minor**: a term is distributed in the conclusion but not in its premise, so the conclusion speaks of all members of a class when the premises spoke only of some.
- **Exclusive premises**: both premises are negative, so neither links the two end terms.

In each case the Venn diagram shows it: after both premises are drawn, the region that the conclusion needs to be empty (or occupied) is left undecided.

### 2.3 The quantifier-shift fallacy

The [seventh entry](/blog/predicate-logic) introduced quantifiers, which allow a subtle error: moving an existential quantifier in front of a universal one. From $\forall x \, \exists y \, R(x, y)$ it does not follow that $\exists y \, \forall x \, R(x, y)$. "Every request is handled by some worker" does not imply "some worker handles every request". A model with two requests and two workers, each request handled by a different worker, makes the premise true and the conclusion false. The same shift appears in everyday arguments: "every event has a cause, so there is one thing that causes every event." It also appears in specifications. "For every input there is a timeout that suffices" is much weaker than "there is a timeout that suffices for every input".

### 2.4 Equivocation: an informal fallacy that imitates a valid form

Some arguments have a valid form on paper and still fail because a word changes meaning between premises. This is **equivocation**, the fallacy that ambiguity makes possible (the [second entry](/blog/meaning-clarity-and-definitions), Sections 6.1 and 7.2). "A model that overfits is too complex. This model is simple: it has only one layer. So it does not overfit." The first premise uses *complex* for the capacity of the model relative to the data; the second uses it for the number of layers. Written in symbols, the argument looks valid only because one symbol stands for two different predicates. The remedy follows from the second entry: define the term once and check that every premise uses it in that sense.

## 3. Fallacies of inconsistency and presumption

### 3.1 Inconsistency and self-defeating claims

The [fourth entry](/blog/what-logic-studies) (Sections 4.1 and 4.2) defined inconsistency and self-defeating claims; here they reappear as fallacies. Accepting an inconsistent set is a fallacy because at least one of the claims must be false, whatever the facts are. Inconsistency is easy to spot in two adjacent sentences and hard to spot across a long document. A requirements specification that says in one section "the device shall never store user data" and in another "the device shall cache the last ten user sessions" is inconsistent, though no single sentence is.

A self-defeating claim undermines its own truth or its own grounds. A style guide whose first rule reads "never begin a rule with *never*" breaks itself in the act of stating it. A practical variant is an assertion that the speaker's own act contradicts: the review comment "nitpicking comments waste everyone's time" left as a nitpick, or a policy against meetings announced at a meeting. The claim could be true, but asserting it in that way counts against it.

### 3.2 Begging the question

An argument **begs the question** when a premise assumes the conclusion. A subtler form assumes something that a reasonable opponent would accept only if they already accepted the conclusion. The circle usually runs through a definition or a trusted source. "Our benchmark is the right measure of performance, because systems that perform well on it are the high-performance systems." The premise defines high performance as doing well on the benchmark, which is what was in question.

Circularity is not invalidity: "$P$, so $P$" is valid. Its defect is that it cannot move anyone who doubts the conclusion, which is the job of an argument. That is why it is classed as a fallacy of presumption.

### 3.3 Loaded questions and false dilemmas

A **loaded question** (or complex question) presupposes something that has not been established, so that any direct answer concedes it. "Why did the new library make the build slower?" presupposes that the build is slower and that the library is the cause. The right response is to answer the presupposition first: was the build slower, measured how and against what baseline?

A **false dilemma** presents two options as the only ones when others exist. "Either we rewrite the service from scratch or we live with its bugs" ignores incremental refactoring, better tests and replacing only the worst module. Some dilemmas are genuine (a binary flag is on or off), so the test is to ask whether the two options are exhaustive and exclusive. Listing every option before comparing any of them, as the decision methods of the [eleventh entry](/blog/strategic-thinking-and-decisions) require, is the routine defense.

## 4. Fallacies of relevance

### 4.1 Arguments against the person

The **ad hominem** fallacy (Latin, "to the person") rejects a claim because of something about the person who makes it rather than because of the merits of the claim. "His proposal for a new memory allocator can be ignored; he has been at the company for only three months." Whether the allocator is better is a question about fragmentation, throughput and latency; the proposer's tenure has no bearing on it. The *tu quoque* ("you too") answers a criticism by pointing out that the critic does the same thing, which may show hypocrisy but does not show the criticism is wrong. The **genetic fallacy** judges a claim by its origin: "that design pattern came from a consultancy that went bankrupt" says nothing about whether the pattern is sound.

Attacking the person is not always fallacious. When an argument rests on someone's *testimony*, their reliability is relevant: a vendor reporting benchmarks for its own product gives grounds for checking the claim more carefully. The fallacy lies in treating such facts as a refutation rather than as a reason to look at the evidence.

### 4.2 Appeals to popularity, pity, tradition and authority

Several fallacies of relevance substitute a social fact for evidence about the question:

- **Appeal to popularity** (*ad populum*): "Most teams use this framework, so it is the best choice for our system." Popularity says something about support and hiring, not about fitness for a particular problem.
- **Appeal to pity** (*ad misericordiam*): "This paper should be accepted; the authors worked on it for three years." Effort and hardship do not bear on whether the results hold.
- **Appeal to tradition**: "We have always deployed on Fridays." That a practice is old shows that it has not yet failed badly enough to be dropped, not that it is the best available.
- **Appeal to inappropriate authority**: citing an authority outside their field, or citing one expert against a settled consensus of others.

When the conclusion is a value judgment, the first three appeals also need a hidden bridging premise, as the [tenth entry](/blog/reasoning-about-values) (Section 3.3) showed. Appeal to authority in general is not a fallacy. Nobody can verify every claim personally, and relying on a recognized expert speaking within their field, on a standard or on a reviewed textbook is how knowledge is shared. The appeal goes wrong when the authority lacks expertise on the question, when experts disagree or when the authority is used to end a discussion that the evidence could settle.

### 4.3 Straw men and red herrings

A **straw man** misrepresents an opposing position, usually by making it more extreme or less careful, and then refutes the misrepresentation. If a colleague proposes adding type annotations to the public interface of a module, the reply "so you want us to stop shipping and annotate every local variable in the codebase" attacks a position nobody holds. The antidote is the principle of charity of the [third entry](/blog/arguments-and-their-structure) (Section 2.2): restate the other side's argument in its strongest form before criticizing it.

A **red herring** changes the subject to something easier to discuss. Asked whether a release meets its latency requirement, a manager who answers by describing how hard the team has worked has offered a red herring. Its cousin, **suppressed evidence**, leaves out relevant facts that count against the conclusion, as when a report of a model's accuracy omits the subgroup on which it fails.

## 5. Fallacies of insufficient evidence

### 5.1 Hasty generalization and the single case

A **hasty generalization** draws a general conclusion from too few cases or from cases that are not representative. "I ran the new compiler on my laptop and it was faster, so it is faster" generalizes from one machine, one program and probably one run. The cases are relevant but too few and perhaps atypical. The sampling ideas of the [ninth entry](/blog/probability-and-statistical-reasoning) say how many cases are needed and how they should be chosen.

A sample can also be biased by the way it was collected. **Survivorship bias** arises when only the cases that passed some filter are visible. Studying only the open-source projects still maintained, to learn what makes projects succeed, makes practices common to failed and successful projects alike look like causes of success.

### 5.2 Appeal to ignorance

An **appeal to ignorance** (*ad ignorantiam*) concludes that a claim is false because it has not been proved true, or true because it has not been proved false. "No one has shown that this optimization breaks anything, so it is safe."

The phrase "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" is a useful warning but not exactly right. Absence of evidence *is* evidence of absence when a search that would probably have found the evidence came up empty. Bayes' theorem from the ninth entry makes this exact. Suppose that before testing, the probability that a change introduced a regression is 0.3, and that the test suite never fails when there is no regression. If the suite would detect a regression with probability 0.9, a clean run lowers the probability to

$$
P(\text{regression} \mid \text{clean}) = \frac{0.3 \times 0.1}{0.3 \times 0.1 + 0.7 \times 1} = 0.041.
$$

If the suite would catch it with probability only 0.2, the same clean run leaves $0.3 \times 0.8 / (0.3 \times 0.8 + 0.7) = 0.255$, barely below the starting point. The fallacy is to treat a weak search as if it were a thorough one.

### 5.3 Causal fallacies

Section 9.1 of the eighth entry listed the recurring causal mistakes. They include **post hoc ergo propter hoc** ("after this, therefore because of this"), which infers causation from order in time, along with treating correlation as causation, reversing the direction of cause, assuming a single cause and ignoring regression to the mean. They belong in this catalog as fallacies of insufficient evidence: the observed association is relevant, but it does not exclude the rival explanations. Regression to the mean, explained in Section 3.4 of the ninth entry, is the easiest to miss. Modules picked for refactoring because they had the most bug reports last quarter will tend to have fewer next quarter, refactored or not, because part of the extreme count was bad luck.

### 5.4 Statistical fallacies

**Base-rate neglect** judges the probability of a hypothesis from the reliability of the evidence while ignoring how common the hypothesis was to begin with. Section 5.2 of the ninth entry worked through an inspection example. The same arithmetic applies to any alarm: if a fault detector catches 99% of real faults, stays silent for 95% of healthy units and 1 unit in 200 is faulty, then of 10 000 units, 49.5 faulty ones and 497.5 healthy ones raise an alarm on average. Only $49.5 / (49.5 + 497.5) = 0.090$ of alarms, about one in eleven, signal a real fault.

The **gambler's fallacy** (Section 3.1 of the ninth entry) expects independent random events to balance out in the short run: after five heads in a row, tails feels "due", yet for a fair coin its probability is still one half. Expecting a streak to continue, when the events are independent, is the same mistake in the other direction.

The fallacies of **composition** and **division** move a property between a whole and its parts without justification. Composition: every component passed its unit tests, so the system works (integration can still fail). Division: the system is fault-tolerant, so each node is fault-tolerant (the tolerance may come from redundancy among nodes that are individually fragile).

### 5.5 Slippery slopes and the naturalistic fallacy

A **slippery slope** argument claims that a first step will lead through a chain of further steps to an unacceptable outcome. "If we allow one exception to the code-review rule, soon nobody will review anything." The argument is fallacious when the links in the chain are asserted rather than supported. It can be legitimate when each link is likely and the chain is short. The probability that every link holds is the product of the link probabilities, each conditional on the links before it, so five links that each hold with conditional probability 0.8 all hold with probability $0.8^5 = 0.33$. Long chains of individually plausible steps are much weaker than they feel.

The **naturalistic fallacy**, in the broad sense, infers what ought to be from what is, across the is-ought gap of the tenth entry (Section 3.1; Section 3.3 notes Moore's narrower original sense). "Users already ignore most warnings, so it is fine to show fewer of them." The factual premise may be relevant, but a value judgment also needs an evaluative premise, here something about the costs of missed warnings.

## 6. Cognitive biases

### 6.1 Two modes of thinking

The [first entry](/blog/what-is-critical-thinking) (Section 4.1) distinguished fast, automatic System 1 thinking from slow, deliberate System 2 thinking. Most of the time System 1 works well, and System 2 accepts its suggestions without checking them. On Kahneman's account, many biases arise when System 1 answers an easier question than the one asked and System 2 does not notice the substitution (Kahneman, 2011).

In an influential paper, Tversky and Kahneman (1974) described three such shortcuts, or **heuristics**, and the biases they produce. The **representativeness** heuristic judges probability by similarity to a stereotype, which leads to base-rate neglect (Section 5.4). The **availability** heuristic judges frequency by how easily examples come to mind, so recent or vivid events, such as a dramatic outage, are judged more frequent than they are. **Anchoring and adjustment**, the third, is treated next.

### 6.2 Anchoring and framing

**Anchoring** is the tendency of an estimate to stay close to a number that was presented first, even when that number is irrelevant. In Tversky and Kahneman's demonstrations, estimates of a quantity were pulled toward an arbitrary starting value that the subjects had seen generated at random (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974). In engineering, the first estimate spoken in a planning meeting tends to anchor the rest.

**Framing effects** occur when logically equivalent descriptions of the same choice lead to different decisions. Tversky and Kahneman showed that people tend to avoid risk when the options are described in terms of gains (lives saved) and to seek risk when the same options are described in terms of losses (lives lost) (Tversky and Kahneman, 1981). "This release fixes 90% of the known crashes" and "this release leaves 10% of the known crashes unfixed" carry the same information. A careful reader translates both into the same numbers before reacting.

### 6.3 Confirmation bias

**Confirmation bias** is the tendency to seek, notice and remember evidence that supports what one already believes, and to discount evidence against it. A classic experiment by Wason (1960) gave subjects a sequence of three numbers that fitted a hidden rule and asked them to discover the rule by proposing new sequences. Many subjects formed a narrow hypothesis and proposed only sequences that fitted it. Every test came back positive, and they announced their hypothesis with confidence, though the hidden rule was much more general; only a sequence their hypothesis forbade could have shown them wrong.

The engineering form is familiar. A developer who believes a bug lies in the network layer adds logging there, finds some anomaly (there is always some anomaly), and stops. The corrective is the habit of testing that the eighth entry described for scientific hypotheses: ask what observation would show the hypothesis false, and look for that.

### 6.4 Overconfidence and the planning fallacy

**Overconfidence** is the tendency to hold beliefs more firmly than the evidence warrants. In a survey by Svenson (1981), most drivers rated themselves as safer and more skillful than the median driver, which cannot be true of most of them. A well-calibrated forecaster who gives 90% intervals for many quantities should see about 90% of the true values fall inside them; studies of such intervals typically find far fewer inside, because the intervals are too narrow (Kahneman, 2011).

The **planning fallacy** is overconfidence about one's own projects: plans and forecasts stay close to a best-case scenario and neglect the record of similar projects (Kahneman, 2011). Kahneman's recommended remedy is the *outside view*: find a reference class of comparable projects, start from their distribution of outcomes and adjust from there, rather than building the estimate only from the details of the case at hand.

### 6.5 Seeing patterns in noise

The **clustering illusion** is the tendency to see meaningful clusters or streaks in random data. Random sequences contain longer runs than intuition expects. The listing below computes exactly the probability that 20 flips of a fair coin contain a run of at least $k$ identical outcomes (all heads or all tails), by counting the sequences that do not.

```python
def p_longest_run_at_least(n, k):
    """Probability that n fair coin flips contain a run of k or more equal outcomes."""
    # counts[L] = number of sequences so far with no run >= k that end in a run of length L
    counts = {1: 2}
    for _ in range(n - 1):
        new = {1: sum(counts.values())}          # next flip differs: run restarts
        for L, c in counts.items():
            if L + 1 < k:
                new[L + 1] = new.get(L + 1, 0) + c   # next flip repeats: run grows
        counts = new
    return 1 - sum(counts.values()) / 2**n

for k in (4, 5, 6):
    print(f"n = 20, run >= {k}: {p_longest_run_at_least(20, k):.3f}")
```

```text
n = 20, run >= 4: 0.768
n = 20, run >= 5: 0.458
n = 20, run >= 6: 0.237
```

A run of five or more identical results in 20 fair trials happens almost half the time. Suppose two equally fast configurations are compared in 20 benchmark runs, each run equally likely to favor either. A streak of five wins for one of them is then unremarkable, though it feels like a signal. The clustering illusion and the gambler's fallacy share one misconception: that random sequences should look evenly mixed.

### 6.6 Sunk costs and hindsight

The **sunk cost fallacy** continues a project because of what has already been spent on it, although only future costs and benefits bear on whether to continue. **Hindsight bias** is the tendency, after an outcome is known, to believe it was more predictable than it was. It distorts post-incident reviews: signals that seem glaring afterward were buried among many others at the time.

## 7. Using the catalog well

### 7.1 Naming a fallacy is a claim, not a verdict

The names in this entry are tools for diagnosis, and a diagnosis can be wrong. Most informal fallacies are fallacious only in some contexts, as Sections 4.1, 4.2 and 5.5 showed for attacks on a witness, appeals to authority and slippery slopes. Saying "that is an ad hominem" does not settle anything by itself. A useful criticism explains why the consideration is irrelevant or the evidence insufficient *in this case*.

### 7.2 Arguing from a fallacy

Finding a fallacy in an argument shows that the argument fails to support its conclusion. It does not show that the conclusion is false. Inferring falsehood from a bad argument is itself fallacious, and it has its own name: the **argument from fallacy**. In sentential terms it is an instance of denying the antecedent: with $P$ for "the argument is good" and $Q$ for "the conclusion is true", it infers $\neg Q$ from $P \to Q$ and $\neg P$. A colleague may defend the right design for the wrong reasons; the task is then to find better reasons, not to reject the design.

### 7.3 Procedures that reduce bias

Knowing about a bias does little by itself to remove it: Kahneman (2011) reports that his own intuitions remained prone to the biases he studied, because they act before deliberate reasoning starts. What helps is a change of procedure that makes the bias harder to act on:

- **Consider the opposite.** Before accepting a conclusion, write down the strongest reasons it might be false.
- **Collect estimates independently** before discussing them, so that the first number spoken does not anchor the rest.
- **Use the outside view.** Start forecasts from the record of similar projects (Section 6.4).
- **Hold a premortem.** Before committing to a plan, imagine that it has failed and ask each participant to write the most likely reasons; Kahneman describes this technique, devised by Gary Klein, as a way to legitimize doubt in a group that has already committed.
- **Blind the analysis.** Fix the evaluation metric, the test set and the stopping rule before seeing results.
- **Use checklists.** The questions in Section 1.2 make a serviceable checklist for arguments.

## 8. Exercises

Name the main fallacy or bias in each passage, and say briefly why it fails. Some passages admit more than one reasonable answer.

1. "Our new caching layer must be working: since we deployed it, the error rate has dropped."
2. "You cannot trust her criticism of our test coverage; her own project has no tests at all."
3. "Either we adopt microservices now or the monolith will collapse under its own weight."
4. "Nobody has ever found a security hole in our protocol, so it is secure." (Under what condition would this argument become reasonably strong?)
5. "All good engineers write documentation. Priya writes documentation. So Priya is a good engineer."
6. "This classifier flags 98% of fraudulent transactions and only 1% of legitimate ones, so a flagged transaction is almost certainly fraud." Assume 1 transaction in 1000 is fraudulent; what is the probability that a flagged transaction is fraudulent?
7. A team's first estimate for a migration is "about two weeks". After discussion the final estimate is three weeks. Similar migrations at the company have taken six to ten weeks.
8. "For every bug there is a test that would catch it. So there is a test that would catch every bug."

**Answers.**

1. *Post hoc ergo propter hoc*. The drop followed the deployment, but other changes, a change in traffic or regression to the mean could explain it. A comparison with a control (Section 5.3 and the eighth entry) is needed.
2. *Tu quoque*, a form of ad hominem. Her own practice is irrelevant to whether the criticism of this project's coverage is correct.
3. False dilemma. Gradual extraction of services, better modularization within the monolith or doing nothing for now are other options; the second disjunct is also an unsupported slippery slope.
4. Appeal to ignorance. The argument becomes reasonably strong when the protocol has been examined thoroughly by capable people who were trying to break it, so that a hole, if present, would probably have been found (Section 5.2).
5. Affirming the consequent, or in categorical form the undistributed middle: "writes documentation" is the middle term and is not distributed in either premise. Bad engineers may write documentation too.
6. Base-rate neglect. Per 100 000 transactions, 100 are fraudulent and 98 are flagged; 99 900 are legitimate and 999 are flagged. The probability is $98 / (98 + 999) = 0.089$, about 9%.
7. Anchoring and the planning fallacy. The first number anchored the discussion, and the estimate ignored the reference class of similar migrations; an outside-view estimate would start from six to ten weeks.
8. Quantifier-shift fallacy. $\forall b \, \exists t \, \mathrm{Catches}(t, b)$ does not imply $\exists t \, \forall b \, \mathrm{Catches}(t, b)$.

## 9. Summary

| Kind of error | Typical members | Detection question |
|---|---|---|
| Formal fallacy | Affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, undistributed middle, quantifier shift | Is there a counterexample with the same form? |
| Inconsistency | Contradictory requirements, self-defeating claims | Can all these claims be true together? |
| Presumption | Begging the question, loaded question, false dilemma | Is every assumption acceptable here, and are the options exhaustive? |
| Relevance | Ad hominem, genetic fallacy, appeals to popularity or pity, straw man, red herring | Does this consideration bear on the truth of the conclusion? |
| Insufficient evidence | Hasty generalization, appeal to ignorance, post hoc, base-rate neglect, gambler's fallacy, composition, slippery slope | Is the evidence strong enough, and what else could explain it? |
| Cognitive bias | Anchoring, framing, availability, confirmation bias, overconfidence, planning fallacy, clustering illusion, sunk costs, hindsight | What procedure would stop this tendency from acting? |

A fallacy is a recurring pattern of bad reasoning, and a bias is a tendency of the reasoner that makes such patterns feel right. The formal fallacies are caught by the methods of the logic entries; the informal ones need judgment about context, relevance and the strength of evidence. The biases are best handled by procedures rather than by resolve. Naming a fallacy is the start of a criticism, not its end. A bad argument for a conclusion does not make the conclusion false.
