## 1. Introduction

Priors, probability distributions that encapsulate initial beliefs about model parameters, lie at the heart of Bayesian neural networks (BNNs). The choice of priors is not merely a theoretical consideration but has profound implications for the statistical properties of the resulting models. The theoretical study of priors in BNNs dates back to the foundational work of Neal, who showed that single-hidden layer neural networks with *i.i.d.* Gaussian weights converge to a Gaussian process as the number of neurons of the hidden layer tends to infinity (the so-called infinite-width limit), see Theorem 29.2. After providing an overview of the statistical properties of priors in Bayesian inference and, more specifically, in BNNs, this chapter is organized as follows. Sections 2 and 3 examine single-hidden-layer and deep neural networks, respectively. Sections 4 and 5 consider the role of different priors and architectures. Finally, the impact of the prior on the posterior is discussed in Section 6.

### 1.1 Simple statistical properties of priors

The core principle of the Bayesian framework is to represent uncertainty about model parameters and predictions using probability distributions. Unlike non-Bayesian or frequentist methods, which treat parameters as fixed but unknown quantities, the Bayesian perspective considers parameters as random variables. Bayesian inference refers to the inductive process of updating beliefs in light of observed data via Bayes' rule. This choice is not arbitrary: it is grounded in rigorous mathematical foundations, notably from the works of Cox and Savage.

The following notation is adopted: let $\mathcal{D} = (X, Y)$ denote a training data set of size $N$, assumed to be generated by an underlying *sampling model* or *likelihood* $p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$. The parameters $\theta$ lie in a parameter space $\Theta$, typically a subset of the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^P$. The *prior distribution* $p(\theta)$ encodes beliefs about the parameters $\theta$ before observing any data, and constitutes the central theme of this chapter.

Specifying both the prior $p(\theta)$ and the likelihood $p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$ defines the *joint distribution* of the parameters and data via the product rule: $p(\theta, \mathcal{D}) = p(\theta)p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$. Bayes' rule combines the prior and the likelihood to produce the *posterior distribution* $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})$:

$$
p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{p(\theta)p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)}{p(\mathcal{D})}.
\tag{29.1}
$$

Here, the denominator $p(\mathcal{D})$, known as the model *evidence* or *marginal likelihood*, serves as the normalization constant.

Bayes' rule (29.1) provides a principled mechanism for updating beliefs in light of new data, but it offers no guidance on how those beliefs, encoded in the prior, should be initially specified. In practice, prior choices are often influenced by computational convenience. A notable example is the use of conjugate priors: a prior is said to be conjugate to a likelihood if the resulting posterior belongs to the same parametric family. Classic instances of conjugate prior-model pairs include the Gaussian-Gaussian, Beta-Binomial, and Gamma-Poisson.

The issue of prior choice often leads to the question: how informative is the prior? This introduces a distinction between objective and subjective priors. For example, Jeffreys' prior, defined as proportional to the square root of the determinant of the Fisher information matrix, is considered objective due to its invariance under reparameterization. See Lee and Nalisnick and Smyth for studies on Jeffreys' priors in BNNs. Objective or uninformative priors are frequently improper, meaning they do not integrate to a finite total probability (e.g., a uniform prior over an unbounded space). Surprisingly, such priors are widely used in Bayesian analysis, provided the resulting posterior is proper and thus suitable for inference. However, improper priors cannot be used to compute prior predictive distributions or Bayes factors, as these too would be improper.

In contrast, subjective priors lie at the core of the Bayesian philosophy, encoding expert beliefs or prior knowledge. The process of eliciting such priors is nuanced and challenging; see, for example, Mikkola et al. for a survey.

The challenge of specifying a prior becomes even more pronounced as models grow in complexity, when parameters lack clear interpretation, or when the parameter space is high-dimensional. In such settings, designing a prior that appropriately covers the space becomes non-trivial, and exact computation of the posterior may be infeasible. In these cases, the quality of Bayesian inference relies heavily on both the approximations made for computational tractability and the form of the prior distribution itself.

### 1.2 Statistical properties of priors in BNNs

The Gaussian distribution is a common and default choice of prior in Bayesian neural networks. Looking for a Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) estimate of such a Bayesian model is equivalent to training a standard neural network under weighted $L_2$ regularization. There is currently neither theoretical nor empirical evidence that Gaussian priors are preferable to alternative choices. Yet, its well-studied mathematical properties lead to Gaussian distributions being the default prior choice in BNNs.

Arguably, the prior that matters the most from a practitioner's point of view is the prior induced in function-space, not in parameter space or weight-space, as the induced prior seen at the function level can provide insight into the typical behavior of the output functions. For reviews on priors in Bayesian deep learning models, refer to Arbel et al., Fortuin, Nalisnick, as well as [Chapter 28](/blog/introduction-to-priors).

### 1.3 Width and depth limits of BNNs and their implications

While inference in Bayesian neural networks is computationally limited to finite networks, the statistical properties of Bayesian neural networks vary depending on whether one considers finite networks or their infinite width or infinite depth limits.

In the finite width regime, BNNs maintain a finite number of parameters. Integrating Bayesian inference principles allows them to represent uncertainty about the output function of interest by considering a distribution over the network parameters. This approach is crucial in scenarios where overconfidence can lead to detrimental outcomes, such as in identifying rare diseases from medical images or predicting financial market trends.

In the infinite width limit, many BNNs exhibit a theoretical convergence to Gaussian processes (GPs), in the sense that, as the width of the network approaches infinity, the prior distribution over functions induced by a BNN approaches that of a GP. The infinite width perspective illuminates the behavior of neural networks at scale, offering insights into why wide networks perform well in practice, and aids in the understanding and development of network architectures and priors that can leverage this convergence for improved generalization and robustness.

In the context of infinite depth limits, BNNs are able to capture increasingly complex functions through deeper architectures. As networks grow deeper, they have the potential to model intricate hierarchical structures within the data. In the infinite depth regime, these networks can exhibit recursive feature transformations that resemble deep GPs, see [Chapter 25](/blog/deep-gaussian-processes). This allows for more expressive power and the capacity to model data with rich, layered patterns. Theoretical analysis in this regime offers insights into phenomena like the interplay between depth and nonlinearity, leading to smoother functions and better generalization. However, maintaining computational tractability and avoiding issues such as vanishing gradients become essential challenges. Exploring infinite depth networks provides a deeper understanding of how neural networks can effectively capture and utilize complex dependencies in data.

Furthermore, the relationship between finite BNNs and their infinite limits also simplifies theoretical analyses and provides a framework for studying the impact of architectural choices, such as depth and activation functions, through a probabilistic lens. This dual perspective makes BNN priors a rich field of study, which the following pages investigate.

## 2. Single-hidden-layer networks

Understanding the distribution of units (pre-activations and post-activations in each layer) under the prior can illuminate how information propagates in an untrained network. This is the focus of a series of works by Vladimirova et al. The key finding is that unit distributions get progressively heavier-tailed in deeper layers when weights have light-tailed prior distributions, such as Gaussian priors.

### 2.1 First-hidden-layer outputs

Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^D$ be an input vector and assume neural network weights $\theta$ follow *i.i.d.* Gaussian distributions $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, and assume, for now, that there are no bias parameters. Then, the pre-activation of the $i$-th hidden unit in the first layer is a sum of weighted inputs:

$$
g_i^{(1)} = \sum_{j=1}^{D} \theta_{i,j}^{(1)} x_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 \|x\|^2),
\tag{29.2}
$$

due to the linearity of expectation and the independence of weights. This result illustrates a key insight: under Gaussian priors, the input to the nonlinearity in the first layer is itself Gaussian.

However, once a nonlinearity $\phi$ (e.g., ReLU) is applied to obtain the activation $h_i^{(1)} = \phi(g_i^{(1)})$, the output distribution becomes non-Gaussian. For example, with ReLU, $h_i^{(1)}$ is a half-Gaussian (i.e., a Gaussian clipped below zero). The resulting distribution is sub-Gaussian, meaning that its tails decay at least as fast as Gaussian:

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(|h_i^{(1)}| > t\right) \leq C \exp(-t^2/K), \quad \text{for any } t \geq 0,
\tag{29.3}
$$

for some constants $C, K > 0$. This sub-Gaussianity implies that, at the first layer, the prior retains relatively light tails, an important property that shapes the induced prior on functions.

Earlier work provides closed-form expressions for the first two moments of post-activation units in single-hidden-layer networks with Gaussian weights. These moment characterizations form the basis for bounding tail behavior and variance growth. However, the restriction to a single hidden layer limits their analysis and does not capture the cumulative nonlinear transformations seen in deeper architectures.

### 2.2 Combining the outputs of a single-hidden-layer neural network

Since the outputs of the first hidden layer are nonlinear transformations of Gaussians, they are no longer Gaussian. When these are multiplied by a new set of independent Gaussian weights in the second layer (see Figure 29.1), the resulting pre-activations become products of sub-Gaussian and Gaussian variables, i.e. $g_i^{(2)} = \sum_{j=1}^{H_1} \theta_{i,j}^{(2)} h_j^{(1)}$. Vladimirova et al. shows that such products are sub-exponential with tail bounds of the form:

$$
\mathbb{P}(|g_i^{(2)}| > t) \leq C \exp(-t/K), \quad \text{for any } t \geq 0,
\tag{29.4}
$$

for some constants $C, K > 0$. This heavier tail behavior marks the transition from sub-Gaussian to sub-exponential distributions and is one of the earliest indicators that deep networks encode increasingly heavy-tailed beliefs under standard Gaussian weight priors.

The previous results provide marginal properties of the prior distribution of hidden units, but their joint distribution is more difficult to characterize as some dependence arises from the second hidden layer, although the units across a layer are uncorrelated. Theorem 29.1 formalizes this property, showing that, under the prior distribution, any two units of any layer are uncorrelated if the weight priors are independent and centered at 0:

**Theorem 29.1** (Hidden units dependence, Vladimirova et al.). *If the prior* $p$ *on the neural network weights* $\theta$ *is centered, in the sense that* $\mathbb{E}_p(\theta) = 0$, *and independent (isotropic), then any two units of any layer are uncorrelated a priori. Also, their Kendall's tau and Spearman's rho vanish.*

The proof is left to the reader as an exercise.

For finite-width networks, these dependent and non-Gaussian distributions complicate analytic treatment. Marginal properties alone are insufficient for accurate description, as the function-space prior cannot be characterized by simple moments. Approximate representations, such as the use of sub-Weibull, generalized Weibull or Meijer $G$-function-based priors (see Section 3), aim to capture these richer properties in analytically tractable forms.

### 2.3 Gaussian process limit for shallow neural networks

As shown by Neal, when the number of hidden units $H_1$ tends to infinity, the sum of independent (or weakly dependent) sub-exponential variables with properly scaled variance converges to a Gaussian via the Central Limit Theorem. Figure 29.2 illustrates the width limit for both the single-hidden-layer case (top row) and its generalization to deeper networks, Theorem 29.4, due to Matthews et al. The induced prior on the function space then becomes a Gaussian process, with a covariance kernel determined by the activation function and weight variance.

**Theorem 29.2** (Neal). *Consider a Bayesian neural network with one hidden layer with* $H_1$ *nodes, and let each weight follow a* $\mathcal{N}(0, 1/H_1)$ *distribution. If the post-activations have bounded variance, then the BNN output converges to a zero-mean Gaussian process as* $H_1$ *tends to* $\infty$.

The proof is left to the reader as an exercise.

## 3. Deep neural networks

As Section 2 showed, some dependence between hidden units arises from the second hidden layer, although the units across a layer are uncorrelated. Thus, as also shown in the previous section for two hidden layers, the marginal distribution of $l$-th layer hidden units is difficult to characterize. Vladimirova et al. described unit priors through sub-Weibull and Weibull-tail distributions on the units using the dependence characterization between the hidden units. Noci et al., Zavatone-Veth et al. provide a precise description of the hidden units prior distribution of both linear and ReLU BNNs. One implication of these works is that the preactivation prior is radial. More specifically, if $\boldsymbol{g}$ denotes the preactivations of some layer, then the prior $p(\boldsymbol{g})$ can be written as $p(\|\boldsymbol{g}\|)$. This property makes it possible to obtain exact expressions for hidden units priors in terms of so-called Meijer $G$-functions by exploiting the relationship between the Fourier transforms of radial functions and the Hankel transform.

### 3.1 Sub-Weibull characterization

The hidden units distributions can be characterized as sub-Weibull distributions, a generalization of sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential characterization. To describe this result, start with the formal definition of a sub-Weibull random variable:

**Definition 29.1** (Sub-Weibull random variable, Vladimirova et al.). *A random variable* $X$ *satisfying for all* $t > 0$ *and for some* $C, \eta > 0$

$$
\mathbb{P}(|X| \geq t) \leq C \exp\left(-t^{1/\eta}\right),
\tag{29.5}
$$

*is called a sub-Weibull random variable with tail parameter* $\eta$, *which is denoted by* $X \sim \mathrm{subW}(\eta)$.

Sub-Weibull distributions are characterized by tails lighter than (or equally light as) Weibull distributions; in the same way as sub-Gaussian or sub-exponential distributions correspond to distributions with tails lighter than Gaussian and exponential distributions, respectively. Sub-Weibull distributions are parametrized by a positive tail index $\eta$ and are equivalent to sub-Gaussian for $\eta = 1/2$ and sub-exponential for $\eta = 1$.

Under a mild assumption on the activation function, namely that it scales roughly linearly as $x$ shoots off to infinity, a notion made precise in Definition 29.2 in Section 5.1, the following theorem holds.

**Theorem 29.3** (Theorem 3.1, Vladimirova et al.). *Consider a Bayesian neural network with* $L - 1$ *hidden layers, centered Gaussian priors and with activation function satisfying the extended envelope property of Definition 29.2. Then conditional on the input* $x$, *the marginal prior distribution on any unit (pre- or post-activation) of the* $l$*-th hidden layer is sub-Weibull with tail parameter* $\eta = l/2$. *That is for any* $1 \leq l \leq L$, *and for any* $1 \leq m \leq H_l$,

$$
u_m^{(l)} \sim \mathrm{subW}(l/2),
$$

*where* $u_m^{(l)}$ *is either a pre-activation* $g_m^{(l)}$ *or a post-activation* $h_m^{(l)}$.

Thus, under certain conditions on the activation function (essentially, that it is ReLU-like and unbounded above), a Gaussian prior on weights induces a $\text{sub-Weibull}(l/2)$ distribution on the $l$-th layer units, where the tail parameter $l/2$ increases with $l$ (larger tail parameter means heavier tail). For example, the first layer is Gaussian, the second layer is sub-exponential. The intuition behind this is as follows: multiplying many random matrices (even if each has nice properties) tends to produce outputs dominated by a few large random terms (due to ReLU passing through large positive sums), hence yielding fatter tails. A practical implication of this is that prior predictive distributions of a deep BNN can have high kurtosis: most of the time outputs look moderate, but occasionally they might be very large in magnitude due to a rare combination of large weights and reinforcing nonlinearities. This is a form of prior belief: namely, a deep random network prior believes that extreme activations, while rare, are more likely than a Gaussian belief would suggest. It is a departure from the infinite-width GP, which would predict output distributions to be Gaussian (no excess kurtosis).

### 3.2 Meijer G-functions characterization

This section introduces a precise characterization of hidden units priors proposed by Noci et al., Zavatone-Veth and Pehlevan via so-called Meijer $G$-functions. These are very general special functions that encompass many classical functions (including hypergeometric and Bessel functions) as special cases. They are particularly useful because products of independent random variables often admit closed-form densities expressed through Meijer $G$-functions Springer and Thompson. While these special functions are not commonly encountered in machine learning, they provide a compact way of expressing otherwise intractable densities and enable the derivation of analytical properties such as moments, tail behavior, and asymptotic expansions.

With this property, Zavatone-Veth and Pehlevan and Noci et al. independently derived the density of the joint prior of the $L$-th layer $\mathbf{h}_L$ of a linear Bayesian neural network with Gaussian priors, with widths $H_1, \dots, H_L$, as

$$
p_L^{\mathrm{lin}}(\mathbf{h}_L \mid x) = \frac{\gamma_L}{(2^L \pi \kappa_L^2)^{H_L/2}} \, G_{0,L}^{L,0}\left( \frac{\|\mathbf{h}_L\|^2}{2^L \kappa_L^2} \,\middle|\, \begin{matrix} - \\ 0, (H_1 - H_L)/2, \dots, (H_{L-1} - H_L)/2 \end{matrix} \right),
\tag{29.6}
$$

where

$$
\kappa_L \equiv \sigma_1 \cdots \sigma_L \|x\| \quad \text{and} \quad \gamma_L \equiv \prod_{l=1}^{L-1} \frac{1}{\Gamma(H_l/2)}.
\tag{29.7}
$$

Here, $z \mapsto G_{0,L}^{L,0}\left( z \,\middle|\, \begin{smallmatrix} - \\ 0, (H_1 - H_L)/2, \dots, (H_{L-1} - H_L)/2 \end{smallmatrix} \right)$ denotes a Meijer-$G$ function with parameters specified by Zavatone-Veth and Pehlevan. Similarly, the authors show that the density of a ReLU Bayesian neural network has the following mixture form

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_L^{\mathrm{ReLU}}(\mathbf{h}_L \mid x) = {} & \left(1 - \frac{(2^{H_1} - 1)(2^{H_2} - 1) \cdots (2^{H_{L-1}} - 1)}{2^{H_1 + \cdots + H_{L-1}}}\right) \delta(\mathbf{h}_L) \\
& + \frac{1}{2^{H_1 + \cdots + H_{L-1}}} \sum_{k_1 = 1}^{H_1} \cdots \sum_{k_{L-1} = 1}^{H_{L-1}} \binom{H_1}{k_1} \cdots \binom{H_{L-1}}{k_{L-1}} p_L^{\mathrm{lin}}(\mathbf{h}_L \mid x; k_1, \dots, k_{L-1}, H_L),
\end{aligned}
\tag{29.8}
$$

where $\delta(\mathbf{h}_L)$ is the $H_L$-dimensional Dirac distribution, and where $p_L^{\mathrm{lin}}(\mathbf{h}_L \mid x; k_1, \dots, k_{L-1}, H_L)$ denotes the linear BNN distribution of Equation (29.6) with specified hidden layer widths $(k_1, \dots, k_{L-1}, H_L)$. Using these exact representations, Zavatone-Veth and Pehlevan derive closed-form expressions for moments, establish tail bounds, and characterize the asymptotic behavior of deep-layer priors. These results provide theoretical insight into how depth and width shape the distributions induced by Bayesian neural network priors.

### 3.3 Gaussian process limit for deep neural networks

The foundational result 29.2 has been generalized to more than one layer by de G. Matthews et al., which is recalled here.

**Theorem 29.4** (Theorem 4, de G. Matthews et al.). *Consider a Bayesian neural network with a continuous activation function satisfying the extended envelope property of Definition 29.2. Let each weight of the* $l$*-th layer follow independently a* $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2/H_l)$ *distribution. Then the output of the network converges in distribution to a zero-mean Gaussian process as all layers widths* $H_1, \dots, H_L$ *tend simultaneously to* $\infty$.

This theorem motivates the use of BNNs in settings where the function space prior is assumed to be a Gaussian process. This approach offers computational advantages in settings where a large number of point evaluations make direct manipulation of the GP kernel matrix infeasible, see also [Chapter 26](/blog/deep-kernel-processes-and-machines).

The above theorem is appealing in its generality, but does not give explicit expressions for the mean and covariance kernel of the resulting GP. For specific choices of activation functions and network architectures, it is possible to explicitly obtain the resulting GP mean function and covariance kernel, see e.g. Dutordoir et al., Yang and Salman. Practitioners may be equally interested in the reverse situation, where one aims to construct a BNN prior based on a target GP on the output function space, one such construction is the Ridgelet prior by Matsubara et al., which results in a prior that is proven to converge to the desired GP as the layer width is taken to infinity.

Notably, Theorem 29.4 requires the variance of the prior to be scaled exactly proportional to $\sigma^2/H_l$ with a *fixed* constant $\sigma^2$. One may alternatively place a hyperprior on $\sigma^2$, a setting which has been investigated by Caporali et al. which shows that the resulting posterior converges to a Student-t process, as is made precise in the following theorem.

**Theorem 29.5** (Corollary 3.8, Caporali et al.). *The posterior of a 1-dimensional-output BNN* ($H_L = 1$) *with Gaussian-Inverse-Gamma prior and Gaussian likelihood, evaluated in the input* $x$, *converges in law to a multivariate Student-t variable as all layers widths* $H_1, \dots, H_{L-1}$ *tend simultaneously to* $\infty$.

## 4. Different priors

Several priors were introduced in [Chapter 28](/blog/introduction-to-priors): informative priors, such as the spike-and-slab prior, which embed expert knowledge; uninformative and reference priors, aiming to minimize subjective influence; Gaussian priors for $L_2$ regularization, controlling model complexity via variance; sparsity-inducing priors such as Laplace, scale-mixture, and horseshoe distributions, encouraging simpler models; and model priors, enabling Bayesian model selection and averaging through structured weight inclusion mechanisms. Properties of the spike-and-slab prior and its role on posterior contraction rates are detailed in Section 6.2, following work by Jantre et al., Polson and Ročková. Two classes of priors are presented in more details in the rest of the section: $\alpha$-stable priors and trace-class priors.

### 4.1 α-stable priors

The family of $\alpha$-stable priors were alluded to by Neal as candidates for avoiding the Gaussian process limit associated to wide neural networks. The $\alpha$-stable distribution is characterized by a stability parameter $\alpha \in (0, 2]$, which controls the tail behavior and the existence of moments. These distributions are defined by their characteristic function, as they typically lack closed-form densities (except for special cases like Gaussian, Cauchy, and Lévy). For a symmetric, centered $\alpha$-stable random variable $X$, the characteristic function is:

$$
t \mapsto \mathbb{E}[e^{itX}] = \exp(-\sigma^\alpha |t|^\alpha),
\tag{29.9}
$$

where $\sigma > 0$ is the scale parameter. When $\alpha = 2$, this reduces to a Gaussian distribution. When $\alpha < 2$, the distribution has heavy tails and infinite variance.

Related work extends the classical Gaussian process limit of deep neural networks by replacing Gaussian weights with symmetric stable distributions. The authors show that, under appropriate scaling, the outputs of fully connected DNNs converge in distribution to a stochastic process whose finite-dimensional marginals are multivariate stable distributions. This stable process generalizes the GP limit stated in Theorem 29.2 and Theorem 29.4, preserving heavy-tailed behavior in the infinite-width limit. The parameters of the limiting distribution are obtained recursively across layers, and the convergence is established jointly over inputs and layers. Favaro et al. extends these results by establishing sup-norm convergence rates under both joint and sequential layer-width growth, and formalizing the recursive characterization of the spectral measure that defines the limiting process.

### 4.2 Trace-class priors

While the infinite width limit of a standard BNN may be attractive at first glance, it results in unfortunate side effects noted by Neal in the discussion of his work: *“Infinite networks drawn from these priors do not have hidden units that represent ‘hidden features’ of the input. The ability to find such hidden features is often seen an interesting aspect of neural network learning.”* One alternative to the uniform scaling of the prior variances is given by trace-class BNN priors, which retain hidden units in the infinite width limit, and aid with identifiability as each weight has a distinct variance. The trace-class BNN prior on parameters $\theta$ consisting of weights $w_{i,j}^{(l)}$ and biases $b_i^{(l)}$ is given by the following independent Gaussian distributions

$$
w_{i,j}^{(1)} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \frac{\sigma_{w^{(1)}}^2}{i^\alpha}\right), \quad w_{i,j}^{(l)} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \frac{\sigma_{w^{(l)}}^2}{(ij)^\alpha}\right) \text{ for } l \geq 2, \quad b_i^{(l)} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \frac{\sigma_{b^{(l)}}^2}{i^\alpha}\right),
\tag{29.10}
$$

where $\alpha > 0$, $\sigma_{w^{(l)}}^2 > 0$ and $\sigma_{b^{(l)}}^2 > 0$ are variance-related parameters to be specified.

Following Sell and Singh, define the infinite width limit of the network. Given an infinite sequence of weights and biases for the first layer, distributed according to the prior of Equation (29.10), i.e. $\left\{ \left(b_i^{(1)}, w_{i,1}^{(1)}, \dots, w_{i,D}^{(1)}\right) : i \in \mathbb{N} \right\}$, all the functions of the first layer, $\{g_i^{(1)} : i \in \mathbb{N}\}$, are clearly well defined. Define all the functions of the second layer corresponding to an infinite-width first layer to be the following almost sure limits, assuming they exist:

$$
G_i^{(2)}(x) = \lim_{H_1 \to \infty} b_i^{(2)} + \sum_{j=1}^{H_1} w_{i,j}^{(2)} \phi(g_j^{(1)}(x)).
\tag{29.11}
$$

Assuming the random functions $\{G_i^{(2)} : i \in \mathbb{N}\}$ are well defined, which will be the case if $\alpha > 1/2$ as shown in Theorem 29.6, proceeding iteratively, all the functions $\{G_i^{(l)} : i \in \mathbb{N}\}$ of subsequent layers, $l = 3, \dots, L$, are also well defined. The functions in each layer of a finite width network are denoted with lower case to clearly distinguish them from their infinite width versions. For the output layer, the finite network gives $g^{(L)}(x)$ while the infinite network gives $G^{(L)}(x)$.

A non-centered trace-class prior can be defined but requires the mean parameters to be appropriately summable. The $\sigma$ parameters are overall scaling parameters of the priors variance, while $\alpha$ controls the decay of the weights in the width: large values result in quickly decaying weights in each layer, while small values result in a slower decay. The constraint $\alpha > 1/2$ ensures that the prior is well-defined in the infinite width limit as shown in the theorem below.

**Theorem 29.6** (Sell and Singh). *Assume the activation function* $\phi$ *is 1-Lipschitz and satisfies* $\phi(0) = 0$. *Let* $L \in \mathbb{N}$ *and* $\alpha > 0$. *Fix further* $\sigma_{w^{(l)}}^2 > 0$ *and* $\sigma_{b^{(l)}}^2 > 0$ *for each* $l \in [L]$. *Then, the functions of the layers of the* finite-width *neural network satisfy*

1. *For all* $x \in [0, 1]^D$ *one has* $|g_i^{(l)}(x)| < \infty$ *almost surely, and* $\mathbb{E}\left[g_i^{(l)}(x)\right] = 0$. *Furthermore, there exists* $\sigma_l^2$ *such that* $\mathbb{E}\left[(g_i^{(l)}(x))^2\right] < \sigma_l^2/i^\alpha$ *for all* $x \in [0, 1]^D$. *Here, both expectations are taken with respect to the prior on the parameters* $\theta$ *of the neural network. In particular this property holds for* $g_1^{(L)}(x)$.
2. *There exists* $c_l \geq 0$ *such that for all* $x_1, x_2 \in [0, 1]^D$, $\mathbb{E}\left[\left(g_i^{(l)}(x_1) - g_i^{(l)}(x_2)\right)^2\right] \leq c_l \|x_1 - x_2\|^2/i^\alpha$, *with the expectation again taken with respect to the prior. In particular this gives* $\mathbb{E}\left[\left(g_1^{(L)}(x_1) - g_1^{(L)}(x_2)\right)^2\right] \leq c_L \|x_1 - x_2\|^2$.

*In addition, if* $\alpha > 1/2$, *the functions on every layer of the infinite-width neural network, see Equation (29.11), exist almost surely and satisfy the above Properties 1 and 2, when the functions* $g_i^{(l)}(x)$ *therein are replaced with* $G_i^{(l)}(x)$ *as defined in Equation (29.11).*

The trace-class prior is attractive due to its interpretability of the functions on the last hidden layers, which do not converge to 0 as one considers the infinite-width limit. It further improves model identifiability, as swapping nodes changes the prior probabilities for the resulting network. The natural ordering of the respective weights lends the trace-class prior as an obvious choice for partial or subnetwork inference, as well as a valuable prior for multilevel Monte Carlo, see [Chapter 4](/blog/sequential-monte-carlo-samplers). The hyperparameters can either be chosen by the user, or inferred by placing a hyperprior on each of them.

## 5. Different architectures

### 5.1 Different activation functions

As described in previous sections, the forward propagation of priors on weights induces progressively heavier-tailed distributions on the units. This section examines how these properties interact with different activation functions. To this aim, the activation function $\phi$ is required to span at least half of the real line as follows. An extended version of the activation function assumption from Matthews et al. is introduced:

**Definition 29.2** (Extended envelope property, Vladimirova et al.). *An activation function* $\phi : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ *is said to obey the* extended envelope property *if there exist* $c_1, c_2 \geq 0$, $d_1, d_2 > 0$ *such that the following inequalities hold*

$$
|\phi(u)| \geq c_1 + d_1 |u| \quad \text{for all } u \in \mathbb{R}_+ \text{ or } u \in \mathbb{R}_-,
\tag{29.12}
$$

$$
|\phi(u)| \leq c_2 + d_2 |u| \quad \text{for all } u \in \mathbb{R}.
\tag{29.13}
$$

The interpretation of this property is that $\phi$ must shoot to infinity at least in one direction ($\mathbb{R}_+$ or $\mathbb{R}_-$, at least linearly (29.12), and also at most linearly (29.13). Of course, compactly supported activation functions such as sigmoid and tanh do not satisfy the extended envelope property but the majority of other activation functions do, including ReLU, ELU, PReLU, and SeLU.

### 5.2 Different layers

While fully connected networks have been the focus of much of the preceding analysis, modern architectures frequently incorporate structural innovations such as residual connections, convolutional layers, and continuous-depth models. These modifications significantly affect the prior and its induced function space behavior.

**Residual layers.** Residual connections, as introduced in ResNets, can be viewed as introducing an identity mapping in addition to the learned transformation. Formally, the pre-activation at layer $l$, $g^{(l)}$, can be written as:

$$
g^{(l)} = g^{(l-1)} + r^{(l)}(g^{(l-1)}; \theta^{(l)}),
\tag{29.14}
$$

for some mapping $r^{(l)}$. Under Bayesian treatment, each residual block contributes a random perturbation to the input of the next layer. This modifies the prior by inducing a random walk structure over functions, leading to smoother transitions across layers. The residual architecture results in slower growth of variance with depth compared to standard feed-forward architectures, and may suppress heavy-tailed behavior.

**Convolutional layers.** Convolutional networks apply shared filters across spatial locations. Let $\theta^{(l)}$ be a convolutional kernel and $*$ denote the convolution operator. Then the pre-activation at layer $l$, $g^{(l)}$, is given by:

$$
g^{(l)} = \theta^{(l)} * h^{(l-1)} + b^{(l)},
\tag{29.15}
$$

where $h^{(l-1)}$ denotes the post-activation at layer $l - 1$. Due to weight sharing and local receptive fields, the induced function-space prior has strong spatial correlations and invariances. This structure leads to a significant reduction in the number of free parameters and typically induces smoother and more data-adaptive priors, which can outperform corresponding infinite-width GPs or neural tangent kernels in practical settings.

**Neural ODEs.** Neural ODEs generalize discrete-depth networks by considering a continuous evolution of hidden states. For a hidden state $h(t)$ and parametrized vector field $v(h, t; \theta)$, the dynamics are governed by:

$$
\frac{dh(t)}{dt} = v(h(t), t; \theta), \quad h(0) = x.
\tag{29.16}
$$

The output of the network is then given by solving the initial value problem over some interval $[0, T]$. The induced prior on functions arises through the distribution over $\theta$:

$$
h(T) = x + \int_0^T v(h(t), t; \theta) \, dt.
\tag{29.17}
$$

If $v$ is linear in $h$ and $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, then $h(t)$ behaves similarly to a Gaussian process in time. More generally, the prior is implicit and can be seen as a stochastic process in function space, influenced by the ODE solver and integration method. The continuous-time formulation enables adaptive computation and smoother transitions, but complicates prior predictive analysis, as closed-form density expressions are generally unavailable.

In practice, modern architectures often combine multiple structural motifs, e.g., residual connections embedded within convolutional layers or ODE blocks used in sequence models. This results in complex, hierarchical priors that defy simple analytic characterization. These hybrid designs further emphasize the role of architectural choices in shaping unit priors.

## 6. Influence of the prior on the posterior

### 6.1 Bayesian asymptotics

The field of Bayesian asymptotics focuses on the behavior of the posterior distribution $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}^n)$ as the sample size $n$ of the data $\mathcal{D} = \mathcal{D}^n$ tends to infinity. In settings where a true data-generating process characterized by a true parameter $\theta_0$ is assumed to exist, the field of Bayesian asymptotics aims to establish the following guarantees: *posterior consistency* at $\theta_0$ refers to $p(U^c \mid \mathcal{D}^n) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ in $P_{\theta_0}$-probability for all neighborhoods $U$ of $\theta_0$, i.e. $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}^n)$ concentrates on the true $\theta_0$; *contraction rates* describe how quickly $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}^n)$ concentrates around $\theta_0$, i.e. how quickly neighborhoods $U$ can shrink with $n$; *Bernstein–von Mises (BvM) theorems* are types of Central Limit theorems which state that under regularity conditions, $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}^n)$ converges to a Gaussian centered at the maximum likelihood estimator $\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{MLE}}$, with covariance given by the inverse Fisher information.

Bayesian neural networks allow one to place prior distributions over network weights and perform inference through the resulting posterior distribution. Understanding the impact of the prior on the asymptotic behavior of the posterior is critical to establishing theoretical guarantees and bridging the gap between Bayesian learning and its frequentist counterpart. This section provides an overview of the asymptotic theory of BNNs, focusing on three key aspects: contraction rates, adaptation, and Bernstein–von Mises theorems. Many of the results below rely on regularity assumptions. Their counterparts for singular models, which frequently arise in Bayesian Deep Learning, are discussed in Chapter 52 on Singular Learning Theory.

### 6.2 Posterior contraction rates

Before stating the main result of posterior concentration for sparse ReLU networks with spike-and-slab prior, the required notation is introduced. Let $f_0$ denote the unknown true regression function defined on $[0, 1]^D$, assumed to belong to a Hölder class of smoothness $\alpha > 0$. Data $(Y_i, x_i)$ are observed, where

$$
Y_i = f_0(x_i) + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), \quad i = 1, \dots, n,
$$

the restriction to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ noise is for simplicity. A Bayesian neural network prior is defined using deep ReLU networks of depth $L$, width $N$, and sparsity $s$, equipped with a spike-and-slab prior on the weights and offsets. The architecture parameters $L$, $N$, and $s$ are chosen depending on the smoothness level $\alpha$, the dimension $D$, and the sample size $n$, to achieve optimal approximation properties.

Denote by $\|\cdot\|_n$ the empirical $L^2$ norm on the training data, and let $\epsilon_n = n^{-\alpha/(2\alpha + D)} \log^\delta(n)$ for some $\delta > 1$ denote the near-minimax rate for estimating an $\alpha$-smooth function in dimension $D$.

**Theorem 29.7** (Posterior concentration, simplified from Theorem 6.1 of Polson and Ročková). *Assume that* $f_0$ *is* $\alpha$*-Hölder smooth with* $\alpha < D$ *and uniformly bounded. Then, for a suitably chosen network architecture depending on* $\alpha$, $D$, *and* $n$, *the posterior distribution concentrates around* $f_0$ *at the rate* $\epsilon_n$, *i.e.,*

$$
\Pi\left(f : \|f - f_0\|_n > M_n \epsilon_n \mid Y^{(n)}\right) \to 0
$$

*in probability as* $n \to \infty$, *for any sequence* $M_n \to \infty$.

Significant progress has been made in establishing posterior contraction rates for BNNs in nonparametric settings. Fully connected deep neural networks have been shown to achieve (near) optimal rates in tasks such as regression and multiclass classification. These results suggest that neural networks possess a remarkable capacity for automatic adaptation to unknown function smoothness.

While classical spike-and-slab priors, that are mixtures of a point mass and a diffuse slab, have been extensively used to achieve optimal rates, their computational complexity makes them less practical in deep learning contexts. Recent developments have focused on alternative priors that retain theoretical optimality while improving computational feasibility.

Contraction rates for variational approximations to BNN posteriors are explored by Bhattacharya and Maiti, Bhattacharya et al., offering insights into the theoretical performance of scalable inference methods. In parallel, related results are derived for deep Gaussian process priors, with recent advances by Bachoc and Lagnoux, Castillo and Randrianarisoa, Finocchio and Schmidt-Hieber who characterize their convergence behavior.

The high-dimensional nature of modern applications raises the question of how posterior rates scale with both sample size $n$ and input dimension $D$. While most existing results assume a fixed $D$, Bhattacharya et al. is a notable exception, deriving minimax-optimal rates that depend jointly on $(n, D)$, and thus addressing more realistic big data scenarios.

### 6.3 Adaptation

Beyond contraction rates, recent work has shown that overparameterized networks with heavy-tailed priors can achieve automatic statistical adaptation, with posterior distributions concentrating at minimax-optimal rates without prior knowledge of function smoothness. This connects with earlier findings that Gaussian priors over network weights can induce increasingly heavy-tailed behavior through the layers of a deep network.

### 6.4 Bernstein–von Mises theorems

Bernstein–von Mises (BvM) theorems describe the asymptotic normality of posterior distributions, providing a Bayesian analog to the classical central limit theorem. In the context of BNNs, exact BvM results are difficult to obtain owing to non-identifiability, symmetries and overparameterization of the models.

For shallow neural networks, with moderate depth and width and well-behaved priors, classical BvM behavior can be recovered. However, as networks become deeper and more expressive, posterior distributions deviate significantly from Gaussian behavior, and standard BvM theorems fail to hold.

Current research aims to extend BvM-type results to functionals of the posterior, such as predictions or feature-specific summaries, rather than the entire parameter space. This more realistic approach aligns with the practical use of BNNs, where uncertainty over predictions is often of primary interest.

## 7. Discussion and open questions

Finite-width networks induce non-Gaussian priors over the output functions, but retain certain "Bayesian" properties. For instance, by de Finetti's theorem, one can view the finite network's function distribution as an exchangeable random process indexed by inputs, but exact distributional forms are intractable beyond very small networks or only under highly restrictive assumptions. The non-Gaussianity means finite networks can, in principle, capture more complex prior beliefs (e.g. multimodal function distributions), but it also complicates analysis. In contrast, in the infinite-width limit, many BNN priors yield a Gaussian process over the output functions, simplifying theoretical analyses and opening exciting connections to the study of GPs.

In some cases, finite-width neural networks outperform their infinite-width counterparts. Arora et al. show that convolutional neural networks outperform their corresponding limiting neural tangent kernel (NTK, see [Chapter 8, Section 2](/blog/linearized-laplace-approximations#2-the-prior), and Jacot et al.). This performance gap is likely due to the finite width effect where a fixed kernel cannot fully describe the convolutional neural network dynamics. The evolution of the NTK along with training has its benefits on generalization as shown in further works. However, since the performance of finite and infinite-width neural networks is different, it is still not clear when the GP framework is more amenable to describe the BNN behavior.
