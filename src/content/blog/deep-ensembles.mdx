The [first entry](/blog/bayesian-inference-for-neural-networks) defined the posterior predictive distribution, an average of the network's predictions over the posterior $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})$. The next three entries approximated that average in different ways: [Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)](/blog/sampling-the-posterior-mcmc) draws samples from the posterior, the [Laplace approximation](/blog/laplace-approximations) fits a Gaussian at one mode, and [variational inference (VI)](/blog/variational-inference-for-neural-networks) optimizes a member of a tractable family. Each of the last two describes the neighborhood of a single mode.

This entry treats a method that starts from the opposite end. A **deep ensemble** trains the same network several times, from different random initializations, and averages the predictive distributions of the trained copies. It was introduced as a practical, non-Bayesian alternative to approximate inference, and it often gives better-calibrated predictions than the methods of the previous entries at a comparable cost. The entry explains the procedure, shows it working and failing on a toy problem, examines in what sense it approximates the posterior predictive, and describes how ensembles can be turned into samplers, combined with local posterior approximations and weighted with frequentist guarantees.

The examples use Python 3.13 and NumPy 2.5.3, with the seeds given in the code. The notation is the series notation: $\theta$ for parameters, $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_n, y_n)\}_{n=1}^N$ for the data, $x^*$ and $y^*$ for a test input and its output. The training of a single network relies on the [gradient descent](/blog/gradient-descent-and-its-variants) and [regularization](/blog/generalization-overfitting-and-regularization) material of the fundamentals series.

## 1. The deep-ensemble procedure

### 1.1 Train several times, average the predictions

A deep ensemble with $J$ **members** is built in two steps.

1. For $j = 1, \dots, J$, draw an independent random initialization $\theta_j^{(0)}$ and train the network by stochastic gradient descent (SGD), or another optimizer, to a solution $\hat\theta_j$. Each run also uses its own random order of minibatches.
2. Predict with the equally weighted mixture of the members' predictive distributions:

$$
p_{\text{ens}}(y^* \mid x^*) = \frac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^{J} p(y^* \mid x^*, \hat\theta_j).
$$

If the training objective is the negative log-likelihood plus a weight penalty, it equals the negative log-posterior up to a constant and a positive factor, so each $\hat\theta_j$ is a local **maximum a posteriori (MAP)** estimate. Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell (2017), who named and popularized the method, trained each member with a proper scoring rule (the negative log-likelihood of a predicted mean and variance, for regression) and optionally added adversarially perturbed inputs; they used $J = 5$ in most experiments. Averaging independently trained networks is much older: Hansen and Salamon (1990) studied it for accuracy, and Breiman's bagging (1996) trains each member on a bootstrap resample of the data. Lakshminarayanan and colleagues reported that random initialization and shuffling alone gave enough diversity, without resampling the data.

The training runs are independent, so they parallelize perfectly across devices. That shortens the wall-clock time but not the total cost: training, storing and evaluating $J$ networks costs about $J$ times as much as one.

### 1.2 Regression: the mixture's mean and variance

In regression with a Gaussian likelihood, member $j$ predicts a mean $\mu_j(x^*)$ and a variance $\sigma_j^2(x^*)$, either learned or fixed. The ensemble predictive is a mixture of $J$ Gaussians, which is not Gaussian in general, but its first two moments follow from the law of total variance:

$$
\bar\mu(x^*) = \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^J \mu_j(x^*), \qquad
v(x^*) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^J \sigma_j^2(x^*)}_{\text{average noise}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^J \big(\mu_j(x^*) - \bar\mu(x^*)\big)^2}_{\text{disagreement}}.
$$

The first term is the **aleatoric** part of the variance, the noise each member believes is in the data. The second is the spread of the members' means, an estimate of the **epistemic** part, the uncertainty about the function itself that more data would remove. The [first entry](/blog/bayesian-inference-for-neural-networks) introduced both terms; the ensemble supplies a cheap estimate of each.

### 1.3 Classification: average probabilities

In classification each member outputs a vector of class probabilities $p_j = p(y^* \mid x^*, \hat\theta_j)$, and the ensemble averages these vectors, $\bar p = \frac{1}{J}\sum_j p_j$. Averaging the members' logits (the inputs to the softmax) gives a different and generally more confident predictor, which is not the mixture above.

The entropy $H[p] = -\sum_c p_c \ln p_c$ of the averaged prediction splits into two non-negative parts:

$$
H[\bar p] = \underbrace{\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^J H[p_j]}_{\text{expected entropy}} + \underbrace{\Big(H[\bar p] - \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^J H[p_j]\Big)}_{\text{mutual information}} .
$$

The second part is the mutual information between the prediction and the choice of member. It is zero when all members agree and grows with their disagreement, so it plays the role of the disagreement term in regression (Depeweg et al., 2018, use this decomposition to separate the two kinds of uncertainty). A two-member example shows the difference. If one member predicts $(0.9, 0.1)$ for a binary problem and the other $(0.1, 0.9)$, the average is $(0.5, 0.5)$ with entropy $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$ nats. Each member alone has entropy $-0.9\ln 0.9 - 0.1\ln 0.1 \approx 0.325$, so the mutual information is $0.693 - 0.325 = 0.368$. A single member predicting $(0.5, 0.5)$ has the same total entropy with zero mutual information: the same total uncertainty, but of the aleatoric kind.

## 2. A toy ensemble

### 2.1 Setup

The data are 40 noisy samples of $\sin x$, drawn in two clusters, $x \in [-3, -1]$ and $x \in [1, 3]$, with noise standard deviation $\sigma = 0.1$. Each member is a network with one hidden layer of 50 $\tanh$ units (151 parameters), trained by full-batch gradient descent on

$$
\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(f_\theta(x_n) - y_n\big)^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\,\lVert\theta\rVert^2 , \qquad \lambda = 10^{-4},
$$

where $f_\theta$ is the network output. Multiplying the negative log-posterior for Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$ and a prior $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s^2 I)$ by $\sigma^2/N$ gives this objective with $\lambda = \sigma^2/(N s^2)$, so $\lambda = 10^{-4}$ corresponds to a prior standard deviation $s = \sqrt{0.01/(40 \times 10^{-4})} \approx 1.58$. The members predict only a mean, and the noise variance is fixed at its true value, so the average-noise term of Section 1.2 is $\sigma^2 = 0.01$ everywhere.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N, sigma = 40, 0.1
x = np.concatenate([rng.uniform(-3, -1, N // 2), rng.uniform(1, 3, N // 2)])
y = np.sin(x) + sigma * rng.normal(size=N)
X = x[:, None]

def init(seed, H=50):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    return [r.normal(0, 1, (1, H)), r.normal(0, 1, H),
            r.normal(0, 1 / np.sqrt(H), (H, 1)), np.zeros(1)]

def forward(p, X):
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = p
    h = np.tanh(X @ W1 + b1)
    return h, (h @ W2 + b2).ravel()

def train(seed, steps=20_000, lr=0.1, lam=1e-4):
    p = init(seed)
    for _ in range(steps):
        h, f = forward(p, X)
        r = (f - y)[:, None] / N                   # d/df of (1/2N) sum (f - y)^2
        dh = (r @ p[2].T) * (1 - h ** 2)
        g = [X.T @ dh, dh.sum(0), h.T @ r, r.sum(0)]
        p = [w - lr * (gw + lam * w) for w, gw in zip(p, g)]  # weight decay = Gaussian prior
    return p

J = 5
members = [train(seed) for seed in range(1, J + 1)]
xs = np.array([-6.0, -4.0, -2.0, 0.0, 2.0, 4.0, 6.0])
F = np.array([forward(p, xs[:, None])[1] for p in members])   # J x len(xs)
mean, spread = F.mean(0), F.std(0)
total_sd = np.sqrt(sigma ** 2 + spread ** 2)
print("train MSE per member:", np.round([np.mean((forward(p, X)[1] - y) ** 2) for p in members], 4))
print("   x   sin(x)   mean   member sd   predictive sd")
for i, x0 in enumerate(xs):
    print(f"{x0:5.1f} {np.sin(x0):7.3f} {mean[i]:7.3f} {spread[i]:9.3f} {total_sd[i]:12.3f}")
```

It runs in about five seconds and prints:

### 2.2 Reading the output

Every member reaches a training mean squared error (MSE) close to the noise variance of 0.01, so none of them overfits badly. At the training inputs $x = \pm 2$ the members agree to within 0.002, and the predictive standard deviation is the noise level 0.100. In the gap at $x = 0$ the member spread rises to 0.087, more than 40 times its value in the data, and at $x = \pm 6$ to about 0.15–0.17. This is the behavior that makes ensembles useful: disagreement appears where the data do not pin the function down.

The output also shows the limit of that signal. At $x = 4$ the ensemble mean is $-0.305$ while the true value is $-0.757$, an error of 0.452, almost four predictive standard deviations. At $x = 6$ the error is $0.649$, about 3.2 standard deviations. Five networks with the same architecture, trained on the same data, share their inductive bias: all of them extrapolate with saturating $\tanh$ units, so they disagree less than the true uncertainty warrants. ## 3. In what sense is an ensemble Bayesian?

### 3.1 Not a Monte Carlo estimate

The posterior predictive is the integral $p(y^* \mid x^*, \mathcal{D}) = \int p(y^* \mid x^*, \theta)\, p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})\, d\theta$, also called the **Bayesian model average (BMA)**. The ensemble formula has the shape of its Monte Carlo estimate, but a Monte Carlo estimate needs the $\hat\theta_j$ to be draws from $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})$, and they are not. They differ from posterior samples in three ways.

- **Location.** Each member sits at a mode, where the density is highest, not in the typical set, where most of the posterior mass lies in high dimensions.
- **Weights.** Every member gets weight $1/J$, whatever the posterior mass of its basin. The fraction of runs that end in a basin depends on the optimizer and the initialization distribution, not on the posterior.
- **Within-basin spread.** A member is a single point, so the ensemble ignores the uncertainty within each basin, which is all that a Laplace or variational approximation represents.

So a deep ensemble is an equally weighted mixture of point predictions that may approximate the BMA, not a sampler for it.

### 3.2 Why it can still be a good approximation

Two observations explain why ensembles nevertheless often predict as well as, or better than, single-mode approximations. The first is that neural network posteriors have many modes that give different functions. Fort, Hu and Lakshminarayanan (2019) found that networks trained from different initializations make substantially different predictions, while points sampled around one solution (for example along an SGD trajectory) differ much less from each other.

The second is a question of budget. With room for $J$ forward passes per test input, the integral is approximated best by the evaluations that capture the most predictive variation. If predictions vary less within a basin than between basins, $J$ points in $J$ basins cover more of that variation than $J$ points in one basin. Wilson and Izmailov (2020) made this argument and proposed it as the reason ensembles approximate the BMA well. Izmailov et al. (2021) tested it by running full-batch Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) on image classification networks as a reference. In their experiments a deep ensemble agreed with the HMC predictive distribution more closely than mean-field VI did, and about as closely as several stochastic-gradient MCMC methods.

These are empirical findings for particular architectures, data sets and budgets. They support the reading that ensembles are a useful finite-budget approximation to the BMA. They do not show that ensembles beat a well-mixed sampler run for long enough, and they say nothing about networks or data unlike those tested.

### 3.3 Model averaging and model combination

A further distinction explains part of the ensembles' accuracy. The BMA weights hypotheses by their posterior probability; as the data grow, the posterior concentrates and the BMA approaches a single hypothesis. An ensemble does something different: its mixture is a new predictive distribution that may not be representable by any single member, for example a bimodal predictive in regression. Minka (2000) called this **model combination**, to contrast it with model averaging, and pointed out that the two answer different questions. Part of an ensemble's advantage may therefore come from a richer predictive family rather than from better marginalization. The same caution applies to Monte Carlo dropout (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016), whose dropout masks define an approximating distribution, not posterior samples.

## 4. Turning an ensemble into a sampler

### 4.1 Randomize, then optimize

A convex problem shows why plain ensembles are not samplers: with a quadratic loss, every initialization converges to the same minimum, and an ensemble of $J$ identical members has zero spread. In a network, diversity comes only from the non-convexity of the loss. A small change to each member's objective repairs this, exactly in the linear-Gaussian case.

Take a linear model $f_\theta(x) = \phi(x)^\top \theta$ with $D$ features, feature matrix $\Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$, Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$ and prior $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s^2 I)$. The [first entry](/blog/bayesian-inference-for-neural-networks) derived its posterior, $\mathcal{N}(m, S)$ with

$$
S = \Big(\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\Phi^\top\Phi + \tfrac{1}{s^2} I\Big)^{-1}, \qquad m = \tfrac{1}{\sigma^2} S \Phi^\top y .
$$

Now let member $j$ draw an **anchor** $\theta_{0,j} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s^2 I)$ from the prior and a target perturbation $\varepsilon_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_N)$, and minimize

$$
L_j(\theta) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert y + \varepsilon_j - \Phi\theta\rVert^2 + \frac{1}{2s^2}\lVert\theta - \theta_{0,j}\rVert^2 .
$$

Setting the gradient to zero gives $\hat\theta_j = S\big(\Phi^\top(y + \varepsilon_j)/\sigma^2 + \theta_{0,j}/s^2\big)$. This is a linear function of independent Gaussian vectors, so it is Gaussian; its mean is $S\Phi^\top y/\sigma^2 = m$, because $\varepsilon_j$ and $\theta_{0,j}$ have mean zero. Its covariance is

$$
S\Big(\frac{\Phi^\top (\sigma^2 I)\Phi}{\sigma^4} + \frac{s^2 I}{s^4}\Big)S = S\,S^{-1}S = S ,
$$

so each member is an exact posterior sample. Bardsley et al. (2014) studied this **randomize-then-optimize** idea for inverse problems. For neural networks it becomes an approximation: Pearce et al. (2020) proposed anchored ensembles that regularize each member toward its own prior draw, and Osband, Aslanides and Cassirer (2018) add a fixed, randomly initialized "prior network" to each member's output, which has a similar effect.

### 4.2 A numerical check

The listing below fits a quadratic in $x$ (features $1, x, x^2$) to 12 noisy points and compares the exact posterior with 20 000 randomize-then-optimize members. Since each $L_j$ is quadratic, the members are found in closed form; for a network each would be trained by gradient descent.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
N, sigma, s = 12, 0.3, 1.0                       # data size, noise sd, prior sd
x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, N)
Phi = np.stack([np.ones(N), x, x ** 2], axis=1)   # features 1, x, x^2
y = 0.5 - x + 0.8 * x ** 2 + sigma * rng.normal(size=N)

# Exact Gaussian posterior (prior theta ~ N(0, s^2 I))
A = Phi.T @ Phi / sigma ** 2 + np.eye(3) / s ** 2
S_post = np.linalg.inv(A)
m_post = S_post @ Phi.T @ y / sigma ** 2

# Randomize-then-optimize: member j fits perturbed targets, anchored at a prior draw
J = 20_000
theta0 = rng.normal(0, s, (J, 3))                # anchors drawn from the prior
eps = rng.normal(0, sigma, (J, N))               # perturbations of the targets
members = (S_post @ (Phi.T @ (y + eps).T / sigma ** 2 + theta0.T / s ** 2)).T

print("posterior mean     ", np.round(m_post, 3))
print("ensemble mean      ", np.round(members.mean(0), 3))
print("posterior sd       ", np.round(np.sqrt(np.diag(S_post)), 3))
print("ensemble sd        ", np.round(members.std(0), 3))
```

It prints:

```text
posterior mean      [ 0.502 -1.203  0.633]
ensemble mean       [ 0.502 -1.202  0.636]
posterior sd        [0.124 0.223 0.396]
ensemble sd         [0.124 0.223 0.396]
```

The means agree to Monte Carlo error (about $0.4/\sqrt{20\,000} \approx 0.003$ for the third coefficient) and the standard deviations to three decimals. Without the anchors and perturbations, every member would be the MAP estimate $m$ and the ensemble would report no uncertainty at all.

## 5. Ensembles combined with local approximations

Sections 3 and 4 suggest a division of labor: an ensemble covers several basins, and a local method describes the spread inside each one. Several methods combine the two.

- **MultiSWAG.** The stochastic weight averaging Gaussian (SWAG) of Maddox et al. (2019) fits a Gaussian with a diagonal plus low-rank covariance to the iterates of SGD run with a constant or cyclical learning rate after convergence. Wilson and Izmailov (2020) fit one SWAG Gaussian in each of several independently trained basins and sample from the resulting mixture.
- **Mixtures of Laplace or variational approximations.** A [Laplace approximation](/blog/laplace-approximations) or a Gaussian [variational posterior](/blog/variational-inference-for-neural-networks) can be fitted around each member, giving an equally weighted mixture of Gaussians.
- **Optimization-initialized sampling.** An [MCMC](/blog/sampling-the-posterior-mcmc) chain started at a random point spends a long time in its warm-up phase, and a single chain rarely crosses the low-density regions between modes. Starting one chain at each ensemble member uses optimization to find the modes quickly and sampling to explore each of them, and the chains run in parallel as the members did.

All three keep the equal weighting of basins, so they inherit the first two limitations of Section 3.1; they remove only the third.

## 6. Cost and cheaper relatives

The factor-of-$J$ cost has motivated methods that obtain diverse members more cheaply.

- **Snapshot ensembles** (Huang et al., 2017) save the network at the end of each cycle of a cyclical learning-rate schedule, so one training run yields several members.
- **Fast geometric ensembling** (Garipov et al., 2018) collects members along a path of low loss between modes, found with a cyclical learning rate.
- **BatchEnsemble** (Wen, Tran and Ba, 2020) shares one weight matrix among all members and gives each member its own rank-one multiplicative factor, so the extra parameters and memory are small.
- **Monte Carlo dropout** (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016) keeps dropout active at test time and averages several stochastic forward passes of a single network.

These methods trade diversity for cost, and in published comparisons the cheap variants are usually less diverse than independently trained members. A fair comparison also fixes the budget: the same computation spent on one larger network may give higher accuracy than five smaller ones. Accuracy is not the only goal, however. A single deterministic network, however large, provides no measure of disagreement; the uncertainty has to come from an explicit mechanism such as an ensemble, a posterior approximation or a Gaussian-process output layer (the [sixth entry](/blog/gaussian-processes-and-kernels) and the [eighth entry](/blog/scaling-bayesian-deep-learning)).

## 7. Where ensembles are used

Ensembles are the most common uncertainty method in applied deep learning, largely because they need no change to the model or the training code. A few uses illustrate what the disagreement signal is good for.

- **Detecting dataset shift.** In a large benchmark of image and text classifiers under corrupted and shifted inputs, Ovadia et al. (2019) found that deep ensembles gave the best calibration and accuracy under shift among the methods they compared, although every method degraded as the shift grew.
- **Exploration in reinforcement learning.** Bootstrapped deep Q-networks (Osband et al., 2016) keep several value-function heads and act greedily with respect to one head chosen at random per episode, so the agent explores where the heads disagree.
- **Model-based control.** Chua et al. (2018) learned an ensemble of probabilistic dynamics models and planned through it, which separated the noise in the system from the uncertainty of the learned model and reduced the amount of interaction needed on their benchmarks.
- **Weather and climate.** Numerical weather prediction has long issued ensemble forecasts from perturbed initial conditions and model variants (Leutbecher and Palmer, 2008). Learned forecasting models are evaluated in the same ensemble framework, which makes the disagreement-based view of uncertainty a natural fit there.

The [ninth entry](/blog/evaluating-and-using-uncertainty) returns to how such uncertainty is evaluated and used, including in active learning.

## 8. Weighting members: a PAC-Bayesian view

### 8.1 Frequentist bounds with a prior

Equal weights are a default, not a result. **Probably approximately correct (PAC)-Bayesian** analysis, introduced by McAllester (1999), gives a principled way to choose weights with a guarantee that holds without assuming the prior is correct. It studies a randomized predictor that, for each new input, draws parameters from a distribution $\rho$ and predicts with them. With a loss bounded in $[0, 1]$, write $L(\theta)$ for the expected loss on new data and $\hat L(\theta)$ for the average loss on $N$ examples. For any distribution $\pi$ chosen before seeing those $N$ examples, Seeger (2002) and Maurer (2004) showed that with probability at least $1 - \delta$ over the examples, simultaneously for all $\rho$,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[L(\theta)] \le \mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\hat L(\theta)] + \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(\rho \,\Vert\, \pi) + \ln(2\sqrt{N}/\delta)}{2N}} ,
$$

where $\mathrm{KL}$ is the Kullback–Leibler divergence. (The bound in the cited papers is tighter; this form follows from it by Pinsker's inequality.) The names "prior" for $\pi$ and "posterior" for $\rho$ are borrowed: $\pi$ need not express a belief, and $\rho$ may be any distribution, including one chosen by minimizing the right-hand side. The bound is frequentist; the probability is over the random data.

For an ensemble, take $\pi$ and $\rho$ as distributions over the $J$ members and evaluate $\hat L$ on held-out data not used to train them. With $J = 5$, $N = 2000$ held-out examples and $\delta = 0.05$, the logarithmic term is $\ln(2\sqrt{2000}/0.05) \approx 7.49$. For uniform $\rho = \pi$ the divergence is zero and the bound adds $\sqrt{7.49/4000} \approx 0.043$ to the average held-out error. Putting all the weight on one member costs $\mathrm{KL} = \ln 5 \approx 1.61$ and raises the term only to about 0.048, so with a few members the price of choosing weights is small.

### 8.2 The majority vote needs second-order information

The quantity above bounds the error of the randomized predictor, not of the ensemble's vote. For the weighted majority vote $\mathrm{MV}_\rho$ in classification with the zero-one loss, the vote errs only if members carrying at least half the weight err, which gives $L(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) \le 2\,\mathbb{E}_\rho[L(\theta)]$. This first-order bound ignores the main reason votes work: members that err on different examples cancel each other's mistakes.

Masegosa et al. (2020) used the **tandem loss** $L(\theta, \theta')$, the probability that two members both err on the same example, to prove $L(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) \le 4\,\mathbb{E}_{\rho \times \rho}[L(\theta, \theta')]$, and turned it into an empirical PAC-Bayesian bound that can be minimized over $\rho$. Suppose each member errs on 10% of examples and a random pair errs together on 4%. The first-order bound gives $2 \times 0.10 = 0.20$, the tandem bound $4 \times 0.04 = 0.16$. If the members' errors were perfectly correlated, the tandem loss would equal 0.10 and the tandem bound 0.40, worse than the first-order one: the second-order bound pays off exactly when the members are diverse. These oracle bounds still need the complexity term of Section 8.1 before they become guarantees.

## 9. Limits and failure modes

- **Cost.** Training, memory and inference all scale with $J$. For large models this cost is often prohibitive; the [eighth entry](/blog/scaling-bayesian-deep-learning) covers cheaper alternatives.
- **Shared inductive bias.** Members with the same architecture extrapolate alike, as Section 2.2 showed, so disagreement underestimates uncertainty far from the data. For classifiers with ReLU activations, Hein, Andriushchenko and Bitterwolf (2019) proved that a single network becomes arbitrarily confident along almost every ray far from the data; averaging members does not by itself remove this behavior.
- **Low diversity.** When members start from the same pretrained weights and are fine-tuned briefly, they can remain in one basin and agree too much. Diversity has to be checked, for example by measuring disagreement on held-out data, not assumed.
- **Equal weights and no within-basin spread.** Section 3.1 listed what the plain ensemble does not represent; Sections 4, 5 and 8 address each in turn at some extra cost.
- **Calibration is not guaranteed.** Ensembles are often better calibrated than single networks, but under strong shift their calibration degrades too (Ovadia et al., 2019). Calibration has to be measured on data like those the model will meet.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Then $\hat\theta_j = S\Phi^\top(y + \varepsilon_j)/\sigma^2$, with mean $m$ and covariance $S(\Phi^\top\Phi/\sigma^2)S$. Since $S^{-1} = \Phi^\top\Phi/\sigma^2 + I/s^2$, this equals $S(S^{-1} - I/s^2)S = S - S^2/s^2$, which is smaller than $S$ by the positive semi-definite matrix $S^2/s^2$.

</details>

**Exercise 4.** Two classifiers each err on 20% of examples. Give the tandem loss if their errors never coincide and if they always coincide, and the corresponding tandem bounds on the majority vote of the two.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With uniform weights, $\mathbb{E}_{\rho\times\rho}[L(\theta,\theta')]$ averages the four ordered pairs: two pairs of a member with itself (tandem loss 0.20 each) and two mixed pairs. If the errors never coincide the mixed pairs have tandem loss 0, the average is 0.10 and the bound is 0.40. If they always coincide every pair has 0.20 and the bound is 0.80. The first-order bound is 0.40 in both cases, so the tandem bound helps only with larger ensembles of diverse members, where self-pairs carry a smaller share of the weight.

</details>

## 11. Summary

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What is a deep ensemble? | $J$ copies of a network trained from different random initializations, whose predictive distributions are averaged with equal weights. |
| How is its uncertainty split? | Regression: average noise plus the variance of the members' means. Classification: expected entropy plus mutual information. |
| Is it a posterior sampler? | No: members are modes, equally weighted, with no within-basin spread. |
| Why does it approximate the BMA well? | Predictions vary more between basins than within one, so a few modes cover much of the predictive variation; supported by comparisons with HMC references. |
| How can it be made exact? | In the linear-Gaussian case, by perturbing targets and anchoring each member to a prior draw (randomize then optimize). |
| How is it combined with local methods? | MultiSWAG, mixtures of Laplace or variational approximations, and MCMC chains started at members. |
| Can the weights be chosen with a guarantee? | Yes: PAC-Bayesian bounds on held-out data; for majority votes, second-order (tandem) bounds reward diverse members. |
| Main failure modes? | $J$-fold cost, shared inductive bias far from data, low diversity, calibration loss under shift. |

Ensembles describe uncertainty through a handful of trained functions. The [next entry](/blog/gaussian-processes-and-kernels) takes that view to its end: it places the prior directly on functions with a Gaussian process, derives the exact posterior for regression, and shows that a randomly initialized network becomes a Gaussian process as its width grows without bound.
