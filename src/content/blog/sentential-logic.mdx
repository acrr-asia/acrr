The [fourth entry](/blog/what-logic-studies) argued that validity is a matter of form: an argument is valid when no argument of the same form has true premises and a false conclusion. The [fifth entry](/blog/categorical-logic-and-venn-diagrams) made that idea precise for one family of arguments, the categorical syllogisms, and gave a mechanical test for them in the Venn diagram. This entry does the same for a different family: arguments whose validity depends on how whole sentences are combined with words such as "not", "and", "or", "if ... then" and "if and only if".

The tool is **sentential logic** (also called propositional logic): a small formal language whose basic units are sentences that are true or false, together with rules for combining them. The entry first defines the language and the meaning of its connectives, then uses truth tables to classify formulas and test arguments, including a short method that avoids writing out whole tables. It then translates English into the language, examines the one connective that departs most from English usage, and builds a natural-deduction proof system. It ends with what the system can and cannot do: it is sound and complete, and it is blind to the internal structure of sentences, which motivates the [seventh entry](/blog/predicate-logic).

Notation follows the series overview: $\neg$ (not), $\land$ (and), $\lor$ (or), $\to$ (if ... then) and $\leftrightarrow$ (if and only if). Truth values are written T and F. Greek letters $\varphi$, $\psi$ and $\chi$ stand for arbitrary formulas when a rule or definition is stated in general.

## 1. The language of sentential logic

### 1.1 Why a formal language

English is a poor medium for checking validity. The same word does different jobs: "or" sometimes excludes both options and sometimes does not. Sentences hide their structure: "the alarm sounds unless the door is closed and the key is turned" can be grouped two ways. And an argument's form is mixed up with its subject matter. A formal language removes these problems by fixing a vocabulary, a grammar and a single meaning for every symbol. Once an argument is written in it, the question "is this valid?" has a definite answer that can be computed.

The price is that only some features of meaning survive translation. Sentential logic keeps exactly one feature of each simple sentence, its truth value, and studies how the truth values of compound sentences depend on the truth values of their parts.

### 1.2 Symbols and well-formed formulas

The vocabulary of sentential logic has three kinds of symbols:

- **Sentence letters** $P$, $Q$, $R$, $S$ and so on (with subscripts if more are needed), each standing for a simple declarative sentence.
- **Connectives**: $\neg$, $\land$, $\lor$, $\to$ and $\leftrightarrow$.
- **Brackets** ( and ), which group.

Not every string of these symbols is meaningful. The **well-formed formulas** (WFFs) are exactly the strings produced by three formation rules:

1. Every sentence letter is a WFF.
2. If $\varphi$ is a WFF, then $\neg\varphi$ is a WFF.
3. If $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are WFFs, then $(\varphi \land \psi)$, $(\varphi \lor \psi)$, $(\varphi \to \psi)$ and $(\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi)$ are WFFs.

Nothing else is a WFF. The definition is **recursive**: rules 2 and 3 build longer formulas from shorter ones, starting from the sentence letters of rule 1. So $(P \land \neg Q)$ is a WFF, built by rule 1 twice, rule 2 once and rule 3 once, while $P \neg \land Q$ and $(P \lor Q$ are not, because no sequence of rules produces them. Programmers will recognize this as a context-free grammar, and the procedure that checks a candidate string as a parser.

Two conventions keep formulas readable. The outermost pair of brackets may be dropped, so $P \land \neg Q$ abbreviates $(P \land \neg Q)$. Every other bracket stays: $P \land Q \lor R$ is not a WFF, because it could mean $(P \land Q) \lor R$ or $P \land (Q \lor R)$, and those two formulas have different truth tables (Section 2.2 shows how to check).

### 1.3 Main connective and scope

Every compound WFF was produced by one last application of rule 2 or rule 3. The connective introduced at that step is the formula's **main connective**, and it determines what kind of formula it is: a negation, conjunction, disjunction, conditional or biconditional. The **scope** of a connective occurrence is the smallest WFF that contains it. Finding the main connective is the first step in computing a truth value, in translating back into English and in planning a proof.

## 2. Connectives and truth tables

### 2.1 The meaning of each connective

The connectives of sentential logic are **truth-functional**: the truth value of a compound formula is fixed entirely by the truth values of its immediate parts. A connective can therefore be defined by a **truth table** that lists every combination of values for its parts and the resulting value. With two parts there are four combinations:

| $\varphi$ | $\psi$ | $\neg\varphi$ | $\varphi \land \psi$ | $\varphi \lor \psi$ | $\varphi \to \psi$ | $\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | F | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | F | T | F | F |
| F | T | T | F | T | T | F |
| F | F | T | F | F | T | T |

In words: a **negation** reverses the value of the formula it negates. A **conjunction** is true when both conjuncts are true. A **disjunction** is true when at least one disjunct is true; this is the inclusive "or", and an exclusive "or" must be written out (Section 4.2). A **conditional** $\varphi \to \psi$, with **antecedent** $\varphi$ and **consequent** $\psi$, is false only when the antecedent is true and the consequent false; Section 6 discusses why. A **biconditional** is true when both sides have the same value.

Digital designers will recognize these tables: negation, conjunction and disjunction behave like the NOT, AND and OR logic gates, and the biconditional behaves like the exclusive-NOR (XNOR) gate, the negation of exclusive or.

### 2.2 Truth tables for compound formulas

A formula with $n$ distinct sentence letters has $2^n$ possible assignments of truth values, so its truth table has $2^n$ rows. A convenient convention lists them like binary numbers counted downward: the first letter alternates in blocks of $2^{n-1}$, the next in blocks of $2^{n-2}$, and the last letter alternates every row. The table is then filled one column per subformula, working from the sentence letters up the parse tree to the main connective.

For $(P \lor Q) \to (P \land R)$, three letters give eight rows:

| $P$ | $Q$ | $R$ | $P \lor Q$ | $P \land R$ | $(P \lor Q) \to (P \land R)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | T | T | T |
| T | T | F | T | F | F |
| T | F | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | F | F |
| F | T | T | T | F | F |
| F | T | F | T | F | F |
| F | F | T | F | F | T |
| F | F | F | F | F | T |

The final column is the formula's truth value under each assignment. It is true in four rows and false in four. The bracket warning of Section 1.2 can be checked the same way: in the row where $P$ is F, $Q$ is T and $R$ is T, $(P \land Q) \lor R$ is T while $P \land (Q \lor R)$ is F, so the two groupings say different things.

## 3. Logical properties and relations

### 3.1 Tautologies, contradictions and contingent formulas

The final column of a truth table classifies a formula:

- A **tautology** is true in every row: $P \lor \neg P$, or $(P \to Q) \to (\neg Q \to \neg P)$.
- A **contradiction** (an inconsistent formula) is false in every row: $P \land \neg P$.
- A **contingent** formula is true in some rows and false in others, as the formula of Section 2.2 is.

A tautology is true whatever the facts are, which is why it carries no information about them. "Either the test passes or it does not" is guaranteed true and tells an engineer nothing. Conversely, a specification that turns out to be a contradiction can never be met by any implementation, which is a useful thing to detect before anyone writes code.

### 3.2 Consistency, entailment and equivalence

Truth tables also decide relations between formulas. A set of formulas is **consistent** when at least one row makes all of them true together, and inconsistent otherwise. A set of formulas $\Gamma$ **entails** a formula $\varphi$, written $\Gamma \vDash \varphi$, when every row that makes all formulas in $\Gamma$ true also makes $\varphi$ true. Two formulas are **logically equivalent** when they have the same value in every row, that is, when each entails the other, or when the biconditional between them is a tautology.

Equivalences are rewriting rules. The most used are De Morgan's laws, $\neg(P \land Q)$ equivalent to $\neg P \lor \neg Q$ and $\neg(P \lor Q)$ equivalent to $\neg P \land \neg Q$, which say how negation passes through conjunction and disjunction. The conditional has two standard rewritings: $P \to Q$ is equivalent to $\neg P \lor Q$ and to its **contrapositive** $\neg Q \to \neg P$, but not to its **converse** $Q \to P$. A programmer who negates the loop guard `while (!done && retries < 3)` is applying De Morgan's law, and a wrong negation here is a classic source of off-by-one and never-terminating loops.

These equivalences show that the five connectives are more than strictly needed. Every truth-functional connective, of any number of places, can be defined from $\neg$ and $\land$ alone, or from $\neg$ and $\lor$, or from $\neg$ and $\to$. A set of connectives with this property is **functionally complete**. H. M. Sheffer showed in 1913 that a single two-place connective suffices for Boolean algebra, and both "not both" (NAND) and "neither ... nor" (NOR) have this property. That every Boolean circuit can be built from NAND gates alone, or from NOR gates alone, is the same theorem in hardware.

### 3.3 Explosion in truth-table terms

The fourth entry, Section 4.5, showed that an inconsistent set of premises entails every statement, and that the resulting argument is valid but never sound. A truth table shows the mechanism at work. Write out the four rows for $P$, $\neg P$ and an unrelated $Q$: the columns for $P$ and $\neg P$ are never T together, so no row is a candidate counterexample, and $P, \neg P \vDash Q$ holds whatever $Q$ is. Logicians call this the **principle of explosion**.

Its practical lesson runs the other way: a set of requirements, beliefs or axioms that contains a contradiction no longer constrains anything, so finding the contradiction matters more than any conclusion drawn from the set.

## 4. Formalization

### 4.1 From English to formulas

To **formalize** an argument is to translate it into the formal language so that its form can be tested. The procedure has three steps:

1. Identify the simple sentences, the ones that contain no connective, and assign each a sentence letter in a **translation key**. Use the same letter for the same claim even when the wording changes, and a negation for its denial rather than a new letter.
2. Find the main connective of each premise and the conclusion, and work inward, adding brackets where English grouping is implicit.
3. Check the result by reading it back into English and asking whether it says the same thing.

Here is a deployment policy as an example. "A release goes out only if the tests pass and the security scan is clean. The tests passed but the scan was not clean. So the release does not go out." With the key $G$: the release goes out, $T$: the tests pass, $C$: the security scan is clean, the argument is

$$
G \to (T \land C),\quad T \land \neg C \;\therefore\; \neg G
$$

Section 5 shows how to test it; it is valid.

### 4.2 English connectives

English has many ways to express the five connectives, and some of them are easy to get wrong:

| English | Formula | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| not $P$; it is false that $P$ | $\neg P$ | |
| $P$ and $Q$; $P$ but $Q$; $P$ although $Q$ | $P \land Q$ | "but" adds contrast, not a different truth condition |
| $P$ or $Q$; either $P$ or $Q$ | $P \lor Q$ | inclusive unless the context excludes both |
| $P$ or $Q$ but not both | $(P \lor Q) \land \neg(P \land Q)$ | exclusive or |
| neither $P$ nor $Q$ | $\neg P \land \neg Q$ | equivalently $\neg(P \lor Q)$ |
| if $P$ then $Q$; $Q$ if $P$; $P$ is sufficient for $Q$ | $P \to Q$ | |
| $P$ only if $Q$; $Q$ is necessary for $P$ | $P \to Q$ | not $Q \to P$ |
| $P$ unless $Q$ | $\neg Q \to P$ | equivalently $P \lor Q$ |
| $P$ if and only if $Q$; $P$ exactly when $Q$ | $P \leftrightarrow Q$ | |

"Only if" causes the most errors. "The pump runs only if the tank has water" says that running requires water, so $R \to W$. It does not say that water is enough to start the pump. "Unless" is the second: "the valve stays closed unless the operator opens it" is $\neg O \to C$, and on the inclusive reading it is equivalent to $C \lor O$. Whether it also promises that the valve opens when the operator acts is a question about the speaker's intent that the translator has to settle, and the two readings are different formulas.

### 4.3 What formalization loses

Truth-functional translation drops everything except truth value. Two kinds of loss matter in practice. First, some English uses of "and" carry order or causation: "the controller crashed and the arm stopped" suggests that one event caused or preceded the other, but $C \land A$ is equivalent to $A \land C$. Second, some connectives are not truth-functional at all. The truth of "the arm stopped because the controller crashed" is not fixed by the truth values of its two parts: both parts can be true while the causal claim is false. Such a sentence can only be formalized as a single sentence letter, which hides its structure. When an argument's validity depends on these features, sentential logic cannot capture it, and a careful analyst says so rather than forcing a translation.

## 5. Testing validity

### 5.1 The full truth-table method

An argument form is valid in sentential logic exactly when its premises entail its conclusion: no assignment of truth values makes every premise true and the conclusion false. The **truth-table method** tests this directly. Write one column for each premise and one for the conclusion, compute them for all $2^n$ rows, and look for a row with all premises T and the conclusion F. If there is none, the form is valid. If there is one, that row is a **counterexample**: an assignment under which the premises are true and the conclusion false, which proves invalidity.

Two arguments about a build pipeline show both outcomes. Let $B$: the build passes and $D$: the image is deployed.

| $B$ | $D$ | $B \to D$ | $\neg D$ | $\neg B$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | F | F |
| T | F | F | T | F |
| F | T | T | F | T |
| F | F | T | T | T |

*Modus tollens* (third entry, Section 5), $B \to D$ and $\neg D$, therefore $\neg B$, has both premises true only in the last row, where the conclusion $\neg B$ is also true. It is valid. *Affirming the consequent*, $B \to D$ and $D$, therefore $B$, has the premise $B \to D$ and the premise $D$ both true in the first and third rows, and in the third row the conclusion $B$ is false: the image can be deployed by some other route while the build failed. It is invalid, and the third row is the counterexample.

### 5.2 The short truth-table method

A full table for $n$ letters has $2^n$ rows, so ten letters need 1024 rows. The **short truth-table method** (also called the indirect method) searches for a counterexample directly instead of listing every row. It runs as a proof by contradiction:

1. Assume the argument is invalid: assign T to every premise and F to the conclusion.
2. Work out every value these assumptions force, using the truth tables of the connectives. A conditional assigned F forces its antecedent to T and its consequent to F; a conjunction assigned T forces both conjuncts to T; a disjunction assigned F forces both disjuncts to F; and so on.
3. If some formula is forced to be both T and F, no counterexample exists and the argument is valid. If a complete and consistent assignment results, it is a counterexample and the argument is invalid.

Take the argument $P \to (Q \lor R)$, $Q \to S$, $R \to S$, therefore $P \to S$. Assume the conclusion is F: then $P$ is T and $S$ is F. The premise $Q \to S$ is T with $S$ F, so $Q$ must be F; likewise $R \to S$ forces $R$ to F. Now $Q \lor R$ is F and $P$ is T, so the first premise $P \to (Q \lor R)$ is F, contradicting the assumption that it is T. No counterexample exists, and the argument is valid. Four forced steps replaced a 16-row table.

Now take $P \lor Q$, $Q \to R$, therefore $R$. Assume $R$ is F. The premise $Q \to R$ then forces $Q$ to F, and $P \lor Q$ forces $P$ to T. Every premise is T and the conclusion is F under $P$ = T, $Q$ = F, $R$ = F, with no clash. That assignment is a counterexample, so the argument is invalid.

Sometimes nothing is forced: a disjunction assigned T, a conjunction assigned F or a biconditional either way leaves several options. The method then splits into cases and tries each. The argument is valid only if every case ends in a contradiction; one consistent case is enough for invalidity. A short table must always be checked by substituting the final assignment back into every premise and the conclusion, because a slip in propagation is easy to make.

### 5.3 Decidability and its cost

The truth-table method always terminates with the correct answer, for any argument in the language. A logic with such a procedure for validity is **decidable**, and sentential logic is decidable. The predicate logic of the next entry is not, which is one of the main differences between them.

Decidable does not mean cheap. The number of rows doubles with each new letter, and no method is known that decides every formula in time polynomial in its length. Deciding whether a formula has any row that makes it true, the Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT), was the first problem shown to be NP-complete (nondeterministic polynomial time complete, roughly: as hard as any problem whose solutions can be checked quickly), by S. A. Cook in 1971. Validity reduces to it: an argument is valid exactly when the premises together with the negated conclusion are unsatisfiable. Modern SAT solvers handle many large industrial instances, such as checks on hardware designs, but they do so by clever search, not by escaping the worst case.

## 6. The material conditional

### 6.1 Why the table looks the way it does

The conditional of Section 2.1 is called the **material conditional**. Its table puzzles most readers in the two rows with a false antecedent: "if the moon is made of cheese, then $2 + 2 = 4$" comes out true, and so does "if the moon is made of cheese, then $2 + 2 = 5$".

A mathematical example shows why those rows are right for at least one use of "if". The claim "for every integer $x$, if $x > 2$ then $x^2 > 4$" is true. A universal claim is true only if it holds for every instance, so each instance must come out true, including $x = -3$ (antecedent F, consequent T) and $x = 0$ (antecedent F, consequent F). The only instance that could refute the claim would be one with $x > 2$ true and $x^2 > 4$ false. The material conditional encodes exactly this: a conditional is refuted only by a true antecedent with a false consequent.

There is also a structural argument. If the conditional is to be truth-functional at all, its value in each row is either T or F. Its first two rows are not in dispute: a true antecedent with a true consequent makes it true, and a true antecedent with a false consequent makes it false. That leaves four choices for the last two rows, and the other three are all unacceptable. Making the fourth row F turns $\varphi \to \psi$ into a formula equivalent to $\psi$ alone, so the antecedent would not matter. Making the third row F turns it into the biconditional, so every conditional would be equivalent to its converse. Making both F turns it into the conjunction, so asserting "if it rains, the match is canceled" would assert that it rains. The material conditional is the only truth-functional option left.

### 6.2 Where it departs from English

The price of the material conditional shows in arguments that are valid for $\to$ but sound odd for "if":

- $\neg P \vDash P \to Q$: from "the server is not overloaded" it follows that "if the server is overloaded, then the database is on fire".
- $Q \vDash P \to Q$: any true sentence is materially implied by every sentence, related or not.
- $\neg(P \to Q) \vDash P$: denying a conditional commits one to its antecedent, although in English "it is not true that if it rains the match is canceled" does not assert that it rains.

These are called the paradoxes of material implication. They arise because many English conditionals assert a connection between antecedent and consequent (a cause, a rule, a reason), while $\to$ asserts only that the combination T then F does not occur. Counterfactual conditionals ("if the relief valve had opened, the tank would not have burst") are the clearest case: their antecedents are false, so the material reading makes all of them true, which is plainly wrong for engineering failure analysis.

The practical rule is this. When an English conditional states a general rule or an engineering invariant, the material conditional captures what matters for validity. When it asserts a counterfactual, a causal link or a relevance relation, sentential logic captures only part of the claim, and conclusions that depend on the rest need other tools.

## 7. Natural deduction

### 7.1 Derivations

Truth tables establish validity by checking every case. A **derivation** establishes it by a chain of small steps, each licensed by a rule whose validity can be checked at a glance, from the premises to the conclusion. A system of such rules modeled on ordinary step-by-step reasoning is a **natural-deduction system**; the approach goes back to the work of G. Gentzen in the 1930s. Derivations are worth learning even though truth tables already decide validity: they scale to predicate logic, where no table exists, and they display the reasoning, not only the verdict.

This entry uses a compact layout in the style of E. J. Lemmon's *Beginning Logic*. Each line has four entries: a line number, the formula, the rule and lines that justify it, and the **dependencies**, the numbers of the assumptions on which that line rests. Writing $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ (read "$\varphi$ is derivable from $\Gamma$", with the **turnstile** $\vdash$) means there is a derivation whose last line is $\varphi$ and whose dependencies are all formulas in $\Gamma$. If the last line has no dependencies, $\varphi$ is a **theorem**, written $\vdash \varphi$.

### 7.2 The rules

The system has one structural rule and two rules for each connective, one that introduces it and one that eliminates it:

| Rule | From | Write | Dependencies of the new line |
|---|---|---|---|
| A (assumption) | nothing | any WFF $\varphi$ | the line itself |
| $\land$I | $\varphi$ and $\psi$ | $\varphi \land \psi$ | those of both lines |
| $\land$E | $\varphi \land \psi$ | $\varphi$, or $\psi$ | those of the conjunction |
| $\to$E (*modus ponens*) | $\varphi \to \psi$ and $\varphi$ | $\psi$ | those of both lines |
| $\to$I | an assumption $\varphi$ and a later line $\psi$ | $\varphi \to \psi$ | those of $\psi$, minus the assumption $\varphi$ |
| $\lor$I | $\varphi$ | $\varphi \lor \psi$, or $\psi \lor \varphi$, for any WFF $\psi$ | those of $\varphi$ |
| $\lor$E (cases) | $\varphi \lor \psi$, $\varphi \to \chi$ and $\psi \to \chi$ | $\chi$ | those of all three lines |
| $\neg$I | an assumption $\varphi$ and a later line $\psi \land \neg\psi$ | $\neg\varphi$ | those of the contradiction, minus $\varphi$ |
| $\neg$E (*reductio*) | an assumption $\neg\varphi$ and a later line $\psi \land \neg\psi$ | $\varphi$ | those of the contradiction, minus $\neg\varphi$ |
| $\leftrightarrow$I | $\varphi \to \psi$ and $\psi \to \varphi$ | $\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi$ | those of both lines |
| $\leftrightarrow$E | $\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi$ | $\varphi \to \psi$, or $\psi \to \varphi$ | those of the biconditional |

The rule of assumption lets anyone write down any formula, but only as resting on itself: the line records "if this, then this", which is harmless. The rules that remove a dependency, $\to$I, $\neg$I and $\neg$E, are said to **discharge** an assumption. They are what make the system powerful: a temporary assumption is made, reasoned from and then discharged, leaving a conclusion that no longer depends on it. A derivation proves $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ only when the dependencies of its final line are among the premises, so every temporary assumption has to be discharged.

### 7.3 Worked derivations

*Modus tollens* follows from the basic rules by assuming the antecedent and deriving a contradiction:

| Line | Formula | Justification | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $P \to Q$ | A | 1 |
| 2 | $\neg Q$ | A | 2 |
| 3 | $P$ | A | 3 |
| 4 | $Q$ | 1, 3 $\to$E | 1, 3 |
| 5 | $Q \land \neg Q$ | 4, 2 $\land$I | 1, 2, 3 |
| 6 | $\neg P$ | 3, 5 $\neg$I | 1, 2 |

Line 3 is a temporary assumption. Lines 4 and 5 show that it, together with the premises, leads to a contradiction, so line 6 concludes its negation and drops 3 from the dependencies. The result is $P \to Q, \neg Q \vdash \neg P$.

Hypothetical syllogism (third entry, Section 5), the chaining of conditionals, uses $\to$I:

| Line | Formula | Justification | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $P \to Q$ | A | 1 |
| 2 | $Q \to R$ | A | 2 |
| 3 | $P$ | A | 3 |
| 4 | $Q$ | 1, 3 $\to$E | 1, 3 |
| 5 | $R$ | 2, 4 $\to$E | 1, 2, 3 |
| 6 | $P \to R$ | 3, 5 $\to$I | 1, 2 |

Disjunctive syllogism, $P \lor Q, \neg P \vdash Q$, needs proof by cases. Each disjunct must lead to $Q$. The second case is immediate. The first is not: from $P$ and $\neg P$ one reaches $Q$ by assuming $\neg Q$, noting the contradiction already present and applying *reductio*:

| Line | Formula | Justification | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $P \lor Q$ | A | 1 |
| 2 | $\neg P$ | A | 2 |
| 3 | $P$ | A | 3 |
| 4 | $\neg Q$ | A | 4 |
| 5 | $P \land \neg P$ | 3, 2 $\land$I | 2, 3 |
| 6 | $Q$ | 4, 5 $\neg$E | 2, 3 |
| 7 | $P \to Q$ | 3, 6 $\to$I | 2 |
| 8 | $Q$ | A | 8 |
| 9 | $Q \to Q$ | 8, 8 $\to$I | none |
| 10 | $Q$ | 1, 7, 9 $\lor$E | 1, 2 |

Line 6 is the derivational form of explosion (Section 3.3): once a contradiction is available, any formula can be derived. Line 9 shows that $\to$I may use the assumption itself as the derived line, which proves the theorem $\vdash Q \to Q$.

Finally, the law of excluded middle, $\vdash P \lor \neg P$, is a theorem with no premises at all:

| Line | Formula | Justification | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\neg(P \lor \neg P)$ | A | 1 |
| 2 | $P$ | A | 2 |
| 3 | $P \lor \neg P$ | 2 $\lor$I | 2 |
| 4 | $(P \lor \neg P) \land \neg(P \lor \neg P)$ | 3, 1 $\land$I | 1, 2 |
| 5 | $\neg P$ | 2, 4 $\neg$I | 1 |
| 6 | $P \lor \neg P$ | 5 $\lor$I | 1 |
| 7 | $(P \lor \neg P) \land \neg(P \lor \neg P)$ | 6, 1 $\land$I | 1 |
| 8 | $P \lor \neg P$ | 1, 7 $\neg$E | none |

Once proved, *modus tollens*, hypothetical syllogism and disjunctive syllogism can be used as **derived rules**: any later derivation could replace one step of a derived rule with a copy of the derivation above, so using them as single steps adds convenience but no new power.

### 7.4 Strategies

A truth table is filled in mechanically; a derivation has to be found. A few heuristics turn most searches into routine work:

- **Read the goal's main connective first.** It usually names the last rule of the derivation. A conditional goal $\varphi \to \psi$ comes from $\to$I: assume $\varphi$, aim for $\psi$. A conjunction comes from $\land$I, so derive each conjunct separately. A negation $\neg\varphi$ comes from $\neg$I: assume $\varphi$, aim for a contradiction. A biconditional needs both conditionals.
- **Eliminate before you introduce.** Break the premises down into what they give directly: conjuncts, consequents of conditionals whose antecedents are available, and the two cases of a disjunction. Many derivations close once the pieces are on the page.
- **Know how each assumption will end.** Every temporary assumption has to be discharged by $\to$I, $\neg$I or $\neg$E before the derivation is finished, so decide which of those rules will discharge it when you write it down.
- **If no direct route appears, argue by contradiction.** Assume the negation of the goal and look for any formula together with its negation. Completeness (Section 8.2) guarantees that a derivation exists for every valid form, and this route is always available as a last resort.
- **Build a library.** Derived rules such as *modus tollens* and the disjunctive syllogism, and theorems such as excluded middle, are proved once and then used as single steps, in the same way that a tested function is called rather than rewritten.

## 8. Soundness, completeness and limits

### 8.1 Two kinds of consequence

The entry now has two notions of "follows from". The **semantic** notion, $\Gamma \vDash \varphi$, is defined by truth: no assignment makes $\Gamma$ true and $\varphi$ false. The **syntactic** notion, $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, is defined by rules: a derivation exists. Claims about how the two relate are **metatheory**: statements made in English about the formal system, not inside it.

The system is **sound**: if $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, then $\Gamma \vDash \varphi$. A derivation never takes true premises to a false conclusion. The proof checks each rule. Call a line *good* if its dependencies entail its formula. An assumption is good because every formula entails itself. Each other rule turns good lines into a good line; for example, if the dependencies of $\varphi \to \psi$ entail it and those of $\varphi$ entail $\varphi$, then any assignment making all of both sets true makes $\varphi \to \psi$ and $\varphi$ true, hence $\psi$ true. Checking the discharging rules takes one more step of the same kind. By induction on the length of the derivation, every line is good, including the last.

### 8.2 Completeness

The system is also **complete**: if $\Gamma \vDash \varphi$, then $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$. Every valid argument form of sentential logic has a derivation in this system. Completeness is harder to prove than soundness, because it asserts the existence of a derivation for every valid form; for sentential logic it was proved by E. L. Post in a paper published in 1921. One standard proof shows how to convert each row of a truth table into a derivation and then combine the rows using the excluded-middle theorem of Section 7.3.

Together, soundness and completeness say that $\vdash$ and $\vDash$ coincide: the rules prove exactly the valid forms, no more and no fewer. This has two practical consequences. If a truth table shows a form valid, a derivation exists, and a systematic search will eventually find it. If a truth table shows a form invalid, no derivation exists, and searching further is pointless. Failure to find a derivation, on the other hand, proves nothing by itself; the truth table, or a counterexample from the short method, settles the question.

### 8.3 What sentential logic cannot see

The smallest unit of sentential logic is a whole sentence. Consider:

> Every sensor that passed calibration is in service. Sensor S7 passed calibration. So sensor S7 is in service.

The fourth entry (Section 5.3) made the general point with a different syllogism. Sentential logic can render such an argument only as an invalid form with three unrelated letters, because its validity lies inside the sentences, in the words "every" and "that" and in the repeated name S7. Sentential logic also cannot show that "some sensor is faulty" follows from "S7 is faulty", or that "someone reviews every change" entails "every change is reviewed by someone". These forms belong to predicate logic, the subject of the next entry.

## 9. Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Which of these strings are WFFs (with outer brackets omitted)? For each WFF, name the main connective. (a) $\neg\neg P \to Q$; (b) $P \land \lor Q$; (c) $\neg(P \leftrightarrow (Q \land \neg R))$; (d) $P \to Q \to R$.

*Answer.* (a) A WFF; main connective $\to$, with $\neg\neg P$ as antecedent. (b) Not a WFF: no rule places two two-place connectives side by side. (c) A WFF; main connective the first $\neg$. (d) Not a WFF: it lacks the bracket that decides between $(P \to Q) \to R$ and $P \to (Q \to R)$, which are not equivalent (try $P$ = F, $Q$ = T, $R$ = F).

**Exercise 2.** Classify $(P \to Q) \to (\neg Q \to \neg P)$ and $P \land \neg(Q \to P)$ as tautology, contradiction or contingent.

*Answer.* The first is a tautology: it states that a conditional implies its contrapositive, and its truth table has T in all four rows. The second is a contradiction: $\neg(Q \to P)$ is true only when $Q$ is T and $P$ is F, but the first conjunct requires $P$ to be T.

**Exercise 3.** Formalize, using $M$: the robot moves, $C$: the path is clear, $E$: the emergency stop is pressed. (a) The robot moves only if the path is clear. (b) The robot moves unless the emergency stop is pressed. (c) The robot moves if and only if the path is clear and the emergency stop is not pressed.

*Answer.* (a) $M \to C$. (b) $\neg E \to M$, equivalently $M \lor E$. (c) $M \leftrightarrow (C \land \neg E)$.

**Exercise 4.** Use the short truth-table method on (a) $P \to Q$, $\neg P \to R$, therefore $Q \lor R$, and (b) $(P \land Q) \to R$, therefore $P \to R$.

*Answer.* (a) Valid. Assuming $Q \lor R$ is F forces $Q$ and $R$ to F. Then $P \to Q$ forces $P$ to F, while $\neg P \to R$ forces $\neg P$ to F, that is $P$ to T: a contradiction. (b) Invalid. Assuming $P \to R$ is F forces $P$ to T and $R$ to F. The premise is then true only if $P \land Q$ is F, so $Q$ is F. The assignment $P$ = T, $Q$ = F, $R$ = F makes the premise T and the conclusion F.

**Exercise 5.** Derive (a) $P \land Q \vdash Q \land P$ and (b) $P \to (Q \to R) \vdash (P \land Q) \to R$.

*Answer.* (a) Line 1: $P \land Q$ (A; depends on 1). Line 2: $P$ (1 $\land$E; 1). Line 3: $Q$ (1 $\land$E; 1). Line 4: $Q \land P$ (3, 2 $\land$I; 1).

(b)

| Line | Formula | Justification | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $P \to (Q \to R)$ | A | 1 |
| 2 | $P \land Q$ | A | 2 |
| 3 | $P$ | 2 $\land$E | 2 |
| 4 | $Q$ | 2 $\land$E | 2 |
| 5 | $Q \to R$ | 1, 3 $\to$E | 1, 2 |
| 6 | $R$ | 5, 4 $\to$E | 1, 2 |
| 7 | $(P \land Q) \to R$ | 2, 6 $\to$I | 1 |

**Exercise 6.** A colleague spends an hour failing to derive $Q$ from $P \to Q$ and $\neg P \to Q$, and concludes that the argument is invalid. Is the conclusion justified?

*Answer.* No. Failure to find a derivation does not show that none exists. The short method settles it: assuming $Q$ is F forces $P$ to F through the first premise and $\neg P$ to F through the second, a contradiction, so the argument is valid and, by completeness, derivable. (Hint for the derivation: derive $P \lor \neg P$ as in Section 7.3 and use proof by cases.)

## 10. Summary

| Topic | Main result |
|---|---|
| Language | WFFs are built recursively from sentence letters with $\neg$, $\land$, $\lor$, $\to$, $\leftrightarrow$; every compound WFF has one main connective. |
| Meaning | Each connective is truth-functional and defined by a truth table; $\varphi \to \psi$ is false only when $\varphi$ is T and $\psi$ is F. |
| Properties | A formula is a tautology, a contradiction or contingent; sets are consistent or not; $\Gamma \vDash \varphi$ when no row makes $\Gamma$ true and $\varphi$ false. |
| Formalization | Fix a translation key, find each main connective, read the result back; "only if" and "unless" need care; causal and counterfactual content is lost. |
| Validity tests | Full truth tables always decide validity in $2^n$ rows; the short method searches for a counterexample by forced values and case splits. |
| Natural deduction | Assumption plus introduction and elimination rules for each connective; $\to$I, $\neg$I and $\neg$E discharge assumptions. |
| Metatheory | The system is sound and complete, so $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ exactly when $\Gamma \vDash \varphi$. |
| Limits | Validity that depends on "every", "some" or names inside sentences is invisible to sentential logic. |

Sentential logic gives a complete account of arguments that turn on how sentences are combined, with a mechanical test and a proof system that agree. Its blind spot is the inside of a sentence. The [next entry](/blog/predicate-logic) opens sentences up into names, predicates and the quantifiers "every" and "some", and extends both the semantics and the derivation rules to them.
