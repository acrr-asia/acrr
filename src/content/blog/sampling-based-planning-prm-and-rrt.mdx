The [previous entry](/blog/graph-search-dijkstra-and-a-star) turned the configuration space into a grid and searched it with Dijkstra's algorithm and A*. Those searches are optimal on the grid, but [Section 6 of that entry](/blog/graph-search-dijkstra-and-a-star#6-the-limits-of-grids) showed their price. The number of cells grows as the resolution raised to the power of the dimension, so a grid that is fine for a mobile robot in the plane becomes impossible for an arm with six or seven joints. This third entry of [Motion Planning for Robots](/series/motion-planning) describes the methods that replaced the grid in high-dimensional planning. They place configurations at random, keep the ones that are free, join them with short collision-free motions and search the graph that results. The two families covered are **probabilistic roadmaps**, which build a reusable graph of the free space, and **rapidly-exploring random trees**, which grow a tree from the start toward the goal.

The entry builds on the configuration space, the obstacle region and the collision checker of the [first entry](/blog/configuration-space-and-the-planning-problem), and on the guarantees defined in [its Section 4](/blog/configuration-space-and-the-planning-problem#4-paths-cost-and-what-a-planner-guarantees). It uses Dijkstra's algorithm from the second entry as a subroutine. It explains how the two families work and what their guarantees mean, which variants converge to optimal paths, where sampling fails and how a sampled path is shortened before a robot uses it.

**Conventions.** The notation is that of the series. A configuration is $q$, the configuration space $\mathcal{C}$ has dimension $d$, its free part is $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}}$ and its obstacle region $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{obs}}$; a query asks for a path from $q_{\mathrm{start}}$ to $q_{\mathrm{goal}}$. A planner draws $n$ samples and joins each to the samples within a connection radius $r_n$ or to its $k$ nearest neighbors; a random tree advances by at most a step size $\eta$ per iteration. The cost of a path is its length, and $C^*$ is the optimal cost. Logarithms are natural. Lengths are in meters for the planar examples and in radians for the arm, whose joint angles $\theta_1, \theta_2$ and link lengths $l_1 = 1.0$ m and $l_2 = 0.7$ m are those of [Forward and Inverse Kinematics of a Planar Arm](/blog/planar-arm-kinematics). The code is Python 3.13 with NumPy 2.5. Every random choice is drawn from `numpy.random.default_rng(seed)` with the seed stated, and results are reported as the median, minimum and maximum over 10 or 20 seeds rather than as one run, because a single run of a randomized planner can be unusually lucky or unlucky.

## 1. Why sample

### 1.1 The cost of covering a space

A grid with $m$ cells per axis has $m^d$ cells, and [Section 6.4 of the second entry](/blog/graph-search-dijkstra-and-a-star#64-the-curse-of-dimensionality) showed what that means: $10^{12}$ cells for an arm with six joints at 100 cells per joint, each of which needs a collision check. No refinement of A* changes this count, because the grid spends the same effort on every region of the space, whether or not a path could pass there.

Most of that effort is wasted. A planner needs only enough of the free space to connect the start to the goal. In many practical problems the free space is large and well connected: an arm reaching into a shelf has a few awkward regions near the shelf and a great deal of open space elsewhere. A method that places a modest number of configurations where they help, and connects them with straight segments, can answer such a query with thousands of collision checks instead of billions.

### 1.2 The collision checker as the only geometry

The methods of this entry share one design decision: they never build $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{obs}}$ explicitly. The first entry showed that even for a two-link arm and a disk the obstacle region has a curved and nonconvex shape, and in higher dimensions it has no usable closed form. Sampling-based planners replace it with two queries:

- a **point check**, which asks whether a single configuration $q$ is free, by placing the robot's geometry at $q$ and testing it against the obstacles in the workspace;
- an **edge check**, which asks whether the straight segment between two configurations is free, by running point checks at configurations spaced at most a resolution apart along it, as in [Section 5 of the first entry](/blog/configuration-space-and-the-planning-problem#5-collision-checking).

Everything the planner knows about the obstacles comes through these two calls, which is why the same planner code works for a point robot in the plane and for a seven-joint arm: only the collision checker and the distance function change. It also means that collision checks are the natural measure of a planner's cost, and that the planner inherits the checker's weaknesses. An edge check at resolution $\delta$ can miss an obstacle thinner than $\delta$, so a path returned by any planner of this entry is collision-free only up to the resolution of its checker, unless the checker is exact or conservative.

### 1.3 Probabilistic completeness

Giving up the grid also gives up the grid's guarantee. A grid search is resolution complete: it finds a path if one exists at the grid's resolution. A sampling-based planner is at best **probabilistically complete**, in the sense of [Section 4.2 of the first entry](/blog/configuration-space-and-the-planning-problem#42-completeness): if a path exists, the probability that the planner finds one tends to 1 as the number of samples grows. The textbooks of LaValle and of Choset et al. treat this notion and the planners of this entry at greater length. The definition says nothing about how fast the probability approaches 1, and that rate depends on the geometry of the free space, as Section 5 shows. Nor can a sampling-based planner prove that no path exists. When it fails after $n$ samples, the failure means only that $n$ was not enough or that there is no path, and the planner cannot tell which.

## 2. Probabilistic roadmaps

### 2.1 A graph of random free configurations

A **probabilistic roadmap** (PRM), introduced by Kavraki, Švestka, Latombe and Overmars in 1996, is a graph whose vertices are random free configurations and whose edges are collision-free straight segments between nearby vertices. The method has two phases. In the **construction phase** (which the original paper calls the learning phase) it samples configurations uniformly in $\mathcal{C}$, keeps those that pass the point check and tries to connect each to its neighbors with edge checks. In the **query phase** it connects $q_{\mathrm{start}}$ and $q_{\mathrm{goal}}$ to nearby roadmap vertices and searches the graph, for example with Dijkstra's algorithm.

The split into phases is the point of the method. The construction phase is expensive and does not depend on the query, so its result can be stored and reused. A robot that works in a fixed cell, such as an arm at a workstation, can build one roadmap and answer many queries from it, each costing only a few edge checks and a graph search. A PRM is therefore called a **multi-query** planner. The original paper was written for robots with many degrees of freedom, as its title says.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[Sample q uniformly] --> B{Point check}
  B -- in collision --> A
  B -- free --> C[Add vertex]
  C --> D[Neighbors within r or k nearest]
  D --> E{Edge check}
  E -- free --> F[Add edge]
  F --> G[Roadmap]
  E -- blocked --> G
  G --> H[Query: join start and goal, search]
```

*The two phases of a probabilistic roadmap. Everything before the query box is done once; each query then costs a few edge checks and a graph search.*

### 2.2 Choosing neighbors

The construction phase must decide which pairs of vertices to try. Two rules are common: join each vertex to all vertices within a **connection radius** $r$, or to its **$k$ nearest neighbors**. A larger neighborhood gives a better-connected graph and shorter paths, but every candidate edge costs an edge check, and edge checks dominate the cost of construction. Finding the neighbors is a nearest-neighbor query; the programs here compute all distances with NumPy, which is adequate for a few thousand vertices, while larger planners use spatial data structures such as k-d trees.

The listing below is the construction phase used in this entry. It omits the query phase (`prm_query`), which joins the start and goal to their $k$ nearest vertices and runs Dijkstra's algorithm, and the collision checker and distance functions, which are methods of the `space` object; all are in `mp.py`, the module that the programs of this entry share.

```python
def sample_free(space, rng, n):
    pts = []
    while len(pts) < n:
        q = space.sample(rng)
        if space.point_free(q):
            pts.append(q)
    return np.array(pts)


def prm_build(space, rng, n, radius=None, k=None):
    """Roadmap on n free samples. Each sample is joined to every sample
    within `radius` or to its k nearest neighbors, if the edge is free."""
    V = sample_free(space, rng, n)
    adj = {i: [] for i in range(n)}
    seen = set()
    for i in range(n):
        dd = space.dists(V, V[i])
        if radius is not None:
            nbrs = [j for j in np.nonzero(dd <= radius)[0] if j != i]
        else:
            nbrs = [j for j in np.argsort(dd)[1:k + 1]]
        for j in nbrs:
            e = (min(i, j), max(i, j))
            if e in seen:
                continue
            seen.add(e)
            if space.edge_free(V[i], V[j]):
                w = float(dd[j])
                adj[i].append((j, w))
                adj[j].append((i, w))
    return V, adj
```

This version tries every neighbor, even one that is already reachable through the graph. Some formulations skip a neighbor that lies in the same connected component, which keeps the roadmap a forest and saves edge checks; Karaman and Frazzoli showed that this economy prevents the roadmap's paths from improving as samples are added, a point Section 4 returns to.

## 3. Rapidly-exploring random trees

### 3.1 Growing a tree from the start

Many queries are asked once: a mobile robot in a building that changes, an arm picking objects whose positions differ each time. Building a roadmap of the whole space for one query wastes most of its edge checks. A **rapidly-exploring random tree** (RRT), introduced by LaValle in a 1998 technical report and developed with Kuffner in later papers, is a **single-query** planner: it grows a tree from $q_{\mathrm{start}}$ until a branch reaches $q_{\mathrm{goal}}$.

Each iteration draws a random configuration $q_{\mathrm{rand}}$, finds the tree vertex $q_{\mathrm{near}}$ nearest to it, and moves from $q_{\mathrm{near}}$ toward $q_{\mathrm{rand}}$ by at most the step size $\eta$, giving $q_{\mathrm{new}}$. If the segment from $q_{\mathrm{near}}$ to $q_{\mathrm{new}}$ is free, $q_{\mathrm{new}}$ joins the tree as a child of $q_{\mathrm{near}}$. When the random sample lies farther than $\eta$ from the tree, it is only a direction to grow in: it is not added and is never checked itself. When it lies within $\eta$, $q_{\mathrm{new}}$ is the sample.

### 3.2 The Voronoi bias

The rule explores well because of the way it selects the vertex to extend. A vertex is selected when the random sample falls closer to it than to any other vertex, that is, inside its **Voronoi region**, the set of points nearer to it than to every other vertex. Vertices on the frontier of the tree, next to unexplored space, have large Voronoi regions; vertices in the interior, surrounded by other vertices, have small ones. For a uniform sample, the probability that a vertex is selected for extension is proportional to the volume of its Voronoi region within $\mathcal{C}$, so the tree is pulled toward the largest unexplored parts of the space. Kuffner and LaValle described this **Voronoi bias** as what makes the tree explore rapidly, the property the method is named for. As the tree fills the space, the regions shrink and even out, and the extensions become more uniform.

The bias has a cost near obstacles. A frontier vertex facing a wall keeps a large Voronoi region, so it keeps being selected, and its extensions keep failing the edge check. Such failed iterations add collision checks without adding vertices, which is visible in the gap between iterations and vertices in the results below.

### 3.3 Goal bias and the stopping rule

Uniform samples ignore the goal. A common refinement, the **goal bias**, replaces the random sample by $q_{\mathrm{goal}}$ with a small probability, here 0.05, so that the tree occasionally tries to grow straight at the goal. A larger value speeds up easy problems and slows down hard ones, because an extension toward the goal that is blocked by an obstacle fails every time it is tried. After each new vertex, the planner checks whether the goal lies within $\eta$ and the segment to it is free; if so, it adds the goal and reads the path back through the parent pointers. The listing is the complete planner as run; `steer` moves from one configuration toward another by at most $\eta$, and `space.interp` interpolates (on the torus, the short way around).

```python
def steer(space, a, b, eta):
    d = space.dist(a, b)
    if d <= eta:
        return b.copy()
    return space.interp(a, b, eta / d)


def rrt(space, rng, qs, qg, eta, goal_bias=0.05, max_iter=20000):
    """Basic RRT. Returns (path or None, tree size, iterations)."""
    V = [qs.copy()]
    parent = [-1]
    for it in range(1, max_iter + 1):
        qr = qg if rng.random() < goal_bias else space.sample(rng)
        i = int(np.argmin(space.dists(np.array(V), qr)))
        qn = steer(space, V[i], qr, eta)
        if not space.edge_free(V[i], qn):
            continue
        V.append(qn)
        parent.append(i)
        if space.dist(qn, qg) <= eta and space.edge_free(qn, qg):
            V.append(qg.copy())
            parent.append(len(V) - 2)
            path = [len(V) - 1]
            while parent[path[-1]] != -1:
                path.append(parent[path[-1]])
            return [V[j] for j in path[::-1]], len(V), it
    return None, len(V), max_iter
```

### 3.4 RRT-Connect

Kuffner and LaValle's **RRT-Connect** (2000) grows two trees, one from the start and one from the goal, and makes them meet. Each iteration extends one tree by one step toward a random sample, exactly as above. Then it tries to connect the other tree to the new vertex greedily: it steps toward it repeatedly, by $\eta$ each time, until it reaches the vertex or hits an obstacle. Then the two trees swap roles. When the greedy connection reaches the new vertex, the two branches form a path. The greedy step makes the method fast in open space, where one connection attempt often covers a long distance, while the random extensions keep it exploring when the greedy step is blocked. The original paper reported it on planning problems for a seven-degree-of-freedom human arm model.

### 3.6 Completeness of roadmaps and trees

A PRM that joins vertices within a connection radius, and RRT, are both probabilistically complete under mild conditions; a PRM with a fixed number $k$ of nearest neighbors, like the one in this entry, need not be (Karaman and Frazzoli show that it is not for $k = 1$). For roadmaps, the analysis by Hsu, Latombe and Motwani introduced **expansiveness**, a measure of how much of the free space each region can see by straight segments. In a space that is expansive in their sense, the probability that a uniformly sampled roadmap fails to connect two configurations that lie in one component of the free space decreases exponentially in the number of samples. The constants in that bound depend on the expansiveness parameters, and a narrow passage makes them poor.

For RRT the question was settled carefully only later: Kleinbort, Solovey, Littlefield, Bekris and Halperin gave a proof for geometric planning that requires only that some solution path keep a positive clearance from the obstacles. A path that touches an obstacle, or squeezes through a gap of zero width, is a solution that no sampling-based planner can be expected to find.

## 4. Asymptotic optimality: PRM* and RRT*

### 4.1 Why the basic planners do not converge

The results above show that PRM and RRT find paths of mediocre quality. A natural hope is that running them longer helps. Karaman and Frazzoli examined this question in 2011. A planner is **asymptotically optimal**, as [Section 4.3 of the first entry](/blog/configuration-space-and-the-planning-problem#43-optimality-guarantees) defined it, if the cost of its solution converges to $C^*$, almost surely, as the number of samples grows. They proved that, under mild technical conditions, RRT is not: the probability that its solution converges to the optimal cost is zero. The reason is that the tree never changes a vertex's parent, so a branch that starts in a bad direction stays bad. A PRM with a fixed number of neighbors $k$ is not asymptotically optimal either, nor is the variant that skips neighbors in the same component (Section 2.2). A PRM that joins every pair within a fixed radius is asymptotically optimal, but its number of edges grows quadratically with $n$, which makes it expensive for large $n$.

### 4.2 The connection radius

The remedy is to let the neighborhood shrink as samples are added, but more slowly than the typical spacing between samples. In a $d$-dimensional space, $n$ uniform samples are spaced roughly $(\mu(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}})/n)^{1/d}$ apart, where $\mu(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}})$ is the volume of the free space; this length is proportional to $n^{-1/d}$. Karaman and Frazzoli's **PRM*** joins each vertex to all vertices within

$$
r_n = \gamma_{\mathrm{PRM}} \left( \frac{\log n}{n} \right)^{1/d},
\qquad
\gamma_{\mathrm{PRM}} > 2 \left( 1 + \frac{1}{d} \right)^{1/d} \left( \frac{\mu(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}})}{\zeta_d} \right)^{1/d},
$$

where $\zeta_d$ is the volume of the unit ball in $d$ dimensions ($\zeta_2 = \pi$). The extra factor $(\log n)^{1/d}$ over the sample spacing makes each vertex see, on average, a number of neighbors that grows like $\log n$: enough for the graph to stay connected and for its paths to approach the optimum, few enough that the number of edges grows only as $n \log n$. They showed that the computational cost of PRM* and of RRT* (Section 4.3) is within a constant factor of that of PRM and RRT.

The same idea works with nearest neighbors. The $k$-nearest version of PRM* joins each vertex to its $k(n) = k_{\mathrm{PRM}} \log n$ nearest neighbors with $k_{\mathrm{PRM}} > e\,(1 + 1/d)$; since this bound never exceeds $2e$, the value $k_{\mathrm{PRM}} = 2e$ works in every dimension $d \ge 2$ and needs no knowledge of the free volume. That makes the $k$-nearest form the easier one to use. The radius form needs $\mu(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}})$, which is rarely known; the volume of the whole of $\mathcal{C}$ is a safe overestimate.

For the plane map, $d = 2$ and $\mu(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}}) = 71.80$ m², so the threshold is $2 \times 1.5^{1/2} \times (71.80/\pi)^{1/2} = 11.71$ m. With $n = 1000$, $r_n = 11.71 \times (\log 1000 / 1000)^{1/2} = 0.973$ m, and $k(1000) = \lceil 2e \log 1000 \rceil = 38$.

### 4.3 RRT*

**RRT*** changes how a new vertex joins the tree. After steering to $q_{\mathrm{new}}$ as in RRT, it looks at all tree vertices within a radius $r_n$ of the same form, capped at $\eta$, and does two things. It chooses as the parent of $q_{\mathrm{new}}$ the vertex in that set through which the cost from the start is lowest and whose edge is free, rather than the nearest vertex. Then it **rewires**: for each vertex in the set, if reaching it through $q_{\mathrm{new}}$ is cheaper than its current route, its parent becomes $q_{\mathrm{new}}$, and the saving propagates to its descendants. The tree thus keeps repairing itself, and the branches straighten as samples accumulate. The extra work per iteration is a set of edge checks to the vertices within $r_n$, about $\log n$ of them.

The original proof of RRT*'s asymptotic optimality used a radius of the form above with its own lower bound on the constant. Solovey, Janson, Schmerling, Frazzoli and Pavone later found a gap in that proof, and showed asymptotic optimality for a radius that shrinks as $(\log n / n)^{1/(d+1)}$, slightly more slowly. The program here uses the exponent $1/d$ with $\gamma = 11.71$ m, the PRM* threshold for this map, and caps the radius at $\eta$; that is a practical choice for a demonstration, not a parameter that carries a proof.

### 4.5 What asymptotic optimality promises

The guarantee is a limit: the cost converges to $C^*$ as $n \to \infty$. It does not say how many samples bring the cost within 1% of the optimum; that number depends on the problem and is not known in advance. It does not make a planner find its first solution faster; RRT* spends more checks per iteration than RRT, so it is best used as an **anytime planner**, one that returns its first path early and keeps improving it while time remains. It concerns convergence to the optimum among paths that keep some clearance, so an optimal path that grazes an obstacle corner, as the one here does at $(4.6, 4.5)$, is approached from the free side but never reached. And it is a statement about cost alone: the optimal path in length still has corners, which a robot cannot follow at speed, a problem for the next entry.

## 5. Narrow passages and other failure modes

### 5.1 Why narrow passages are hard

A uniform sample that passes the point check, as every roadmap vertex does, lands in a region with probability equal to the region's share of the volume of $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}}$. A passage that is narrow in one or more directions has a small volume, so it receives few samples, even though every path must go through it. The plane map makes this measurable: the door is 0.8 m deep (the wall's thickness), so a door of width $w$ occupies $0.8\,w$ m² of a free area of about 71 m². With $w = 1.0$ m, a uniform sample lands in the door with probability about 1.1%; with $w = 0.125$ m, about 0.14%. In $d$ dimensions the effect compounds, because a passage that is narrow in several directions has a volume that shrinks as the product of its widths.

The program `exp_narrow.py` narrows the door while keeping everything else fixed. It runs a PRM with $n = 300$ and $k = 10$ for 20 seeds, and RRT-Connect for 10 seeds:

```text
PRM n=300 k=10, 20 seeds: solved, median point checks
door 1.000 m: uniform 19/20 (28753 checks)   hybrid 18/20 (418991 checks)
door 0.500 m: uniform 14/20 (28740 checks)   hybrid 18/20 (277642 checks)
door 0.250 m: uniform  9/20 (28690 checks)   hybrid 18/20 (240839 checks)
door 0.125 m: uniform  2/20 (28715 checks)   hybrid 16/20 (259604 checks)
RRT-Connect eta=0.5, 10 seeds: iterations to solve, median [min, max]
door 1.000 m: median    94.0 [39, 227]
door 0.500 m: median   114.5 [41, 1440]
door 0.250 m: median   321.5 [43, 1440]
door 0.125 m: median   518.5 [48, 3277]
```

With uniform sampling, the roadmap's success rate fell from 19 of 20 to 2 of 20 as the door narrowed from 1.0 m to 0.125 m, with the same number of checks. RRT-Connect solved every run within its limit of 20,000 iterations, but its median number of iterations grew more than fivefold and its worst run more than fourteenfold. The tree has to wait for a sample whose direction, seen from a vertex near the door, threads the opening.

Section 3.6 described Hsu, Latombe and Motwani's expansiveness as the quantity that governs the success rate of a roadmap. A narrow passage is the typical cause of poor expansiveness: from inside one room, only a small part of the other is visible through the door. The guarantee of probabilistic completeness still holds, but the number of samples needed to make failure unlikely can be impractically large.

### 5.2 Sampling where it matters

Since uniform sampling wastes samples in open space, several strategies concentrate samples near obstacles or inside passages. Two influential ones use only point checks:

- **Gaussian sampling** (Boor, Overmars and van der Stappen, 1999) draws a configuration $q_1$ uniformly and a second configuration $q_2$ from a normal distribution centered on $q_1$; if exactly one of the two is free, it keeps the free one. The samples cluster near the boundary of $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{obs}}$, where passages are.
- The **bridge test** (Hsu, Jiang, Reif and Sun, 2003) draws $q_1$ and a nearby $q_2$ as above, and keeps their midpoint only if both $q_1$ and $q_2$ are in collision and the midpoint is free. Such a "bridge" spans a gap between two obstacles, so its midpoint tends to lie in a narrow passage. Because it rejects most candidates, the authors combined it with uniform sampling in a hybrid strategy.

The "hybrid" column above draws half the samples uniformly and half by the bridge test with a standard deviation of 0.5 m. It solved 16 to 18 of 20 queries at every door width, including 16 of 20 at 0.125 m, where uniform sampling solved 2. It paid with collision checks: most bridge candidates are rejected, and the median run used 8 to 15 times as many point checks as uniform sampling. At the widest door the hybrid did slightly worse than uniform sampling (18 against 19 of 20), probably because it had only 150 uniform samples to cover the open rooms. Whether that trade pays depends on how expensive a point check is compared with an edge check and on how narrow the passages of the actual problem are, so such strategies are tuned per application rather than chosen once.

### 5.3 Other failure modes

Narrow passages are the best-known failure, but not the only one.

- **The distance function.** Nearest neighbors, steering and connection radii all depend on the metric on $\mathcal{C}$. On the arm's torus, a metric that ignored the wrap-around would see $\theta_1 = 3.1$ and $\theta_1 = -3.1$ as 6.2 rad apart instead of 0.08 rad, and would miss short paths. For a robot that translates and rotates, the metric must weigh meters against radians, and a poor weighting makes the nearest vertex a poor choice for extension.
- **Checking resolution.** As Section 1.2 noted, an edge checked at resolution $\delta$ can pass through an obstacle thinner than $\delta$ in configuration space. For the arm, a joint-space step of 0.02 rad moves the tip of the arm by at most $0.02 \times \sqrt{1.7^2 + 0.7^2} = 0.037$ m between checks (1.7 m is the lever arm of the shoulder, 0.7 m that of the elbow). A thinner obstacle can slip between two checks unnoticed.
- **Differential constraints.** The planners here join configurations by straight segments, which assumes the robot can move in any direction of $\mathcal{C}$. A car-like robot cannot move sideways, so a straight segment in $(x, y, \theta)$ is not a motion it can execute. LaValle and Kuffner's 2001 paper adapted RRT to such systems by growing the tree with applied controls instead of straight segments; the topic is beyond this entry.

## 6. Shortening and smoothing paths

### 6.1 Shortcutting

The paths of PRM and RRT are feasible but wasteful, and a cheap post-processing step recovers much of the waste. **Random shortcutting** repeats a simple move: pick two points at random along the path, which may lie in the middle of segments. If the straight segment between them is free, it replaces the part of the path between them. No successful move lengthens the path, by the triangle inequality, and a move shortens it unless the replaced piece is already straight; the result stays collision-free up to the checker's resolution, because every new segment was checked. The program uses 100 such attempts on each RRT-Connect path of the arm problem (the seed of the shortcut generator is the planner's seed plus 100):

```text
RRT-Connect eta=0.3 rad: solved 10/10
  ...
  cost           median      6.47   min      5.98   max      8.39
  shortcut_cost  median      4.87   min      4.79   max      6.23
  shortcut_checks median   3371.00   min   2373.00   max   4188.00
```

The median path length fell from 6.47 rad to 4.87 rad, a reduction of 25%, at the cost of about 3,400 extra point checks, more than the planner itself used. For comparison, `ref_arm.py` ran a $k$-nearest PRM* with 3000 samples on the same problem for seeds 0 to 2 and shortcut each result 300 times; the best path found was 4.671 rad. That number is an upper bound on $C^*$ for the arm problem, not the optimum itself, but it shows that RRT-Connect followed by 100 shortcuts came within about 4% of it in the median.

Shortcutting cannot do everything. It is a local improvement: it straightens the route the path already takes, and it cannot find a different route that the path never comes near. Two paths that can be deformed into each other without crossing an obstacle belong to the same **homotopy class**, and a shortcut changes the class only when an obstacle happens to lie between a piece of the path and the free segment that replaces it.

The program `probe_shortcut.py` shows the effect on the arm. Nine of the ten shortcut paths bend the elbow to a positive angle and end with lengths from 4.79 to 5.35 rad. Within them two routes appear, one turning the shoulder forward by a net 2.70 rad and one turning it backward by 3.58 rad, with similar lengths. The tenth path, from seed 8, bends the elbow to a negative angle, past the other end of the bands, and stays at 6.23 rad after shortcutting, a sixth longer than the next longest.

Shortcuts also pull the path tight against obstacles, leaving no **clearance**, the distance between the robot and the nearest obstacle. Geraerts and Overmars addressed both limits: they proposed a partial shortcut that interpolates one degree of freedom at a time, which can shorten paths that an ordinary shortcut cannot, and methods that increase clearance after shortening.

### 6.2 Smoothing

A shortened path is still a polyline with corners. At a corner the direction of motion changes instantly, so the velocity would have to drop to zero there, or the acceleration would be unbounded. The remedy, **smoothing**, replaces the polyline with a curve that has continuous derivatives, for example by fitting a spline through its vertices or by rounding each corner with an arc. Any smoothing moves the path away from the checked segments, so the smoothed curve must be checked again, and where it collides the smoothing is relaxed locally. Geometric smoothing also says nothing about how fast the robot moves along the curve; that is the job of a time scaling.

## 7. Exercises

**Exercise 1.** For the plane map ($\mu(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}}) = 71.80$ m²), compute the PRM* radius threshold and the radius for $n = 2000$. Then compute the $k$-nearest value $k(2000)$ with $k_{\mathrm{PRM}} = 2e$. Compare with the output in Section 4.4.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The threshold is $2 \times (3/2)^{1/2} \times (71.80/\pi)^{1/2} = 2 \times 1.2247 \times 4.7807 = 11.71$ m. For $n = 2000$, $\log 2000 / 2000 = 7.601/2000 = 0.0038005$, whose square root is 0.061648, so $r_{2000} = 11.71 \times 0.061648 = 0.722$ m, as printed. $k(2000) = \lceil 2e \times 7.601 \rceil = \lceil 41.32 \rceil = 42$, also as printed.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Suppose RRT's goal bias is set to 1, so that every iteration steers toward $q_{\mathrm{goal}}$. What happens on the plane map, and what does this say about the role of the random samples?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The nearest vertex to the goal is always chosen, and the tree grows in a straight line from the start toward $(9, 9)$ until the first obstacle on that line, the box at $x$ from 2.0 m to 3.5 m. From then on every iteration selects the same vertex, steers into the box and fails the edge check, so the planner spins without progress until its iteration limit. The random samples supply the exploration (Section 3.2); a goal bias is useful only as a small perturbation of them.

</details>

**Exercise 3.** For the plane map with a door of width $w = 0.125$ m, estimate the expected number of uniform samples that land inside the door (0.8 m deep) when $n = 300$. Uniform PRM still solved 2 of 20 queries with this door. How can a roadmap pass through the door with no vertex in it?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The obstacles cover $21.0 + 0.8 \times (10 - 0.125) = 28.9$ m², so the free area is 71.1 m², and the door occupies $0.8 \times 0.125 = 0.1$ m². The probability per sample is $0.1/71.1 = 0.0014$, and the expected number in 300 samples is 0.42. A roadmap does not need a vertex inside the door: an edge between a vertex just left of the wall and one just right of it can pass through the opening if both lie nearly in line with the door. Such pairs are rare, which is why the success rate is low.

</details>

**Exercise 4.** A colleague runs RRT on a new problem with a limit of 50,000 iterations, and it fails. They conclude that no path exists. What can be concluded, and what are sensible next steps?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Nothing can be concluded about existence: probabilistic completeness (Section 1.3) says only that the probability of success tends to 1, at a problem-dependent rate. The failure is consistent with a narrow passage or with infeasibility. Useful next steps are to run several seeds, try RRT-Connect and add a sampling strategy for narrow passages (Section 5.2). The distance function and the checking resolution should be checked against the problem (Section 5.3). If infeasibility is suspected, it needs other evidence, such as a check that the start and goal are free and in reach. A search on a coarse grid also counts, provided its cells are marked blocked only when every configuration in them is in collision; a failure of that search proves that no path exists.

</details>

**Exercise 5.** Why can random shortcutting never produce a path that collides, apart from the checker's resolution? Can it move a path to the other side of an obstacle?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Every segment that shortcutting inserts has passed an edge check, and the rest of the path is unchanged, so the new path is as collision-free as the checks are. It can move the path to the other side of an obstacle, but only of one that lies between the replaced piece and the new segment, such as a small obstacle inside a wide detour. It cannot move the path across a large obstacle that the path goes around as a whole: any segment that would cross it is blocked, so the route around the far side is never tried. This is the behavior of seed 8 in Section 6.1, which stayed on its own side of the obstacle bands.

</details>

## 8. Summary

| Concept | Result | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Sampling instead of a grid | The collision checker is the only geometry; cost scales with the checks needed, not with $m^d$ cells | Collision-free only up to the checking resolution |
| Probabilistic completeness | Probability of finding an existing path tends to 1 as $n \to \infty$ | No rate in general; cannot prove that no path exists |
| PRM | Multi-query roadmap; plane map, $n = 1000$, $k = 10$: about 55,000 checks to build, about 150 per query | Zigzag paths, 7% above $C^*$ in the median; narrow passages |
| RRT and RRT-Connect | Single-query trees with a Voronoi bias; plane map: median 2,456 and 1,159 checks | Paths 30% to 40% above $C^*$; do not improve with more samples |
| PRM* and RRT* | $r_n = \gamma (\log n / n)^{1/d}$ or $k = k_{\mathrm{PRM}} \log n$; asymptotically optimal; RRT* reached 1.3% above $C^*$ after 4000 iterations | A limit without a rate; constants need $\mu(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}})$; RRT* proof needed a revised radius |
| Narrow passages | Uniform PRM success fell from 19 to 2 of 20 as the door narrowed eightfold | Gaussian and bridge sampling help at a cost in checks |
| Shortcutting | Arm paths shortened by 25% in the median with 100 attempts | Stays in one homotopy class; leaves no clearance |

Sampling-based planners trade the grid's exhaustive coverage for random coverage that is cheap where the free space is open. The trade works because a collision checker is all they need, so the same code plans for a point in a room and for an arm on a torus, and in the examples here the trees found a path with a few thousand checks. The guarantees are weaker than a grid's and need careful reading: completeness only in probability and optimality only in the limit, both slowed by narrow passages. Every planner of this entry returns a geometric path, a sequence of configurations with no notion of time, and even a shortened path has corners that no real actuator can turn instantly. The next entry, [From Paths to Trajectories: Time Scaling and Velocity Profiles](/blog/trajectories-and-time-scaling), adds time: it turns such a path into a trajectory with bounded velocity and acceleration that a robot can follow.
