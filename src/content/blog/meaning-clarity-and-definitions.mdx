The [first entry](/blog/what-is-critical-thinking) of this series proposed a six-step method for judging a claim, and its first step was to clarify what the claim says. That step comes first for a practical reason: one cannot decide whether a claim is true, or whether an argument supports it, until one knows which claim is meant. Two engineers who disagree about whether a service is "reliable" may be disagreeing about the service, or only about the word. Until that is settled, more evidence will not help them.

This entry provides the tools for the clarification step. Section 1 separates what a sentence literally says from what it suggests. Sections 2 and 3 cover definitions: what kinds there are, how they are built and how to tell a good one from a bad one. Section 4 distinguishes disputes about words from disputes about facts. Section 5 introduces necessary and sufficient conditions, the most useful single tool for stating precisely how two ideas are related. Sections 6 and 7 catalog the ways in which language goes wrong: ambiguity, vagueness, incomplete meaning, distortion and statements with no content. The logical symbols used here ($\to$ for "if ... then" and $\neg$ for "not") are introduced informally and treated properly in the sixth entry, [Sentential Logic](/blog/sentential-logic).

## 1. Why meaning comes first

### 1.1 Claims and their truth conditions

A **statement**, or claim, is what a declarative sentence asserts: something that is either true or false. Questions, commands and exclamations assert nothing of the kind, so they make no statement. To understand a statement is, at a minimum, to know its **truth conditions**: what the world would have to be like for it to be true. "The test suite passes on the main branch" has clear truth conditions; one can run the suite and see. "The codebase is in good shape" does not, until one says which properties of the code count.

A useful habit follows directly: before asking whether a claim is true, ask what observation would show it to be false. If no observation could, either the claim is not about the world at all (Section 7.4) or its meaning has not yet been fixed.

### 1.2 Literal meaning and what is implied

What a sentence literally says and what a speaker conveys by saying it can differ. Suppose a reviewer is asked whether a pull request is ready to merge and replies, "The commit messages are well formatted." Literally, the reply is about commit messages and says nothing about the code. Yet any colleague would hear it as a hint that the code is not ready, because a cooperative speaker who had something good to say about the code would have said it.

The philosopher Paul Grice called this kind of conveyed meaning a **conversational implicature**: content that a listener infers from the assumption that the speaker is being cooperative, informative and relevant, rather than from the words alone (Grice, 1975). Implicatures matter to critical thinking in two ways.

- **They can be canceled.** "Some of the integration tests failed" implies that not all of them did, but the speaker can add "in fact, all of them did" without contradiction. Literal content cannot be canceled this way. When a conclusion depends on an implicature, it rests on weaker ground than the words suggest.
- **They allow deniable suggestion.** An advertisement that says "No battery lasts longer than ours" literally permits every competitor's battery to last exactly as long. Separating what was said from what was suggested is often the first move in evaluating such claims.

## 2. Definitions

### 2.1 Definiendum and definiens

A **definition** explains the meaning of a term. It has two parts: the **definiendum**, the term being defined, and the **definiens**, the expression that gives its meaning. In "a prime number is an integer greater than 1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and itself", the definiendum is "prime number" and the definiens is everything after "is".

### 2.2 Four purposes of a definition

Definitions serve different purposes, and the purpose decides how a definition should be judged. Logic textbooks classify definitions by purpose in slightly different ways; Hurley, for instance, also separates out theoretical definitions, which define a term through a scientific theory, such as defining a gene by its role in heredity and its molecular structure (Hurley, 2015). Four purposes matter most for everyday and engineering reasoning.

A **reportive definition**, also called a lexical definition, reports how a term is already used by some community of speakers. The definition of "prime number" above reports the usage of mathematicians. A reportive definition is true or false: it can misdescribe the usage it claims to report. General dictionaries offer reportive definitions, but they are written for everyday use and limited space, so they are a starting point rather than an authority, especially for technical terms. For "latency" or "eigenvalue", a specialist reference reports the relevant usage far better than a general dictionary.

A **stipulative definition** is a decision rather than a report: the author announces how a term will be used from now on. The term may be newly coined or may be an existing word put to a new job. A paper that writes "we call an input *adversarial* if it is within distance $\varepsilon$ of a correctly classified input but is itself misclassified" is stipulating. A stipulative definition cannot be false, since it reports nothing, but it can be useless, confusing or inconsistent with itself.

A **precising definition** starts from an existing term whose meaning is too vague for some purpose and draws a sharp boundary within the range of ordinary usage. An operations team might define an **outage** as "any period of at least 5 consecutive minutes in which more than 5% of requests fail". No one thinks this is what "outage" has always meant; the point is that a contract, an alert or a report needs a boundary that everyone applies the same way. A precising definition is judged by two standards: whether it stays within ordinary usage, so that it does not change the subject, and whether its boundary serves the purpose for which it was drawn.

A **persuasive definition** attaches a favorable or unfavorable attitude to a term under the guise of explaining it. "Technical debt is the cost of a team's laziness" and "open-source software is software that nobody is accountable for" are persuasive definitions. They do not clarify a meaning; they smuggle a contestable judgment into a premise where it is less likely to be examined. Section 7.1 returns to this pattern.

### 2.3 How definitions are given

A definiens can be built in several ways, with different strengths.

- **By genus and difference.** Name a wider class and the feature that marks off the term within it: "a *watchdog timer* is a timer (the genus) that resets or halts the system if software fails to restart it within a set period (the difference)." This is the classic form and the easiest to check.
- **By example.** List or point at instances: "sorting algorithms such as quicksort and mergesort". Examples are indispensable for learning a term, but a list does not say which further cases belong.
- **By operation.** Specify a procedure and its outcome: "the *hardness* of the sample is the depth of the indentation left by a specified indenter under a specified load." The physicist Percy Bridgman argued that scientific concepts should be defined by the operations used to measure them (Bridgman, 1927). Operational definitions are the norm in engineering specifications because they make a claim testable. Their weakness is that two different procedures define two different concepts, even when the same word is used for both.

## 3. Evaluating definitions

### 3.1 Too wide and too narrow

A reportive or precising definition should apply to exactly the things that the term applies to. It can fail in two directions.

- **Too wide** means the definition lets in cases that the term excludes. "A robot is a machine controlled by a computer" admits a laser printer and a dishwasher, and few people would call them robots.
- **Too narrow** means the definition shuts out cases that the term covers. "A robot is a machine that walks" shuts out industrial arms and wheeled warehouse robots.
- A definition can be both at once. "A robot is a computer-controlled machine with a humanoid shape" excludes the industrial arm (too narrow) and includes a computer-controlled mannequin in a shop window (too wide).

Each of these judgments is supported by a **counterexample**: a single case that fits one side of the definition and not the other. Testing a definition means searching for counterexamples in both directions.

### 3.2 Other defects

Beyond width, a definition can fail in ways that apply to every purpose, stipulative ones included.

- **Circularity.** "Software reliability is the degree to which software is reliable" uses the definiendum in the definiens. A subtler circle passes through a second term: defining a *heartbeat message* as "a message sent by a live node" and a *live node* as "a node that is sending heartbeat messages".
- **Obscurity.** The definiens is harder to understand than the definiendum. "A bug is a non-conformance between the realized and the intended behavioral manifold of an artifact" explains nothing to someone who did not know what a bug was.
- **Inconsistency.** The definiens contains conditions that cannot all hold, so nothing satisfies it.

### 3.3 Worked example: what counts as downtime

Definitions carry real consequences in contracts and specifications. Consider a service-level agreement (SLA) that promises 99.9% monthly availability for a hosted service, where availability is $1 - D/T$ with $D$ the minutes of downtime and $T$ the minutes in the month. For a 30-day month, $T = 30 \times 24 \times 60 = 43\,200$ minutes, so the promise allows $0.001 \times 43\,200 = 43.2$ minutes of downtime.

Suppose a month contains these incidents (invented for the example):

- one 40-minute period in which every request failed;
- three 15-minute periods in which about 20% of requests failed;
- twelve isolated 1-minute periods in which about 10% of requests failed.

Now compare two definitions of downtime:

| | Definition A | Definition B |
|---|---|---|
| A minute counts as down if | more than 5% of requests fail in it | it lies in a run of at least 5 consecutive minutes in which more than 50% of requests fail |
| Downtime $D$ | $40 + 3 \times 15 + 12 = 97$ min | $40$ min |
| Availability $1 - D/T$ | 99.775% | 99.907% |
| SLA of 99.9% met? | No | Yes |

The incidents are the same; only the definition differs, and with it the answer to whether the provider owes compensation. Neither definition is wrong. Definition A reflects the customer's experience of partial failures; Definition B reflects a provider's view that brief or partial degradation is not an outage. A reader who sees only the headline "99.9% availability" has not yet learned what was promised.

## 4. Verbal and factual disputes

### 4.1 Two kinds of disagreement

A **factual dispute** is a disagreement about how the world is: whether a bug is in the driver or the firmware, or whether a material's yield strength exceeds a given value. A **verbal dispute** is a disagreement that arises because the parties use a key term in different senses, so that each may be right on their own reading.

Consider two engineers arguing whether a database change made queries faster. One points out that the median query time fell from 120 ms to 80 ms. The other points out that the 99th-percentile time rose from 400 ms to 650 ms. (The figures are invented for the example.) If the facts are as both report, there is nothing left to discover about the database. They disagree because "faster" means "lower median latency" to one and "lower tail latency" to the other. Their dispute is verbal.

### 4.2 Resolving a verbal dispute

Exposing the two senses usually ends the argument about the database, but it may leave a decision to make. If nothing hangs on the word, the facts can be stated in both senses at once: the change improved median latency and worsened tail latency. If something does hang on it, such as whether to keep the change, the question becomes which sense of "faster" the users of the system care about, and that is a question about the system's purpose, not about the word. If the queries serve an interactive page whose load time is set by the slowest of many parallel requests, tail latency is the relevant sense, and the change made things worse for that purpose.

Many disputes are partly verbal and partly factual. Separating the two parts turns a contest of wills into a question of definitions, which can be agreed, and a question of facts, which can be investigated.

## 5. Necessary and sufficient conditions

### 5.1 Definitions

Necessary and sufficient conditions state precisely how the presence of one thing is related to the presence of another.

- $X$ is a **sufficient condition** for $Y$ when every case of $X$ is a case of $Y$. In the notation of later entries, $X \to Y$ ("if $X$ then $Y$").
- $X$ is a **necessary condition** for $Y$ when every case of $Y$ is a case of $X$. In symbols, $Y \to X$, or equivalently $\neg X \to \neg Y$: wherever $X$ is missing, so is $Y$.

The two notions are mirror images. If $X$ is sufficient for $Y$, then $Y$ is necessary for $X$. Being a multiple of 6 is sufficient for being even; therefore being even is necessary for being a multiple of 6.

### 5.2 Four possible relationships

For any two conditions, exactly one of four relationships holds.

| Relationship of $X$ to $Y$ | Example ($X$; $Y$) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Necessary, not sufficient | Being divisible by 2; being divisible by 6 | Every multiple of 6 is even, but 4 is even and not a multiple of 6. |
| Sufficient, not necessary | Being divisible by 6; being even | Every multiple of 6 is even, but 4 is even without being a multiple of 6. |
| Necessary and sufficient | Having an even last decimal digit; being an even integer | Each guarantees the other. |
| Neither | Being prime; being odd | 2 is prime and not odd; 9 is odd and not prime. |

A condition that is both necessary and sufficient for a term is what a good definition by genus and difference supplies. The test for each relationship is a counterexample of a specific shape: to show that $X$ is **not sufficient** for $Y$, find a case with $X$ but without $Y$; to show that $X$ is **not necessary** for $Y$, find a case with $Y$ but without $X$.

### 5.3 Reading conditionals in ordinary language

Many errors in reading requirements come from misplacing the necessary condition. The following translations are standard.

| English | Condition | Symbolic form |
|---|---|---|
| If $A$, then $B$ | $A$ is sufficient for $B$ | $A \to B$ |
| $A$ only if $B$ | $B$ is necessary for $A$ | $A \to B$ |
| $A$ if and only if $B$ | each is necessary and sufficient for the other | $A \leftrightarrow B$ |
| $A$ unless $B$ | if not $B$, then $A$ | $\neg B \to A$ |
| $B$ is required for $A$ | $B$ is necessary for $A$ | $A \to B$ |

"A release may ship only if it passes the security review" makes the review necessary, not sufficient: passing it does not by itself authorize the release. Reading it as "if it passes the review, it may ship" is a common and consequential mistake.

### 5.4 Necessary according to what?

Claims of necessity are often made with words like "cannot" and "must", and the question to ask of each is what forbids the alternative. Four engineering-flavored claims show how different the answers can be.

| Claim | What forbids the alternative | Could that change? |
|---|---|---|
| "This pull request cannot be merged without an approving review." | A rule: the repository's protection settings | Yes, by anyone allowed to change the settings |
| "This 2 TB data set cannot be loaded into memory on that laptop." | The available means: the laptop has 16 GB of memory | Yes, with different hardware |
| "No signal can carry information faster than light." | A law of nature, as far as physics knows | Not by any engineering effort |
| "A process that has crashed must have been running at some point." | Meaning: crashing is something only a running process can do | No; denying it is self-contradictory |

Philosophers call the last kind **logical** (or conceptual) necessity and the third **physical** or nomological necessity; the first two are often grouped as practical or normative. The labels matter less than the habit of asking. "This service cannot lose data" may rest on the design, which is a strong guarantee, or only on a policy that forbids deleting data, which is a much weaker one.

## 6. Obscurity: ambiguity, vagueness and incomplete meaning

A claim can be unclear in three distinct ways, and each calls for a different remedy.

### 6.1 Ambiguity

An expression is **ambiguous** if it has more than one distinct meaning and the context does not settle which is intended. Ambiguity comes in several kinds.

- **Lexical ambiguity**: a single word has several meanings. "Release" can mean a published version of software or the act of freeing a lock; "table" can mean a database relation or a piece of furniture; "significant" can mean important or statistically significant, and confusing the two is a classic error in reading research.
- **Referential ambiguity**: it is unclear what a word refers to. In "the controller sent the command to the actuator, and then it failed", "it" may be either device. "Everyone has approved the design" leaves open who "everyone" is.
- **Syntactic ambiguity**: the grammar allows more than one structure. "The script deletes log files older than 30 days and backups" may delete all backups or only old ones. "We tested the robot with the camera" may describe the robot's camera or the test equipment.
- **Scope ambiguity**: words such as "all", "some" and "not" can combine in more than one order. "All the nodes did not respond" may mean that no node responded or that not every node did. The seventh entry, [Predicate Logic](/blog/predicate-logic), gives a notation in which the two readings look different.

The remedy for ambiguity is **disambiguation**: list the possible readings and either determine which one the speaker intended or evaluate the claim under each reading separately.

### 6.2 Vagueness

A term is **vague** if it has borderline cases: cases in which it is unclear whether the term applies, even when all the relevant facts are known. "Large file", "high CPU load", "tall" and "old" are vague. One may know that a file is 80 MB and still be unable to say whether it is large, because the meaning of "large" does not draw a line.

Vagueness and ambiguity are different. "Bank" is ambiguous but each of its meanings is fairly sharp; "large" has one meaning, but that meaning is blurred at the edges. A term can be both.

Vagueness is often harmless and sometimes useful: "come early" serves well when no one needs a precise time. It becomes a problem when a claim must be tested, enforced or compared. "The new design significantly reduces power consumption" cannot be checked until one says how much reduction counts.

The remedy for harmful vagueness is a precising definition (Section 2.2): replace "high CPU load" by "utilization above 85% averaged over 5 minutes". The threshold is chosen, not discovered, and a slightly different threshold would often serve as well. That is not a defect; it is what drawing a boundary on a continuum involves.

A bad argument exploits vagueness: "there is no sharp line between X and Y, so there is no real difference between them." There is no sharp line between a light load and a heavy one, but a server at 5% utilization and one at 99% are in different situations. A distinction with borderline cases can still be real, and it can still have clear cases on each side. The ancient sorites paradox asks which single grain, removed from a heap one at a time, turns it into a non-heap. It shows how hard vagueness is to model exactly (Williamson, 1994); it does not show that heaps do not exist.

### 6.3 Incomplete meaning

Some terms express a relation but are used as if they expressed a property. Their meaning is **incomplete** until a missing parameter is supplied. Typical examples are "better", "faster", "similar", "efficient", "secure", "important" and "useful".

- "Rust is safer than C" is incomplete until one says safer with respect to what: memory errors, logic errors or supply-chain attacks.
- "This system is secure" is incomplete without a threat model: secure against whom, with what access, trying to do what.
- "Our product is similar to theirs" says nothing until one names the respects. Any two things are similar in some respects and different in others.

The remedy is to ask for the missing parameter. A claim that cannot supply it is not yet a claim that can be evaluated.

## 7. Distortion and empty content

### 7.1 Loaded language and persuasive definitions

Many words carry an attitude as well as a description. "Refactor" and "rewrite", "deprecated" and "abandoned", "bold" and "reckless" can describe the same facts while inviting different reactions. Using such words is not wrong, but an argument that relies on their connotation, rather than on the facts they describe, has not yet made its case. The persuasive definitions of Section 2.2 are the extreme form: they build a verdict into a meaning.

A useful test is to restate the claim in neutral language and see whether the argument still works. "We should not adopt this framework, since it is a fad" becomes "... since it has become popular recently", which is visibly weaker: recent popularity is not by itself a reason against anything.

### 7.2 Shifting meanings under pressure

A claim can be rescued from a counterexample by quietly narrowing one of its words. The exchange below shows the move.

> A: Our framework has no performance problems.
>
> B: This benchmark shows a threefold slowdown against the alternative.
>
> A: That benchmark is not a realistic workload.
>
> B: It is taken from our largest customer's production traces.
>
> A: Well, no workload that a well-designed application would generate.

Each reply narrows the meaning of "workload" until the original claim can no longer be tested: in the end it says only that the framework performs well on the workloads where it performs well, which is safe because it is empty. The defense against this move is to fix the meaning of the key terms at the start and to notice when a counterexample is answered by redefinition rather than by evidence.

### 7.3 Reification and category mistakes

**Reification** is treating an abstraction as if it were a concrete thing with location, will or agency. "The data says we should cut the feature" and "the market punished the company" are reifications. As figures of speech they are often harmless, and they can be vivid. They become a problem when they hide the actual agents and inferences. Data does not say anything. Someone analyzed it under certain assumptions and drew a conclusion that can be questioned.

Gilbert Ryle introduced the term **category mistake** for a confusion about what sort of thing something is (Ryle, 1949). The mistake is to file an item under the wrong logical type and then ask of it questions that only make sense for the other type. Imagine a visitor who is shown the processes, the scheduler, the file system and the device drivers and then asks, "But where is the operating system?" The visitor has made a category mistake: the operating system is not one more component alongside these but the organized whole they make up. Asking what color an algorithm is, or how much a proof weighs, is the same error in a cruder form.

### 7.4 Empty statements

A statement is **empty** in a context if it appears to give information but gives none that bears on the question at hand.

- **Tautologies** are true by their logical form alone: "the deployment will either succeed or fail", "if the build passes, it passes". They cannot be false, and for that reason they say nothing about the deployment or the build. The sixth entry gives tautology a precise definition.
- **Analytic statements** are true in virtue of the meanings of their words: "a redundant system has more than one component able to perform a function". They inform someone learning the words but are empty as answers to a factual question.
- **Unfalsifiable hedges** are compatible with every outcome: "the migration may cause some issues", "market conditions could affect results". Such a claim cannot be wrong, but it also gives no guidance.

A related phenomenon is language that sounds profound but has little content: strings of abstract buzzwords that are grammatical and suggestive but do not commit the speaker to anything checkable. Pennycook and colleagues found that how readily people rate such statements as profound is negatively related to measures of analytic thinking (Pennycook et al., 2015). The practical test is the one from Section 1.1: ask what the world would have to be like for the statement to be false.

## 8. Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Classify each definition as reportive, stipulative, precising or persuasive. (a) "In this paper, a *stable matching* is one in which no two agents prefer each other to their assigned partners." (b) "A *mammal* is a vertebrate animal that nurses its young with milk." (c) "For the purposes of this warranty, *water damage* means damage caused by immersion in liquid to a depth of more than 1 m." (d) "*Bureaucracy* is the organized prevention of useful work."

*Answer.* (a) Stipulative (the term is fixed for the paper; in fact it matches established usage, but the author is setting it). (b) Reportive. (c) Precising: it draws a sharp boundary within the ordinary meaning of "water damage" for a specific purpose. (d) Persuasive.

**Exercise 2.** Evaluate as a reportive definition: "A *sensor* is a device that produces an electrical signal." Is it too wide, too narrow or both? Give counterexamples.

*Answer.* Both. Too wide: a battery and a signal generator produce electrical signals but are not sensors. Too narrow: a liquid-in-glass thermometer and a bimetallic strip are sensors that produce no electrical signal. A better definition mentions what is essential: a device that responds to a physical quantity with an output that depends on that quantity.

**Exercise 3.** State the relationship (necessary, sufficient, both or neither) of the first condition to the second, with a counterexample where relevant. (a) Having a valid password; being logged in to an account protected by password and second factor. (b) Being divisible by 9; being divisible by 3. (c) Being a square; being a rectangle with four equal sides. (d) Compiling without errors; producing correct output when run.

*Answer.* (a) Necessary, not sufficient: without the password one cannot log in, but the second factor is also needed. (b) Sufficient, not necessary: 6 is divisible by 3 but not by 9. (c) Necessary and sufficient. (d) Necessary, not sufficient: a program that does not compile cannot be run at all, but a program that compiles may still compute the wrong result.

**Exercise 4.** Rewrite each sentence as "$X$ is necessary for $Y$" or "$X$ is sufficient for $Y$". (a) "The robot moves only if the safety interlock is closed." (b) "If the checksum fails, the packet is discarded." (c) "The job will run tonight unless the cluster is down."

*Answer.* (a) The interlock being closed is necessary for the robot to move. (b) A failed checksum is sufficient for the packet to be discarded. (c) The cluster not being down is sufficient for the job to run tonight ($\neg \text{down} \to \text{run}$). On the standard reading, "unless" does not promise that the job will not run if the cluster is down.

**Exercise 5.** Identify the kind of ambiguity in each sentence and give two readings. (a) "The engineer told the technician that she had made a mistake." (b) "Every sensor did not report a fault." (c) "We need more experienced developers."

*Answer.* (a) Referential: "she" may be the engineer or the technician. (b) Scope: no sensor reported a fault, or not every sensor reported one. (c) Syntactic: more developers who are experienced, or developers who are more experienced than the current ones.

**Exercise 6.** A vendor claims that its storage system is "highly reliable and much faster than traditional solutions". Rewrite the claim so that it could be tested, and say which kinds of unclarity your rewrite removes.

*Answer.* One possible rewrite: "Over 12 months of operation at the stated load, the system lost no committed writes, and its 99th-percentile write latency was below half that of [named product] on [named benchmark]." The rewrite removes vagueness ("highly", "much") by stating thresholds. It completes the incomplete terms by saying in what sense the system is reliable and faster than what, on which workload. It also replaces the loaded word "traditional" with a named comparison.

## 9. Summary

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Why clarify meaning first? | A claim can be evaluated only once its truth conditions are known. |
| What is an implicature? | Content conveyed by the assumption that a speaker is cooperative, which can be canceled without contradiction. |
| What kinds of definitions are there? | Reportive (describes usage), stipulative (assigns a meaning), precising (sharpens a vague term) and persuasive (smuggles in an attitude). |
| How is a definition evaluated? | By counterexamples showing it too wide or too narrow, and by checking for circularity, obscurity and inconsistency. |
| What is a verbal dispute? | A disagreement caused by different senses of a key term; it is resolved by stating facts under each sense or choosing the sense the purpose requires. |
| What are necessary and sufficient conditions? | $X$ is sufficient for $Y$ if $X \to Y$; necessary if $Y \to X$. "$A$ only if $B$" makes $B$ necessary. |
| What are the forms of obscurity? | Ambiguity (several meanings), vagueness (borderline cases) and incomplete meaning (a missing parameter). |
| What are distortion and emptiness? | Loaded language, meanings shifted under pressure, reification, category mistakes and statements such as tautologies that convey no relevant information. |

With the meaning of a claim settled, the next question is what reasons are offered for it and how they are supposed to support it. The [next entry](/blog/arguments-and-their-structure) introduces arguments: how to find premises and conclusions in ordinary text, how to set them out and map them, how to reveal hidden assumptions, and how deductive validity differs from inductive strength.
