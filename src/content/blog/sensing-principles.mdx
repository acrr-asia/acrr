The previous entry treated the sensing element as the first box in the measurement chain: the place where a physical effect turns the measurand into an electrical quantity. This entry opens that box. There are thousands of sensor products, but only a handful of physical effects underlie them. Knowing those effects explains why a thermocouple needs a reference temperature, why a piezoelectric sensor cannot weigh a parcel, and why a pH electrode must be compensated for temperature.

The entry starts with a way of organising sensing principles, then takes each family in turn: what physical law it relies on, what that law predicts for a typical device, and what follows for using it. Each family also inherits the distinction made in the previous entry between **modulating** sensors, which change an electrical property and need excitation, and **self-generating** sensors, which produce a signal from the measurand's own energy.

## 1. Organising Sensing Principles

A useful way to classify sensors is by **energy domain**: the physical form in which the measurand presents itself. Six domains cover most cases:

- **mechanical**: position, displacement, force, pressure, strain, acceleration, flow;
- **thermal**: temperature and heat flow;
- **magnetic**: magnetic field, and anything that can be turned into one, such as current or position of a magnet;
- **radiant**: light and other electromagnetic radiation;
- **chemical**: concentration, composition, pH;
- **electrical**: voltage, current, charge.

A sensor converts a quantity from one of these domains into the electrical domain. Some conversions are direct; others pass through an intermediate domain, as when a pressure sensor turns pressure into the strain of a diaphragm (mechanical to mechanical) and then strain into resistance (mechanical to electrical).

The physical effects that make the final conversion fall into a few families:

| Family | Electrical output | Self-generating or modulating | Typical measurands |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resistive | Change of resistance | Modulating | Temperature, strain, position, light, gas |
| Capacitive | Change of capacitance | Modulating | Position, pressure, humidity, level, touch |
| Inductive and magnetic | Change of inductance, or a voltage | Mostly modulating | Position, proximity, speed, magnetic field, current |
| Piezoelectric | Charge | Self-generating | Dynamic force, pressure, vibration, acceleration |
| Thermoelectric | Voltage | Self-generating | Temperature difference |
| Optical (photoelectric) | Current or voltage | Self-generating (or modulating when biased) | Light intensity, and anything converted to light |
| Electrochemical | Voltage or current | Self-generating (potentiometric) or modulating (amperometric) | pH, ion and gas concentration |

## 2. Resistive Sensing

The resistance of a conductor of length $l$, cross-sectional area $A$ and resistivity $\rho$ is

$$
R = \rho\,\frac{l}{A}.
$$

Anything that changes $\rho$, $l$ or $A$ can be sensed as a change in resistance. Resistive sensors are modulating: an excitation current or voltage turns the change of resistance into a change of voltage, usually in a bridge circuit, which the next entry treats.

### 2.1 Temperature: metals and thermistors

The resistivity of metals rises with temperature. The previous entry used the platinum resistance thermometer as its example: a PT100 has $100\ \Omega$ at 0 °C and a sensitivity of about $0.39\ \Omega/^\circ\text{C}$ there, a relative change of 0.39 % per kelvin. Platinum is chosen for stability and a well-standardised curve, not for sensitivity.

A **thermistor** is a resistor made from a semiconducting metal oxide, whose resistance changes far more strongly with temperature. The common **NTC** (negative temperature coefficient) type is often described by the beta model

$$
R(T) = R_{25}\,\exp\!\left[\beta\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_{25}}\right)\right],
$$

with $T$ in kelvin, $R_{25}$ the resistance at $T_{25} = 298.15$ K (25 °C), and $\beta$ a material constant. For a thermistor with $R_{25} = 10\ \text{k}\Omega$ and $\beta = 3950$ K, the resistance is 33.6 kΩ at 0 °C, 3.59 kΩ at 50 °C and 0.70 kΩ at 100 °C. Its relative sensitivity at 25 °C is $-\beta/T^2 = -4.4\ \%$ per kelvin, more than ten times that of platinum and of opposite sign, at the cost of a strongly nonlinear curve, a narrower usable range and poorer long-term stability. Thermistors suit cheap, sensitive measurements over a limited range; platinum suits accurate measurements over a wide one.

### 2.2 Strain: the strain gauge

When a conductor is stretched, it becomes longer and thinner, and in many materials its resistivity changes too. A **strain gauge** is a thin metal-foil pattern bonded to a surface so that it stretches with it. **Strain** $\varepsilon$ is the relative change in length, $\Delta l / l$, a dimensionless number usually quoted in microstrain (µε, parts per million). The gauge's relative change of resistance is proportional to strain:

$$
\frac{\Delta R}{R} = K\,\varepsilon ,
$$

where $K$, the **gauge factor**, is about 2 for common metal-foil gauges. The effect is small: a 350 Ω gauge on a steel member strained by 1000 µε (0.1 %) changes by $2 \times 0.001 \times 350 = 0.7\ \Omega$, a 0.2 % change that must be distinguished from the effect of temperature on the same resistor. Strain gauges are the sensing element of most load cells, many pressure sensors and torque sensors; the bridge circuits that make them usable are the subject of the next entry.

### 2.3 Other resistive sensors

- A **potentiometer** measures position: a wiper moves along a resistive track, and the voltage at the wiper is proportional to its position. It is simple and cheap but wears because of sliding contact.
- A **photoresistor** (light-dependent resistor) lowers its resistance as light frees charge carriers in a semiconductor; it is slow and imprecise, suitable for detecting light levels rather than measuring them.
- **Metal-oxide gas sensors** change resistance when gases react at a heated oxide surface; they are sensitive but respond to many gases at once (poor selectivity, in the previous entry's terms).

## 3. Capacitive Sensing

A capacitor stores charge on two conductors separated by an insulator. For two parallel plates of area $A$ a distance $d$ apart,

$$
C = \frac{\varepsilon_0\,\varepsilon_r\,A}{d},
$$

where $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}$ F/m is the permittivity of free space and $\varepsilon_r$ the relative permittivity of the material between the plates. A measurand that changes the gap $d$, the overlapping area $A$, or the material between the plates ($\varepsilon_r$) can therefore be sensed as a change of capacitance:

- **gap**: pressure sensors and many microfabricated accelerometers, where a diaphragm or proof mass moves relative to a fixed electrode;
- **area**: rotary and linear position sensors, where electrodes slide past one another;
- **permittivity**: humidity sensors (a polymer absorbs water and its permittivity rises), liquid-level sensors and touch screens (a finger changes the field between electrodes).

The capacitances involved are small. Two plates of 10 mm × 10 mm, 0.1 mm apart in air, form $8.85$ pF; reducing the gap by 1 µm increases this by about 0.09 pF, or 1 %. Measuring such changes requires excitation with an alternating signal and careful shielding, because the cables and surroundings have stray capacitances of the same order. In return, capacitive sensors draw almost no power, have no moving contacts, and can be built directly into silicon, which is why they dominate microfabricated motion sensors.

## 4. Inductive and Magnetic Sensing

### 4.1 Inductive sensors

An inductor's inductance depends on its geometry and on the magnetic material near it. Moving a ferromagnetic core, or a conductive target, changes the inductance or the coupling between coils.

- A **linear variable differential transformer** (LVDT) has one primary coil and two secondary coils around a movable ferromagnetic core. An alternating current in the primary induces voltages in both secondaries; when the core is centred they are equal, and as it moves one rises and the other falls. Their difference is proportional to displacement, with a sign that gives direction. With no contact between core and coils, LVDTs are robust and practically wear-free.
- An **eddy-current** proximity sensor drives a coil at high frequency; a nearby conductive target carries induced (eddy) currents that change the coil's effective impedance, which indicates distance without contact.

### 4.2 Hall-effect sensors

When a current $I$ flows through a thin conducting strip of thickness $t$ in a magnetic field $B$ perpendicular to it, the moving charge carriers are deflected sideways, and a voltage appears across the strip:

$$
V_H = \frac{I\,B}{n\,q\,t},
$$

where $n$ is the density of charge carriers and $q$ the charge of each. Because $V_H$ is inversely proportional to $n$, the effect is useful in semiconductors, which have far fewer carriers than metals. **Hall-effect sensors** measure magnetic field, and through it the position of a magnet (in brushless-motor commutation, for instance), the speed of a toothed wheel, or electric current without breaking the circuit, by sensing the field around a conductor. They need an excitation current and are therefore modulating; their output also drifts with temperature and must be compensated.

**Magnetoresistive** sensors, whose resistance changes with magnetic field, serve similar purposes with higher sensitivity and are common in electronic compasses.

## 5. Piezoelectric Sensing

Some crystals and ceramics develop an electric charge on their surfaces when they are mechanically stressed: the **piezoelectric effect**. For force applied along a particular axis, the charge is proportional to force,

$$
Q = d\,F ,
$$

where $d$ is the piezoelectric coefficient for that axis. For quartz, the relevant coefficient is about 2.3 pC/N, so a force of 10 N produces 23 pC. On a total capacitance (sensor plus cable) of 100 pF, that charge gives a voltage of $Q/C = 0.23$ V. Piezoelectric ceramics such as lead zirconate titanate have coefficients one to two orders of magnitude larger, at the cost of more temperature dependence and drift.

Piezoelectric sensors are self-generating and very stiff, so they respond quickly and are well suited to dynamic force, pressure pulses, vibration and acceleration. Their defining limitation is that **they cannot measure static quantities**. The generated charge leaks away through the finite insulation resistance of the sensor, cable and amplifier. With 100 pF and 1 GΩ, the time constant is $RC = 0.1$ s, and after one second only $e^{-10} \approx 0.005\ \%$ of a constant force's signal remains. A piezoelectric load cell can measure an impact but not the weight of a parcel resting on it. Special charge amplifiers, discussed in the signal-conditioning entry, extend the low-frequency limit but cannot remove it.

The effect also works in reverse: a voltage applied to a piezoelectric element deforms it. That makes the same materials useful as actuators and as ultrasonic transmitters, as the previous entry noted for transducers in general.

## 6. Thermoelectric Sensing

When two different metals are joined at two junctions held at different temperatures, a voltage appears in the circuit: the **Seebeck effect**. A **thermocouple** is such a pair of wires. Its output depends on the temperature difference between the measuring junction (the hot junction) and the reference junction (the cold junction, where the wires connect to the measuring instrument):

$$
V \approx S\,(T_{\text{hot}} - T_{\text{ref}}),
$$

where $S$ is the Seebeck coefficient of the pair. For the common type K thermocouple (nickel–chromium against nickel–aluminium) near room temperature, $S \approx 41\ \mu\text{V/K}$, so a 100 K difference gives about 4.1 mV. Over wide ranges the relation is not linear, and standard reference tables or polynomials give the voltage for each thermocouple type.

Three consequences follow:

- A thermocouple measures a **difference**. To obtain an absolute temperature, the instrument must measure the reference-junction temperature separately (with a thermistor or semiconductor sensor) and add its contribution: **cold-junction compensation**.
- Its output is small, so amplification, noise and offset matter (later entries).
- It is self-generating, robust and covers very wide ranges (some types reach well above 1000 °C), which is why thermocouples dominate furnace and engine temperature measurement, where platinum resistance thermometers are fragile or impractical.

## 7. Optical Sensing

### 7.1 The photoelectric effect in semiconductors

When a photon with enough energy is absorbed in a semiconductor, it frees an electron and leaves a hole behind. In a **photodiode**, the electric field of a p–n junction separates these carriers and produces a **photocurrent** proportional to the absorbed optical power $P$:

$$
I = \mathcal{R}\,P, \qquad \mathcal{R} = \frac{\eta\,q\,\lambda}{h\,c},
$$

where the **responsivity** $\mathcal{R}$ (in amperes per watt) depends on the quantum efficiency $\eta$ (the fraction of photons that produce a carrier pair), the wavelength $\lambda$, the elementary charge $q$, Planck's constant $h$ and the speed of light $c$. For a silicon photodiode with $\eta = 0.8$ at 850 nm, $\mathcal{R} = 0.55$ A/W, so 1 µW of light produces 0.55 µA. Silicon responds roughly from 400 to 1100 nm; other materials cover the infrared.

A photodiode can be used with no applied voltage (photovoltaic mode, self-generating) or with a reverse bias (photoconductive mode), which speeds it up at the cost of a small dark current that flows even without light. Its current is converted to a voltage by a transimpedance amplifier, another subject of the signal-conditioning entry. Arrays of photodiodes form the image sensors in cameras.

### 7.2 Light as an intermediate domain

Many sensors use light as an intermediate step because light is fast, immune to electrical interference and needs no contact:

- **optical encoders** count light pulses through a slotted disc to measure angle and speed (the encoders of the robotics series);
- **time-of-flight** sensors and lidar measure distance from the travel time of a light pulse;
- **optical fibre** sensors measure strain or temperature from changes in the light guided by a fibre, over long distances and in electrically hostile places.

## 8. Electrochemical Sensing

Electrochemical sensors measure chemical composition through reactions at an electrode in contact with a solution or gas.

A **potentiometric** sensor measures the voltage of an electrode whose potential depends on the concentration (strictly, the chemical activity $a$) of a particular ion, with almost no current flowing. The relation is the **Nernst equation**:

$$
E = E_0 + \frac{R\,T}{n\,F}\ln a ,
$$

where $E_0$ is a reference potential, $R$ the gas constant, $T$ the absolute temperature, $n$ the charge number of the ion and $F$ the Faraday constant. A **pH electrode** is the classic example: pH is $-\log_{10}$ of the hydrogen-ion activity, so with $n = 1$ the electrode's voltage changes by $\ln(10)\,RT/F = 59.16$ mV per pH unit at 25 °C. At 37 °C the same electrode gives 61.54 mV per unit; the slope is proportional to absolute temperature, which is why pH meters measure temperature as well and correct for it.

An **amperometric** sensor applies a voltage and measures the current of a reaction that consumes the target species; many dissolved-oxygen and toxic-gas sensors work this way. Electrochemical sensors are sensitive and selective, but their electrodes age and foul, and they need regular calibration with reference solutions.

## 9. Choosing a Principle

No principle is best in general; each answers some requirements and fails others. The same measurand can often be sensed several ways:

| Measurand | Options | Typical deciding factor |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Platinum resistance, thermistor, thermocouple, infrared (optical) | Accuracy and range (platinum), cost and sensitivity (thermistor), very high temperatures (thermocouple), no contact (infrared) |
| Displacement | Potentiometer, LVDT, capacitive, optical encoder, eddy current | Contact and wear, resolution, range, environment (dirt, oil, magnetic fields) |
| Force or pressure | Strain gauge, capacitive, piezoelectric | Whether static force must be measured (rules out piezoelectric), bandwidth, size |
| Acceleration | Capacitive (microfabricated), piezoelectric | Static tilt and low frequencies (capacitive), high frequencies and shock (piezoelectric) |
| Magnetic field or current | Hall effect, magnetoresistive, inductive | Sensitivity, isolation, cost |

The questions of the previous entry (range, resolution, accuracy, speed, influence quantities, loading) decide between them, together with practical ones: whether the sensor needs excitation, how small its output is, and what conditioning it requires. That last question leads to the next entry.

## 10. Summary

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| How can sensing principles be organised? | By energy domain of the measurand (mechanical, thermal, magnetic, radiant, chemical, electrical), and by the physical effect that converts it into an electrical quantity. |
| How do resistive sensors work? | The measurand changes resistivity, length or cross-section ($R = \rho l / A$); excitation turns the change into a voltage. |
| Why choose a thermistor over platinum, or the reverse? | A thermistor is about ten times more sensitive but nonlinear and less stable; platinum is accurate and stable over a wide range. |
| How large is a strain-gauge signal? | $\Delta R / R = K\varepsilon$; with $K \approx 2$, 1000 µε changes a 350 Ω gauge by only 0.7 Ω. |
| What can capacitive sensors measure? | Anything that changes gap, overlap area or permittivity: pressure, acceleration, position, humidity, level, touch. |
| Why can a piezoelectric sensor not measure static force? | Its charge leaks away with a time constant set by capacitance and insulation resistance. |
| What does a thermocouple measure? | The temperature difference between its measuring and reference junctions; absolute temperature needs cold-junction compensation. |
| What sets a photodiode's output? | Its responsivity, $\eta q \lambda / hc$, times the absorbed optical power. |
| Why do pH meters measure temperature? | The Nernst slope, 59.16 mV per pH unit at 25 °C, is proportional to absolute temperature. |
