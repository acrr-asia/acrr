The previous entry ended with a collection of awkward signals. A strain gauge changes its resistance by 0.2 %. A thermocouple produces about 41 µV per kelvin. A piezoelectric crystal produces a few picocoulombs of charge that leak away within a second, and a photodiode produces a fraction of a microampere. None of these can be connected directly to the next stage of the measurement chain, the **analog-to-digital converter** (ADC): a circuit, treated in the next entry, that accepts a voltage within a fixed input range, such as 0 to 3.3 V, and represents it as a number.

**Signal conditioning** is the analog circuitry that closes this gap. It has five jobs:

- **excite** modulating sensors, which change an electrical property but generate no signal of their own;
- **convert** the sensor's output (resistance, current, charge) into a voltage;
- **amplify** and **shift** that voltage so that the measurand's range fills the converter's input range;
- **filter** out frequencies that carry no information about the measurand;
- **protect and isolate** the electronics and the people around them when the sensor sits in a hostile electrical environment.

This entry follows the signal through those jobs in that order, and then assembles them into one complete channel for a load cell. Circuits are described with ideal components first and then with the imperfections that limit a real design. Throughout, **decibels** express ratios of voltages: a ratio $A$ is $20\log_{10} A$ dB, so a factor of 10 is 20 dB, a factor of 1000 is 60 dB, and a factor of $1/\sqrt{2}$ is −3 dB.

## 1. Excitation

A modulating sensor needs a source of energy, its **excitation**, to turn a change of resistance, capacitance or inductance into a change of voltage. For a resistive sensor, the simplest excitation is a constant current $I$: the voltage across the sensor is then $V = IR$, directly proportional to its resistance. A PT100 platinum sensor (100 Ω at 0 °C, about 0.385 Ω/°C) excited with 1 mA gives 100 mV at 0 °C and changes by 0.385 mV/°C.

Excitation has two costs, and they pull in opposite directions:

- **More excitation gives more signal.** The output is proportional to the excitation, so a larger current or voltage lifts the signal further above the noise and offsets of the electronics that follow.
- **More excitation heats the sensor.** The sensor dissipates $I^2R$; the first entry showed that a PT100 with 1 mA dissipates 0.14 mW at 100 °C and warms slightly above the medium it measures. Self-heating grows with the square of the current, so doubling the current to double the signal quadruples the error.

A third consideration is the **stability** of the excitation. If the output is $V = IR$ and $I$ drifts by 0.1 %, the reading drifts by 0.1 % with it. A well-designed channel avoids depending on the excitation at all by making the measurement **ratiometric**: the same source that excites the sensor also serves as the converter's reference voltage, so the converter reports the ratio of the sensor voltage to the excitation, and any drift of the excitation divides out. Section 7 uses this technique.

### 1.1 Lead resistance

The wires that connect a sensor to its electronics have resistance too, and for a resistive sensor that resistance adds to the measurand's. In a **two-wire** connection, the excitation current flows through both leads, and the voltage measured includes their drop. Two leads of 0.5 Ω each add 1 Ω to a PT100, which the instrument reads as $1 / 0.385 \approx 2.6$ °C, and the error changes with the temperature of the leads themselves.

Two connection schemes remove most or all of this error:

- In a **three-wire** connection, a third lead lets the circuit place one lead resistance in each of two adjacent arms of a bridge (Section 2), where equal resistances cancel. The cancellation is as good as the match between the leads.
- In a **four-wire** (Kelvin) connection, one pair of leads carries the excitation current and a separate pair senses the voltage across the sensor. The sensing pair connects to a high-impedance input and carries practically no current, so its resistance causes no voltage drop. This is the standard connection for precision resistance thermometry.

## 2. Dividers and Bridges

A constant current is one way to excite a resistive sensor; a constant voltage is more common because voltage sources are simpler and more stable. The simplest circuit is a **voltage divider**: the sensor $R_s$ in series with a fixed resistor $R_0$ across an excitation voltage $V_\text{ex}$, with the output taken across the sensor:

$$
V = V_\text{ex}\,\frac{R_s}{R_0 + R_s}.
$$

The divider has a serious drawback for small changes. With $R_0 = R_s = 350$ Ω and $V_\text{ex} = 5$ V, the output is 2.5 V. When a strain gauge changes by 0.2 %, the output changes by about 2.5 mV, a thousandth of the voltage it rides on. Amplifying the change means amplifying the 2.5 V as well, which would saturate any amplifier with enough gain to make the change useful.

### 2.1 The Wheatstone bridge

The **Wheatstone bridge** removes the large constant part by subtracting a second, fixed divider from the first. Four resistors form two dividers across the same excitation, and the output is the difference between their midpoints:

<Figure caption="A Wheatstone bridge. The left divider (R1, R2) and the right divider (R3, R4) share the excitation; the output is the difference between their midpoint voltages. In a quarter bridge, R2 is the sensor and the other three arms are fixed.">

</Figure>

$$
V_\text{out} = V_A - V_B = V_\text{ex}\left(\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} - \frac{R_4}{R_3 + R_4}\right).
$$

The output is zero, and the bridge is **balanced**, when the two divider ratios are equal, which is when $R_1 R_4 = R_2 R_3$. A balanced bridge outputs zero no matter what the excitation is, so the large constant voltage of the divider has disappeared and only the change remains.

In a **quarter bridge**, one arm is the sensor and the other three are fixed resistors equal to the sensor's nominal resistance $R$. Write the sensor's resistance as $R_2 = R(1 + x)$, where $x$ is its fractional change ($x = K\varepsilon$ for a strain gauge with gauge factor $K$ under strain $\varepsilon$). The left midpoint is at $V_\text{ex}(1 + x)/(2 + x)$, the right at $V_\text{ex}/2$, and the difference is

$$
V_\text{out} = V_\text{ex}\,\frac{x}{4 + 2x} \approx V_\text{ex}\,\frac{x}{4} \qquad (x \ll 1).
$$

For the strain gauge of the previous entry ($K = 2$, 1000 µε, so $x = 0.002$) and 5 V excitation, the output is 2.4975 mV. The approximation $V_\text{ex}x/4$ gives 2.5 mV; the difference, a relative error of about $x/2 = 0.1$ %, is the quarter bridge's **nonlinearity**, which grows with the size of the change. For strain gauges it is usually negligible; for sensors with larger fractional changes, such as thermistors, it is not.

### 2.2 Half and full bridges

Placing sensors in more than one arm increases the output and removes the nonlinearity. The bridge equation contains the rule that governs where they go: **changes in adjacent arms subtract, and changes in opposite arms add**. If $R_1$ and $R_2$ change by the same factor, the left divider's ratio is unchanged and the output does not move; if $R_2$ and $R_3$ both increase, both terms of the difference move in the direction that increases $V_\text{out}$.

Consider a beam in bending, which stretches its top surface and compresses its bottom surface by the same strain. Its gauges therefore change by $+x$ and $-x$ (taking tensile strain as positive):

| Configuration | Active arms | Output | Linearity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quarter bridge | $R_2 = R(1 + x)$ | $V_\text{ex}\,x/(4 + 2x)$ | Slightly nonlinear |
| Half bridge | $R_1 = R(1 - x)$, $R_2 = R(1 + x)$ | $V_\text{ex}\,x/2$ | Linear |
| Full bridge | Half bridge, plus $R_3 = R(1 + x)$, $R_4 = R(1 - x)$ | $V_\text{ex}\,x$ | Linear |

The full bridge gives four times the quarter bridge's output, 10 mV instead of 2.5 mV in the example above, and it is how most commercial load cells and pressure sensors are built.

The same rule handles influence quantities. A strain gauge also changes resistance with temperature, which the bridge cannot tell apart from strain. A second, identical **dummy gauge**, mounted on the same material but not strained, placed in an adjacent arm, sees the same temperature change. Its change subtracts from the active gauge's, so the thermal effect cancels while the strain signal remains. In a half or full bridge, every arm sees the same temperature, so the compensation comes for free.

### 2.3 What the bridge leaves behind

The bridge output is small, and it is **differential**: it is the difference between two nodes, neither of which is at 0 V. With 5 V excitation, both $V_A$ and $V_B$ sit near 2.5 V, and the millivolt signal is their difference. A circuit that measures this difference must reject the 2.5 V they have in common, and Section 4 shows how. First, the amplifier itself needs introducing.

## 3. Operational Amplifiers

An **operational amplifier** (op-amp) is an integrated amplifier with two inputs, a non-inverting input ($+$) and an inverting input ($-$), and one output. Its output is the difference between its inputs multiplied by a very large **open-loop gain**, typically $10^5$ to $10^7$. That gain is too large and too variable to use directly. Op-amps are instead used with **negative feedback**: a network of resistors (or capacitors) feeds part of the output back to the inverting input, and the circuit's behaviour is then set by that network rather than by the op-amp.

The analysis of feedback circuits rests on an **ideal op-amp model**: infinite open-loop gain, no current into either input, and an output that can supply any current. With negative feedback, two rules follow from it:

1. The output takes whatever value makes the two input voltages equal (otherwise the infinite gain would drive the output without limit).
2. No current flows into either input.

These two rules give the three circuits on which most signal conditioning is built:

| Circuit | Connection | Gain |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage follower (buffer) | Output connected to $-$, signal to $+$ | 1 |
| Non-inverting amplifier | Signal to $+$; $R_f$ from output to $-$, $R_g$ from $-$ to ground | $1 + R_f / R_g$ |
| Inverting amplifier | $R_\text{in}$ from signal to $-$; $R_f$ from output to $-$; $+$ to ground | $-R_f / R_\text{in}$ |

The voltage follower has no gain, but it is not useless: its input draws practically no current, while its output can drive a load. It therefore isolates a high-resistance source from whatever follows it, which is the electrical form of the **loading** problem from the first entry.

In the inverting amplifier, rule 1 holds the inverting input at the same voltage as the grounded non-inverting input. This node is called a **virtual ground**: it stays at 0 V although it is not connected to ground. Section 5 builds two important sensor interfaces on it.

### 3.1 Real op-amps

Real op-amps depart from the ideal in ways that matter most when the signal is small:

- **Input offset voltage.** A real op-amp behaves as if a small voltage, typically from a few microvolts to a few millivolts depending on the type, were in series with one input. It is amplified with the signal. An offset of 1 mV in front of a bridge signal of 2.5 mV full scale is an error of 40 % of full scale. Precision and **zero-drift** op-amps (which continuously measure and cancel their own offset) reduce it to a few microvolts.
- **Input bias current.** A small current does flow into the inputs, from picoamperes to nanoamperes. Through a source resistance it produces a further offset voltage, and for the charge and current amplifiers of Section 5 it is a direct error.
- **Finite bandwidth.** The open-loop gain falls with frequency. For most op-amps, the product of closed-loop gain and bandwidth is roughly constant and is specified as the **gain–bandwidth product** (GBW). An op-amp with a GBW of 1 MHz configured for a gain of 100 has a bandwidth of about 10 kHz.
- **Output swing.** The output cannot reach beyond its supply voltages, and many op-amps cannot come within a few tenths of a volt of them. "Rail-to-rail" types come within millivolts.

Offset, bias current and their drift with temperature are systematic errors of the conditioning stage. Calibration ([the last entry](/blog/calibration-and-multisensor-fusion) of the series) can remove the part that is constant; the part that drifts adds to the uncertainty.

## 4. Differential Signals and Instrumentation Amplifiers

Any pair of voltages $V_+$ and $V_-$ can be described by their difference and their average:

- the **differential-mode** voltage, $V_d = V_+ - V_-$, which for a bridge carries the measurement;
- the **common-mode** voltage, $V_\text{cm} = (V_+ + V_-)/2$, which for a bridge is about $V_\text{ex}/2$ and carries no information.

An ideal **differential amplifier** responds only to $V_d$. A real one has a small gain $A_\text{cm}$ for the common-mode voltage as well as its intended differential gain $A_d$. Their ratio is the **common-mode rejection ratio**,

$$
\text{CMRR} = \frac{A_d}{A_\text{cm}}, \qquad \text{CMRR}_\text{dB} = 20\log_{10}\frac{A_d}{A_\text{cm}}.
$$

Dividing the output by $A_d$ refers the error back to the input: a common-mode voltage $V_\text{cm}$ looks like a differential input of $V_\text{cm}/\text{CMRR}$. For the 5 V bridge, with $V_\text{cm} = 2.5$ V:

| CMRR | Equivalent input error | Compared with a 2.5 mV full-scale signal |
|---|---|---|
| 80 dB ($10^4$) | 250 µV | 10 % |
| 100 dB ($10^5$) | 25 µV | 1 % |

A constant common-mode error is just an offset, and calibration could remove it. Two things make CMRR matter anyway. The common-mode voltage changes when the excitation changes, and interference picked up by long sensor leads (mains hum, for instance) arrives almost equally on both leads, that is, as a common-mode signal. A high CMRR rejects both.

### 4.1 The instrumentation amplifier

A single op-amp with four resistors forms a **difference amplifier** whose output is proportional to $V_+ - V_-$. It has two weaknesses for sensor work: its input resistances, which are the resistors themselves, load the bridge, and its CMRR is limited by how well the four resistors match. With resistors of 0.1 % tolerance, the CMRR at unity gain can be as low as about 54 dB.

The **instrumentation amplifier** (in-amp) solves both. Its classic form uses three op-amps:

- **Input stage.** Each input drives the non-inverting input of an op-amp, so the bridge sees a very high input resistance and is not loaded. The two op-amps are connected through a resistor network with a single **gain resistor** $R_G$ between them. A differential input is amplified by $1 + 2R/R_G$ (where $R$ is each of the two internal feedback resistors); a common-mode input appears at both outputs unchanged, with a gain of one.
- **Output stage.** A difference amplifier subtracts the two outputs, removing the common-mode voltage. Because the input stage has already amplified the differential signal and not the common-mode one, the overall CMRR improves in proportion to the gain.

The gain is therefore $G = 1 + 2R/R_G$, set by one external resistor. With internal resistors of $R = 25$ kΩ, a gain of 1000 needs $R_G = 50\,\text{k}\Omega / 999 \approx 50.05$ Ω. Integrated in-amps, with laser-trimmed internal resistors, achieve CMRRs of 100 dB or more at high gain.

The output stage has one more useful property: its reference terminal, normally grounded, sets the voltage that corresponds to zero differential input. If a bridge output can be either positive or negative (strain in tension or compression), but the converter accepts only 0 to 3.3 V, connecting the reference to 1.65 V places zero strain in the middle of the range. With a full-scale signal of ±2.5 mV and a gain of 600, the output then spans 0.15 to 3.15 V.

## 5. Converting Current and Charge

Not every sensor produces a voltage. Two self-generating sensors from the previous entry produce a current and a charge, and each has a standard interface built on the virtual ground of the inverting amplifier.

### 5.1 The transimpedance amplifier

A photodiode produces a current proportional to light. A **transimpedance amplifier** converts it to a voltage: the photodiode connects to the inverting input, and a feedback resistor $R_f$ connects that input to the output. Because the inverting input is a virtual ground, the photodiode sees 0 V across it (photovoltaic mode), and all of its current $I$ flows through $R_f$, so

$$
V_\text{out} = -I R_f .
$$

The silicon photodiode of the previous entry produces 0.55 µA from 1 µW of light at 850 nm. With $R_f = 1$ MΩ the output is −0.55 V. A larger $R_f$ gives more output, but together with the photodiode's own capacitance it also makes the circuit prone to oscillation. A small capacitor $C_f$ in parallel with $R_f$ keeps it stable and sets the bandwidth at about $1/(2\pi R_f C_f)$: with 1 MΩ and 1 pF, about 160 kHz. Gain and bandwidth trade against each other through the product $R_f C_f$.

### 5.2 The charge amplifier

A piezoelectric sensor produces a charge $Q$. The previous entry turned it into a voltage by letting it charge the sensor and cable capacitance, $V = Q/C$. That makes the reading depend on the cable: a longer or different cable changes $C$ and therefore the sensitivity.

A **charge amplifier** replaces this with an inverting amplifier whose feedback element is a capacitor $C_f$. The sensor connects to the virtual ground, so the voltage across the sensor and cable capacitance stays at zero and their charge stays at zero too. The charge the crystal generates therefore flows onto $C_f$:

$$
V_\text{out} = -\frac{Q}{C_f}.
$$

The sensor and cable capacitance no longer appear in the result. The 23 pC that a quartz sensor produces for 10 N gives −0.23 V with $C_f = 100$ pF, whatever cable connects it.

A capacitor alone in the feedback path would integrate the op-amp's input bias current until the output saturated, so a large resistor $R_f$ is placed across $C_f$. It also makes the charge leak away, now with a time constant $R_f C_f$ chosen by the designer rather than set by the insulation of the cable. With $R_f = 10$ GΩ and $C_f = 100$ pF, the time constant is 1 s and the lower cut-off frequency $1/(2\pi R_f C_f)$ is about 0.16 Hz. As the previous entry said, the charge amplifier extends the low-frequency limit but cannot remove it.

### 5.3 Other conversions

Two other families need only a mention here:

- **Thermocouples** produce a voltage directly, but a small one, about 41 µV/K for type K. They need a gain of the order of 100 or more, an amplifier with low offset and drift, and the cold-junction compensation described in the previous entry: a measurement of the reference-junction temperature, added to the thermocouple's reading either by a circuit or in software.
- **Capacitive and inductive sensors**, such as the differential capacitor of a microfabricated accelerometer or an LVDT, need **alternating excitation**, because a capacitance or inductance has no effect on a steady current. The measurand then modulates the amplitude (and, for a differential sensor, the phase) of an AC signal. A **synchronous demodulator** recovers it by multiplying the signal by the excitation waveform and low-pass filtering the product, which yields a voltage proportional to the measurand with a sign that shows the direction of displacement. The same principle underlies the lock-in amplifier, which modulates a measurement deliberately so that it can be recovered from noise many times larger.

## 6. Analog Filters

The sensor's output contains the measurand's signal plus everything else: mains interference at 50 or 60 Hz, radio-frequency pickup, and noise spread over all frequencies. A **filter** passes the frequencies that carry the measurand and attenuates the rest. Filtering in the analog domain has two purposes that the next entries develop:

- **Noise reduction.** Much of the noise in a measurement system is spread evenly across frequency, so the noise that reaches the output grows with the bandwidth that is let through. Limiting the bandwidth to what the measurand needs limits the noise; the noise entry quantifies this.
- **Anti-aliasing.** A converter that samples the signal at discrete instants cannot distinguish a high-frequency component from a lower one that matches it at every sample. Such components must be removed before sampling, which only an analog filter can do. The next entry explains why.

### 6.1 The first-order low-pass filter

The simplest filter is a resistor in series with the signal and a capacitor to ground, with the output taken across the capacitor. At low frequencies the capacitor's impedance is high and the signal passes; at high frequencies it falls and the capacitor shunts the signal to ground. The ratio of output to input amplitude at frequency $f$ is the filter's **magnitude response**:

$$
|H(f)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_c)^2}}, \qquad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC},
$$

where $f_c$ is the **cut-off frequency**. At $f_c$ the magnitude is $1/\sqrt{2}$, or −3 dB. Far above it, $|H| \approx f_c / f$: the attenuation increases by a factor of 10, that is 20 dB, for every tenfold increase in frequency (20 dB per **decade**).

A load cell weighing goods on a conveyor might need only the frequencies below 10 Hz. With $R = 16$ kΩ and $C = 1$ µF, $f_c \approx 9.95$ Hz. At 50 Hz, five times the cut-off frequency, the magnitude is $1/\sqrt{1 + 25} \approx 0.196$: the mains interference is reduced by only 14 dB, about a factor of five. A first-order filter falls too gently to separate frequencies that are close together.

### 6.2 Higher-order filters

Steeper filters use several energy-storing elements, normally capacitors combined with op-amps in **active filters** (a common circuit, the Sallen–Key stage, makes a second-order section from one op-amp, two resistors and two capacitors). The **order** $n$ of a filter is the number of such elements, and far above the cut-off frequency its attenuation increases by $20n$ dB per decade.

How the response behaves near the cut-off frequency is a design choice, and three classic families make it differently:

- A **Butterworth** filter has the flattest possible passband: no ripple, and a smooth bend at $f_c$.
- A **Chebyshev** filter accepts a specified ripple in the passband in exchange for a steeper transition.
- A **Bessel** filter has a gentler transition but delays all frequencies in its passband by nearly the same time, so it preserves the shape of pulses and steps, which matters when the waveform, not only its amplitude, is measured.

The Butterworth magnitude response has a simple closed form,

$$
|H(f)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_c)^{2n}}}.
$$

For the 10 Hz load-cell filter, at 50 Hz:

| Order $n$ | $\lvert H \rvert$ at 50 Hz | Attenuation | $\lvert H \rvert$ at 3 Hz |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.196 | 14.1 dB | 0.958 |
| 2 | 0.040 | 28.0 dB | 0.996 |
| 4 | 0.0016 | 55.9 dB | 0.99997 |

The last column shows the other benefit of a higher order: inside the passband, at 3 Hz, the first-order filter already attenuates the signal by 4 %, while the fourth-order filter is flat to 0.003 %. A higher order costs more components and more delay. Every filter delays the signal, and a sharp filter delays it more, which matters when the measurement is part of a feedback loop.

A **notch filter**, which removes a narrow band around a single frequency, can attack mains interference directly. It is fragile: mains frequency varies slightly, the interference also contains harmonics at multiples of the mains frequency, and a narrow notch must be tuned precisely to work. Reducing interference at its source, with differential measurement, shielding and good grounding, usually works better; the noise entry returns to it.

## 7. A Complete Channel: Load Cell to Converter

The pieces assemble into a complete channel. A load cell is a full strain-gauge bridge bonded to a metal element, specified by its **rated output** in millivolts per volt of excitation at full load; 2 mV/V is a common value. Suppose its signal is to be converted by an ADC with an input range of 0 to 3.3 V.

1. **Excitation.** The bridge is excited from the same 3.3 V supply that serves as the ADC's reference voltage. At full load, its output is $2\ \text{mV/V} \times 3.3\ \text{V} = 6.6$ mV.
2. **Amplification.** An instrumentation amplifier with a gain of 450 turns 6.6 mV into 2.97 V, using 90 % of the converter's range and leaving headroom for overload. Its high input resistance does not load the bridge, and its CMRR rejects the 1.65 V common-mode voltage and the interference picked up by the cable. Because the load in a weighing application only compresses the cell, the reference terminal is set slightly above 0 V, so that zero load does not ask the output to reach its lower limit.
3. **Filtering.** A fourth-order low-pass filter with a cut-off of 10 Hz passes the weighing signal, removes mains interference and limits the noise bandwidth before sampling.
4. **Conversion, ratiometric.** The ADC reports its input as a fraction of its reference. At full load that fraction is $450 \times 2\ \text{mV/V} = 0.9$, regardless of the exact supply voltage: if the supply drifts by 1 %, the bridge output and the reference drift together, and the reading does not change.

Each stage contributes to the uncertainty of the result: the bridge's balance and temperature behaviour, the amplifier's gain error, offset and drift, the filter's passband flatness, and the converter's own errors. Calibrating the complete channel with known loads, rather than each stage separately, absorbs the constant parts of all of them at once.

### 7.1 Protection and isolation

Two practical stages complete a channel that leaves the laboratory. **Input protection** (series resistors, clamping diodes and transient suppressors) keeps static discharges and wiring faults on long sensor cables from destroying the amplifier. **Galvanic isolation**, which carries the signal across a barrier through a transformer, capacitor or optical link with no conducting path, is needed when the sensor sits at a dangerous voltage, as in current measurement on a power line or in medical equipment connected to a patient, or when two grounds far apart differ by more than the amplifier's common-mode range.

## 8. Summary

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What does signal conditioning do? | Excites the sensor, converts its output to a voltage, amplifies and shifts it to the converter's range, filters it, and protects and isolates the electronics. |
| What limits excitation? | Self-heating, which grows with the square of the current; ratiometric measurement removes the dependence on its stability. |
| How are lead-wire errors removed? | Three-wire connections cancel them in adjacent bridge arms; four-wire connections sense voltage on leads that carry no current. |
| Why use a bridge rather than a divider? | A balanced bridge outputs zero, so only the change is amplified; a quarter bridge gives about $V_\text{ex}x/4$. |
| How do sensors combine in a bridge? | Changes in adjacent arms subtract, changes in opposite arms add; half and full bridges are linear and compensate temperature. |
| What does an ideal op-amp with feedback do? | Drives its output so the inputs are equal, with no input current; this gives the follower, non-inverting and inverting amplifiers. |
| Why an instrumentation amplifier? | High input resistance, gain set by one resistor, and a high CMRR that rejects the bridge's common-mode voltage and interference. |
| How are current and charge converted? | A transimpedance amplifier gives $-IR_f$; a charge amplifier gives $-Q/C_f$, independent of cable capacitance. |
| What does filter order buy? | Attenuation of $20n$ dB per decade above cut-off and a flatter passband, at the cost of components and delay. |

The output of the channel is now a clean voltage within the converter's range. The next entry follows it across the boundary between the continuous and the discrete: sampling, aliasing and the anti-aliasing filter, quantization, and how analog-to-digital converters work.
