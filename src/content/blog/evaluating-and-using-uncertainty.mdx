## 1. What a good predictive distribution does

### 1.1 Calibration, sharpness and discrimination

A predictive distribution can be wrong in several distinct ways, and a single number rarely captures all of them. Three properties are worth separating.

- **Calibration** is agreement between stated probabilities and observed frequencies: among all inputs for which a classifier says "class 3 with probability 0.8", class 3 should occur about 80% of the time. It is a joint property of the predictions and the data, never of one prediction alone.
- **Sharpness** is how concentrated the predictions are. A classifier that always outputs the class frequencies of the training set is perfectly calibrated and useless; sharp predictions commit to answers.
- **Discrimination** (or ranking quality) is whether higher confidence goes with a higher chance of being right, so that confident predictions can be trusted more than hesitant ones.

Gneiting, Balabdaoui and Raftery (2007) state the goal for probabilistic forecasts as maximizing sharpness subject to calibration. Accuracy measures none of the three: it only asks whether the most probable class is the correct one.

### 1.2 Evaluation data

Every quantity in this entry is an estimate computed from held-out examples, so the rules of [Evaluating Models Honestly](/blog/evaluating-models-honestly) apply unchanged. Anything fitted to improve the uncertainty (a temperature, a rejection threshold, a combination of scores) is fitted on validation data, and the test set is used once. A calibration measured on the data used to tune it is optimistic for the same reason as a test error measured on the training set.

## 2. Proper scoring rules

### 2.1 Definition

A **scoring rule** $S(q, y)$ assigns a number to a predictive distribution $q$ over outputs and the output $y$ that actually occurred. This entry uses the loss orientation, in which lower is better. If outputs are drawn from a distribution $p$, the expected score of a forecast $q$ is $S(q, p) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p}[S(q, y)]$. The rule is **proper** if $S(p, p) \le S(q, p)$ for every $q$, so that reporting the true distribution is never worse than reporting anything else, and **strictly proper** if equality holds only for $q = p$ (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007).

Propriety is what makes a score safe to optimize and to compare models with: a model cannot improve a strictly proper score by distorting its probabilities away from what it believes. Training a network by minimizing a strictly proper score on held-out data, or comparing two Bayesian approximations by their average test score, rewards the honest predictive distribution.

### 2.2 The log score and the Brier score

For a classifier over $K$ classes that outputs probabilities $q = (q_1, \dots, q_K)$, the two scores in common use are

$$
S_{\log}(q, y) = -\log q_y,
\qquad
S_{\text{Brier}}(q, y) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big(q_k - \mathbb{1}[y = k]\big)^2,
$$

where $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ is 1 when its argument is true and 0 otherwise. The **log score** is the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the observed label; averaged over a test set it is the test cross-entropy. The **Brier score** is the squared distance between $q$ and the one-hot vector of the label (Brier, 1950); it lies between 0 and 2.

Both are strictly proper, and the expected scores show why. Taking the expectation over $y \sim p$,

$$
S_{\log}(q, p) = H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p \,\Vert\, q),
\qquad
S_{\text{Brier}}(q, p) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k (1 - p_k) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} (q_k - p_k)^2,
$$

where $H(p) = -\sum_k p_k \log p_k$ is the entropy of $p$ and $\mathrm{KL}(p \,\Vert\, q) = \sum_k p_k \log(p_k / q_k)$ is the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence. In each case the first term does not depend on $q$ and the second is zero exactly when $q = p$. The first term is the irreducible part: even the true distribution pays it, because the label is random.

### 2.3 A worked example

Suppose that for some input the true probabilities of two classes are $p = (0.7, 0.3)$. Three forecasts score as follows.

| Forecast $q$ | Expected log score | Expected Brier score | Predicted class |
|---|---|---|---|
| $(0.7, 0.3)$, the truth | 0.611 | 0.420 | 1 |
| $(0.9, 0.1)$, overconfident | 0.765 | 0.500 | 1 |
| $(0.5, 0.5)$, underconfident | 0.693 | 0.500 | tie |

Both scores rank the true forecast first. Accuracy does not separate the first two rows at all, because both predict class 1; a model could become arbitrarily overconfident without losing accuracy. The two scores also disagree on what to punish. The log score charges more for overconfidence than for an equal error toward 0.5, while the Brier score, being quadratic in $q - p$, charges both equally here.

The difference is starker for a confident mistake. If a forecast gives the observed label probability 0.01, its log score is $-\log 0.01 = 4.61$, whereas its Brier score cannot exceed 2. The log score is therefore sensitive to rare, very confident errors, which is a virtue when such errors are costly and a nuisance when a few mislabeled test examples dominate the average. Reporting both is common practice.

## 3. Calibration and the expected calibration error

### 3.1 Confidence and top-label calibration

For a classifier, write $\hat{y} = \arg\max_k q_k$ for the predicted class and $c = \max_k q_k$ for its **confidence**. The classifier is **top-label calibrated** if, for every value $c_0$, the probability that the prediction is correct given that the confidence equals $c_0$ is $c_0$:

$$
P\big(\hat{y} = y \mid c = c_0\big) = c_0 .
$$

This is weaker than full calibration of the probability vector, which would require every component $q_k$ to match a frequency, but it is the property that matters when the prediction and its confidence are what a user sees.

### 3.2 The estimator

Because $c$ is continuous, the conditional probability cannot be read off a finite test set directly. The **expected calibration error** (ECE) groups the $n$ test predictions into $B$ bins by confidence, usually of equal width on $[0, 1]$, and compares average accuracy with average confidence in each bin (Naeini, Cooper and Hauskrecht, 2015; Guo et al., 2017):

$$
\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{\lvert \mathcal{B}_b \rvert}{n} \,\big\lvert \mathrm{acc}(\mathcal{B}_b) - \mathrm{conf}(\mathcal{B}_b) \big\rvert ,
$$

where $\mathcal{B}_b$ is the set of predictions in bin $b$, $\lvert \mathcal{B}_b \rvert$ its size, $\mathrm{acc}(\mathcal{B}_b)$ the fraction of them that are correct and $\mathrm{conf}(\mathcal{B}_b)$ their mean confidence. A **reliability diagram** plots $\mathrm{acc}(\mathcal{B}_b)$ against $\mathrm{conf}(\mathcal{B}_b)$; a calibrated classifier lies on the diagonal, and an overconfident one lies below it.

### 3.3 Temperature scaling

Guo et al. (2017) found modern image classifiers to be overconfident and proposed a one-parameter fix. **Temperature scaling** divides the logits $\ell$ (the inputs to the softmax) by a scalar $T > 0$ before the softmax, $q = \mathrm{softmax}(\ell / T)$, and chooses $T$ to minimize the log score on validation data. A temperature above 1 softens the probabilities; below 1 sharpens them. Dividing by a positive constant does not change which logit is largest, so the predicted class and the accuracy are unchanged. Temperature scaling is not Bayesian, but it is the baseline every Bayesian method in this series should be compared with: if a posterior approximation is not better calibrated than a temperature-scaled point estimate, its extra cost buys little calibration.

### 3.4 Worked example: an overconfident classifier

The listing builds 6000 synthetic inputs with four classes. Each input has true logits $z$ with independent standard normal entries scaled by 1.5, true probabilities $\mathrm{softmax}(z)$, and a label drawn from them. The "trained model" sees $z$ through noise of standard deviation 0.5 and then multiplies by 2.5, which makes it overconfident by construction. The first 3000 inputs are the validation set, used only to choose $T$ on a grid; the remaining 3000 are the test set.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=7)
n, k = 6000, 4

# Ground truth: each input has true class probabilities softmax(z).
z = rng.normal(0.0, 1.5, size=(n, k))
p_true = np.exp(z) / np.exp(z).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
y = np.array([rng.choice(k, p=p) for p in p_true])

# An overconfident model: a noisy estimate of z, scaled up by 2.5.
logits = 2.5 * (z + rng.normal(0.0, 0.5, size=(n, k)))

def softmax(a):
    e = np.exp(a - a.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return e / e.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def scores(p, y):
    idx = np.arange(len(y))
    nll = -np.mean(np.log(p[idx, y]))
    onehot = np.eye(p.shape[1])[y]
    brier = np.mean(np.sum((p - onehot) ** 2, axis=1))
    return nll, brier

def ece(p, y, bins=10):
    conf, pred = p.max(axis=1), p.argmax(axis=1)
    correct = (pred == y).astype(float)
    edges = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, bins + 1)
    b = np.clip(np.digitize(conf, edges[1:-1]), 0, bins - 1)
    total = 0.0
    for j in range(bins):
        m = b == j
        if m.any():
            total += m.mean() * abs(correct[m].mean() - conf[m].mean())
    return total

val, test = slice(0, 3000), slice(3000, n)
temps = np.linspace(0.5, 5.0, 451)
nll_val = [scores(softmax(logits[val] / t), y[val])[0] for t in temps]
T = temps[int(np.argmin(nll_val))]

for name, p in [("model", softmax(logits[test])),
                (f"T = {T:.2f}", softmax(logits[test] / T)),
                ("truth", p_true[test])]:
    acc = np.mean(p.argmax(axis=1) == y[test])
    nll, brier = scores(p, y[test])
    print(f"{name:9s} acc {acc:.3f}  NLL {nll:.3f}  Brier {brier:.3f}  ECE {ece(p, y[test]):.3f}")
```

```text
model     acc 0.594  NLL 1.455  Brier 0.634  ECE 0.236
T = 2.86  acc 0.594  NLL 0.991  Brier 0.543  ECE 0.021
truth     acc 0.616  NLL 0.932  Brier 0.510  ECE 0.014
```

The model's confidence exceeds its accuracy by about 24 percentage points on average. Temperature scaling with $T = 2.86$ leaves the accuracy at 0.594, lowers the test NLL from 1.455 to 0.991 and the ECE from 0.236 to 0.021. The chosen temperature is a little larger than the factor 2.5 used to build the model, because the noise added to $z$ also spreads the logits. The last row scores the true probabilities, which no model can beat in expectation: their NLL of 0.932 and Brier score of 0.510 are the irreducible terms of Section 2.2, estimated on 3000 labels.

### 3.5 What the ECE does not tell

The last row of the output shows the first limitation: the true probabilities, which are calibrated by construction, have an ECE of 0.014, not 0. The binned estimator is biased upward on a finite sample, because the absolute value turns sampling noise in each bin into a positive contribution, and its value depends on the number and placement of bins (Nixon et al., 2019). Differences in ECE of the size of this bias should not be read as differences in calibration.

The second limitation is more serious: the ECE is not a proper scoring rule. A classifier that ignores its input and outputs the class frequencies of the training set has an ECE near zero and no discrimination at all. The ECE measures one property, calibration, and must be reported alongside a proper score (which also rewards sharpness) and the accuracy. The proper scores contain calibration as a component: Murphy (1973) decomposed the Brier score into a reliability term, which is zero for calibrated forecasts, minus a resolution term that rewards forecasts which separate cases with different outcome frequencies, plus the irreducible uncertainty of the labels.

Third, calibration on the test distribution says nothing about calibration under distribution shift. Ovadia et al. (2019) compared several of the methods of this series on image and text benchmarks under increasing amounts of shift and reported that calibration degraded for all of them, with deep ensembles degrading least in their experiments. An evaluation of predictive uncertainty that matters for deployment includes data from the kinds of shift that deployment will bring.

## 4. Selective classification

### 4.1 The reject option

A classifier with a **reject option** may abstain on an input instead of predicting, for instance by passing it to a person. Chow (1970) analyzed the trade-off between errors and rejections: when the true posterior class probabilities are known, a mistake costs 1 and a rejection costs $c$ with $0 < c < 1/2$, the decision rule that minimizes expected cost predicts the most probable class when its probability is at least $1 - c$ and rejects otherwise. In practice the true posterior is replaced by the model's predictive distribution, and the question becomes how well its confidence ranks correct predictions above incorrect ones.

### 4.2 Coverage and selective risk

A **selective classifier** pairs a classifier with a score $s(x)$ and a threshold $\tau$, and answers only when $s(x) \ge \tau$. Two quantities describe it (Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017):

- the **coverage**, the fraction of inputs it answers;
- the **selective risk**, its error rate on the answered inputs.

Lowering $\tau$ raises the coverage and, with a good score, the risk. Sorting the test inputs by decreasing score and computing the risk of the top fraction for every fraction gives the **risk–coverage curve**. Its average height, the area under the risk–coverage curve (AURC), summarizes the ranking quality in one number, lower being better (Geifman, Uziel and El-Yaniv, 2019). Two reference curves bound it: an **oracle** that rejects exactly the wrong predictions first, and a random order, whose risk equals the overall error rate at every coverage.

### 4.3 Worked example

The listing continues the previous one and uses the temperature-scaled confidence as the score.

```python
p = softmax(logits[test] / T)
correct = p.argmax(axis=1) == y[test]

def risk_coverage(score, correct):
    order = np.argsort(-score, kind="stable")         # most confident first
    errors = np.cumsum(~correct[order])
    kept = np.arange(1, len(order) + 1)
    return kept / len(order), errors / kept           # coverage, selective risk

cov, risk = risk_coverage(p.max(axis=1), correct)
_, risk_oracle = risk_coverage(correct.astype(float), correct)
for c in (1.0, 0.8, 0.5, 0.2):
    i = int(round(c * len(cov))) - 1
    print(f"coverage {c:.1f}: risk {risk[i]:.3f}  oracle {risk_oracle[i]:.3f}")

_, risk_raw = risk_coverage(softmax(logits[test]).max(axis=1), correct)
correct_true = p_true[test].argmax(axis=1) == y[test]
_, risk_true = risk_coverage(p_true[test].max(axis=1), correct_true)
print(f"AURC scaled {risk.mean():.3f}  unscaled {risk_raw.mean():.3f}  "
      f"true probabilities {risk_true.mean():.3f}  oracle {risk_oracle.mean():.3f}")
```

```text
coverage 1.0: risk 0.406  oracle 0.406
coverage 0.8: risk 0.365  oracle 0.258
coverage 0.5: risk 0.294  oracle 0.000
coverage 0.2: risk 0.180  oracle 0.000
AURC scaled 0.275  unscaled 0.277  true probabilities 0.238  oracle 0.097
```

Answering only the most confident half of the inputs lowers the error rate from 0.406 to 0.294; answering the most confident fifth lowers it to 0.180. Two further observations are worth more than the numbers themselves.

First, temperature scaling barely changes the AURC (0.277 before, 0.275 after), although it improved the ECE tenfold. Calibration and ranking are different properties: a monotone rescaling of confidence repairs the first and leaves the second almost untouched. (For two classes it leaves the ranking exactly unchanged; with more classes, dividing the logits by $T$ can reorder a few inputs.)

Second, even the true probabilities reach only an AURC of 0.238, far from the oracle's 0.097. The labels in this example are drawn at random from the true probabilities, so many errors are **aleatoric**: no model can foresee them, and no score can reject them selectively. The gap between the scaled model and the true probabilities, 0.275 against 0.238, is the part that better modeling could still recover.

### 4.4 Choosing the score and the threshold

The threshold is chosen on validation data to meet a target, either a maximum selective risk or a minimum coverage, and then checked once on the test set. Geifman and El-Yaniv (2017) gave a procedure that chooses it with a high-probability guarantee on the selective risk. Conformal prediction, which outputs sets of labels rather than abstaining, offers a different distribution-free guarantee on coverage of the true label (Vovk, Gammerman and Shafer, 2005).

For the score itself, the maximum predictive probability of the posterior predictive is the natural default, and the entropy of the predictive distribution is a common alternative. Which score separates errors best is an empirical question for each model and data set; learned combinations of several uncertainty features have been proposed as well. When the inputs to be rejected include ones unlike the training data, a score that isolates the epistemic part of the uncertainty, introduced next, is the better-motivated choice, although it is not guaranteed to work better.

## 5. Active learning and mutual information

### 5.1 The problem

In **pool-based active learning** a model is trained on a small labeled set, a large pool of unlabeled inputs is available and labels are expensive. At each round an **acquisition function** scores the pool inputs, the highest-scoring ones are sent for labeling, and the model is retrained. The aim is to reach a given accuracy with fewer labels than random selection would need (Settles, 2012).

An input is worth labeling if its label would teach the model something. Predictive entropy is a tempting score, but it is high for two different reasons: the model may not know the answer, or the answer may be inherently random. Labeling an input of the second kind teaches nothing, because a coin flip stays a coin flip however many times it is observed.

### 5.2 The decomposition and BALD

A posterior over $\theta$ separates the two reasons. The entropy of the posterior predictive splits as

$$
\underbrace{H\!\left[\,\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})}\, p(y \mid x, \theta)\,\right]}_{\text{total}}
=
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})}\, H\!\left[\,p(y \mid x, \theta)\,\right]}_{\text{expected entropy}}
+
\underbrace{I\big(y; \theta \mid x, \mathcal{D}\big)}_{\text{mutual information}} ,
$$

where $H[\cdot]$ is the entropy of a distribution over $y$ and $I(y; \theta \mid x, \mathcal{D})$ the mutual information between the label of $x$ and the parameters. The expected entropy is high when each plausible parameter value predicts a random label, which is the aleatoric part. The mutual information is high when plausible parameter values disagree, which is the epistemic part; it is also the expected reduction in the entropy of the posterior over $\theta$ from observing $y$, the information gain that Lindley (1956) proposed for choosing experiments. Housby et al. (2011) showed that computing it in the output space, as written above, is far cheaper than in parameter space, and named the acquisition function **Bayesian active learning by disagreement** (BALD). Gal, Islam and Ghahramani (2017) applied it to image classifiers with Monte Carlo dropout.

With $M$ samples $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_M$ from an approximate posterior, or $M$ members of an ensemble, each giving class probabilities $p_m = p(y \mid x, \theta_m)$, the estimate is

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{BALD}}(x) = H\!\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} p_m\right] - \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} H[p_m] .
$$

### 5.3 Worked example

The listing scores three pool inputs from the probabilities that four posterior samples assign to two classes.

```python
import numpy as np

def entropy(p):
    return -np.sum(p * np.log(np.clip(p, 1e-12, 1.0)), axis=-1)

# Class probabilities from M = 4 posterior samples (rows) for three pool inputs.
pool = {
    "A, members agree, confident": [[0.95, 0.05]] * 4,
    "B, members agree, 50/50":     [[0.50, 0.50]] * 4,
    "C, members disagree":         [[0.95, 0.05], [0.05, 0.95], [0.90, 0.10], [0.10, 0.90]],
}
for name, probs in pool.items():
    probs = np.array(probs)
    total = entropy(probs.mean(axis=0))          # entropy of the posterior predictive
    aleatoric = entropy(probs).mean()            # expected entropy under the posterior
    print(f"{name:28s} total {total:.3f}  expected {aleatoric:.3f}  BALD {total - aleatoric:.3f}")
```

```text
A, members agree, confident  total 0.199  expected 0.199  BALD 0.000
B, members agree, 50/50      total 0.693  expected 0.693  BALD 0.000
C, members disagree          total 0.693  expected 0.262  BALD 0.431
```

Inputs B and C have the same predictive distribution, $(0.5, 0.5)$, and therefore the same total entropy of $\log 2 = 0.693$ nats. Predictive entropy cannot tell them apart. BALD can: for B all samples agree that the label is a coin flip, so its label carries no information about $\theta$, while for C each sample is confident but they disagree, and the label would rule half of them out. BALD ranks C first and B with A at the bottom.

### 5.4 Practical problems

Several difficulties separate this clean picture from working active learning.

- **Batches.** Labeling usually happens in batches, and the top $b$ inputs by BALD are often near-duplicates that carry the same information. BatchBALD scores the mutual information of the whole batch jointly to avoid this (Kirsch, van Amersfoort and Gal, 2019).
- **The target.** BALD rewards information about $\theta$, including about parameters that do not affect predictions on the inputs that matter. The expected predictive information gain instead measures information about predictions on a target distribution of inputs (Bickford Smith, Kirsch, Farquhar, Gal, Foster and Rainforth, 2023).
- **Quality of the posterior.** The mutual information is only as good as the samples behind it. With few samples, or an approximation that underestimates the spread of the posterior (as mean-field VI tends to, see the [fourth entry](/blog/variational-inference-for-neural-networks)), the epistemic term is underestimated.
- **Biased data.** Actively chosen labels are not an i.i.d. sample, so the average training loss on them is a biased estimate of the risk. Farquhar, Gal and Rainforth (2021) analyze when this bias matters and give corrective weights.
- **Cost and cold start.** Retraining after every batch is expensive for large networks, and with very few initial labels the posterior, and therefore the acquisition function, is unreliable. Random selection is the baseline any reported gain must beat.

## 6. Large language models

### 6.1 Why the setting is different

Large language models (LLMs) bring three complications. The parameter count, in the billions, makes inference over all weights impractical with the methods of this series. The output is a sequence of tokens, so the predictive distribution lives on an enormous discrete space. And many different sequences can be equally correct answers, which makes "the probability of the correct output" ill defined.

Pre-trained language models have been reported to be fairly well calibrated on multiple-choice questions when their answer probabilities are read directly (Kadavath et al., 2022). Fine-tuning a large model on a small task data set, on the other hand, can leave it overconfident, which is where much of the Bayesian work has concentrated.

### 6.2 Bayesian fine-tuning and variational training

**Low-rank adaptation** (LoRA) fine-tunes a frozen pre-trained weight matrix $W$ by learning a low-rank update $\Delta W = BA$, where $B$ and $A$ have a small inner dimension $r$ (Hu et al., 2022). The adapters have few parameters, so the methods of earlier entries apply to them. Yang et al. (2024) fitted a Laplace approximation over the LoRA parameters after fine-tuning, the post-hoc approach of the [third entry](/blog/laplace-approximations), and reported clearly better calibration on small multiple-choice reasoning tasks at little cost in accuracy. Ensembles of independently fine-tuned adapters are the corresponding application of the [fifth entry](/blog/deep-ensembles), and are cheap to store because only the adapters differ.

Variational learning has also been run at full scale. Shen et al. (2024) trained networks up to the size of a GPT-2 model with a natural-gradient variational method (IVON), at a cost close to that of the Adam optimizer, and reported predictive performance and calibration comparable to or better than Adam's. Whether such methods give large benefits at the largest scales remains an open empirical question.

### 6.3 Uncertainty of generated text

For a sequence output, a model can average the per-token distributions of several posterior samples while generating (token-level averaging) or average the probabilities of complete sequences (sequence-level averaging); the two define different predictive distributions, and Malinin and Gales (2021) analyze both and their entropy and mutual-information estimates.

A further problem is that many sequences mean the same thing. If a model answers "Paris", "It is Paris." and "The capital is Paris" with probability one third each, the entropy over sequences is high although the model is certain of the answer. **Semantic entropy** groups sampled answers into clusters of equivalent meaning, using a separate model to judge equivalence, and computes the entropy over clusters (Kuhn, Gal and Farquhar, 2023). Its authors report that it predicts the correctness of answers to free-form questions better than sequence-level entropy. Like the decomposition of Section 5.2, it can be combined with posterior samples to separate the epistemic part, although most uses so far apply it to a single point-estimated model.

## 7. Uncertainty passed to downstream models

Predictive uncertainty is also used as an input to a further computation rather than as a number shown to a person. Three settings recur.

- **Missing data.** When some measurements in a record are missing, drawing several completions from a predictive distribution for the missing values, and analyzing each completed data set, propagates the uncertainty about them into the final result, which a single filled-in value would hide. Whether this is valid depends on why the data are missing; Rubin (1976) gave the conditions. Deep generative models such as variational autoencoders (Kingma and Welling, 2014) can learn such a predictive distribution for high-dimensional records, for example in biomedical data sets.
- **Spatio-temporal forecasting.** In environmental and physical forecasting, a decision often depends on the probability of an extreme value at a location and time, which a point forecast cannot provide. Predictive distributions over space and time, from Gaussian processes (sixth entry, [Gaussian processes](/blog/gaussian-processes-and-kernels)) or Bayesian neural networks, supply it, and proper scores evaluate it.
- **Bayesian optimization.** To optimize an expensive function, a probabilistic surrogate model predicts its value and uncertainty at untried points, and an acquisition function such as expected improvement trades the predicted value against the uncertainty to choose the next evaluation (Jones, Schonlau and Welch, 1998). Gaussian processes are the classical surrogate; Bayesian neural networks and ensembles are used when the input space is large.

In each case the downstream result is only as reliable as the calibration of the uncertainty fed into it, which is why the evaluation of Sections 2 and 3 comes first.

## 8. Software

The examples in this series use NumPy only, so that every computation is visible. Larger models are better served by libraries, which fall into four groups.

- **Probabilistic programming languages** let a model be written as a generative program and provide general inference algorithms, usually Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) with the No-U-Turn sampler and variational inference (VI). Examples are Stan (Carpenter et al., 2017), PyMC, Pyro (Bingham et al., 2019), NumPyro and TensorFlow Probability.
- **Libraries for approximate inference in neural networks** add a specific method to an existing deep learning model: for example Laplace approximations in PyTorch with the library described by Daxberger et al. (2021), or variational layers.
- **Diagnostics and evaluation** tools compute convergence diagnostics for samplers, calibration measures and proper scores. ArviZ is a widely used library for the first. Standard convergence diagnostics are hard to interpret for neural-network posteriors, whose symmetries ([seventh entry](/blog/priors-symmetries-and-identifiability)) produce many equivalent modes.
- **Downstream toolkits** use predictive uncertainty for decisions, such as BoTorch for Bayesian optimization (Balandat et al., 2020) and several active-learning libraries.

This entry names no versions: these libraries change quickly, and their documentation, not an article, is the place to check which methods and versions are current. When reporting results, state the library and version used, as the series does for Python and NumPy.

## 9. Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A binary classifier always outputs probability 0.3 for class 1, and 30% of the test labels are class 1. What are its ECE (with any binning) and its accuracy? What does this say about using the ECE to choose between models?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

All predictions fall in one bin with confidence 0.7 (for class 0), and 70% of the labels are class 0, so the accuracy is 0.7 and the ECE is $\lvert 0.7 - 0.7 \rvert = 0$. The classifier ignores its input. A model chosen by smallest ECE could be this one; the ECE must be reported with a proper score, which this classifier does poorly on compared with any model that discriminates.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Using the expected Brier score of Section 2.2, show that for $p = (0.7, 0.3)$ the forecasts $(0.9, 0.1)$ and $(0.5, 0.5)$ have the same expected Brier score, and compute it.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The irreducible term is $0.7 \times 0.3 + 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.42$. For $(0.9, 0.1)$ the second term is $0.2^2 + 0.2^2 = 0.08$; for $(0.5, 0.5)$ it is $0.2^2 + 0.2^2 = 0.08$ as well. Both give $0.50$.

</details>

**Exercise 3.** Under Chow's rule with true posteriors, a rejection costs $c = 0.2$. For which maximum class probabilities does the classifier predict, and why does the rule require $c < 1/2$ to be useful for two classes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It predicts when the maximum probability is at least $1 - c = 0.8$ and rejects otherwise, because predicting costs the error probability $1 - \max_k p_k$ on average and rejecting costs $c$. With two classes the maximum probability is never below 0.5, so the error probability is at most 0.5; if $c \ge 1/2$ rejecting is never cheaper than predicting.

</details>

**Exercise 4.** Two posterior samples give class probabilities $(0.8, 0.2)$ and $(0.2, 0.8)$ for an input. Compute the total entropy, the expected entropy and the BALD score in nats.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The predictive distribution is $(0.5, 0.5)$, with entropy $\log 2 = 0.693$. Each sample has entropy $-(0.8 \log 0.8 + 0.2 \log 0.2) = 0.179 + 0.322 = 0.500$, so the expected entropy is 0.500 and BALD is $0.693 - 0.500 = 0.193$ nats.

</details>

**Exercise 5.** In the selective-classification example, why could no confidence score, however well calibrated, bring the AURC down to the oracle's 0.097?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The labels were drawn at random from the true class probabilities, so some predictions of the most probable class are wrong by chance. A score computed from the input cannot know which of two identical-looking inputs received the unlikely label. The true probabilities, the best possible score here, reach 0.238; the oracle uses the labels themselves.

</details>

## 10. Summary

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What makes a predictive distribution good? | Calibration, sharpness and discrimination; accuracy measures none of them. |
| How is it scored? | With strictly proper scoring rules, such as the log score (NLL) and the Brier score, whose expectation is minimized only by the true distribution. |
| What does the ECE measure? | The binned gap between confidence and accuracy; it is biased on finite samples, depends on the binning and is not proper. |
| What is temperature scaling? | Dividing the logits by a validation-fitted $T$; it improves calibration without changing accuracy and is the baseline for Bayesian methods. |
| How is uncertainty used to abstain? | A selective classifier answers only above a score threshold; the risk–coverage curve and its area measure the ranking. |
| What does BALD select? | Inputs with high mutual information between label and parameters, where posterior samples disagree, rather than inputs that are merely noisy. |
| What is special about LLMs? | Scale, sequence outputs and many equivalent answers; Bayesian adapters, variational training and semantic entropy address them. |

The worked examples separated three properties that are easy to conflate. Temperature scaling repaired calibration without touching accuracy or ranking, the risk–coverage curve exposed the ranking, and the BALD example showed that two inputs with the same predictive distribution can be worth very different amounts of new data. The [final entry](/blog/frontiers-of-bayesian-deep-learning) surveys the research directions that build on these ideas, from low-dimensional subspaces and prior-fitted networks to singular learning theory, imprecise probabilities and Bayesian reinforcement learning, and returns to the objectives of the series.
