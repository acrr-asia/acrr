PAC-Bayesian analysis is a frequentist technique for deriving generalization bounds for randomized classifiers. As a special case, PAC-Bayesian analysis can be used to provide generalization bounds for Bayesian classifiers. The chapter provides a basic introduction to PAC-Bayesian analysis and discusses similarities and distinctions from Bayesian learning. It then discusses ensemble classifiers and weighted majority votes and provides first and second order oracle bounds on the expected loss of weighted majority votes. The oracle bounds relate the expected loss of a weighted ensemble to the expected loss of a randomized classifier. Since the expected loss of a randomized classifier is an unknown (oracle) quantity, the bounds are called oracle bounds. PAC-Bayesian analysis is applied to bound the expected loss of randomized classifiers in terms of their empirical loss, thereby converting oracle bounds into empirical ones. The empirical bounds can be used to provide generalization guarantees, as well as optimize weighting of weighted majority votes.

## 1. A basic introduction to PAC-Bayesian analysis

PAC-Bayesian analysis was invented by Shawe-Taylor and Williamson and McAllester. Their motivation was to introduce a possibility to incorporate prior knowledge within the frequentist learning framework. The name *PAC-Bayesian* is derived from *PAC*, standing for *Probably Approximately Correct* learning framework proposed by Valiant, and *Bayesian*, which refers to the method's ability to incorporate prior knowledge in the analysis. PAC-Bayesian analysis is a technique for deriving frequentist generalization bounds for randomized classifiers. A *randomized classifier* defined by a distribution $\rho$ on a set of parameters $\Theta$ acts by sampling a new parameter vector $\theta \in \Theta$ according to $\rho(\theta)$ each time it classifies a new data point.

**Definition 22.1** (Randomized Classifier). *Let* $\rho$ *be a distribution over* $\Theta$. *A* randomized classifier *associated with* $\rho$ *(and named* $\rho$*) is defined by the following protocol. At each prediction round it:*

1. *Samples* $\theta \in \Theta$ *according to* $\rho(\theta)$
2. *Observes* $x$
3. *Returns* $f(x, \theta)$

$\ell(f(x, \theta), y)$ denotes a loss function for predicting $f(x, \theta)$ when the true label is $y$. In contrast to most other chapters in the book, where $\ell$ is the negative log-likelihood, most results in this chapter assume that $\ell(\cdot, \cdot)$ is bounded in the $[0, 1]$ interval. For example, $\ell(\cdot, \cdot)$ could be the zero-one loss function. $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\ell(f(x, \theta), y)]$ denotes the expected loss of $\theta$ and $\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \ell(f(x_i, \theta), y_i)$ the empirical loss of $\theta$. Then the expected loss of $\rho$ is $\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\rho}[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]$ and the empirical loss of $\rho$ is $\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\rho}\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]$. Whenever it causes no confusion, the notation is shortened to $\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]$ and $\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]$.

There exist numerous forms of PAC-Bayesian bounds. The first one considered here is due to McAllester, which is convenient for interpreting the nature of PAC-Bayes. It is a relaxation of a tighter bound of Seeger that will be shown later in Theorem 22.3, together with a proof of both results.

**Theorem 22.1** (Interpretation-friendly PAC-Bayesian inequality). *Assume that the loss function* $\ell$ *is bounded in the* $[0, 1]$ *interval. For any "prior" distribution* $\pi$ *over* $\Theta$ *that is independent of* $\mathcal{D}$ *and any* $\delta \in (0, 1]$

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(\forall \rho : \mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)] \le \mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right] + \sqrt{\frac{2\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]\left(\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}\right)}{N}} + \frac{2\left(\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}\right)}{N}\right) \ge 1 - \delta. \tag{22.1}
$$

It is worth emphasizing that Theorem 22.1 is a frequentist generalization bound and the random variable inside the probability statement is $\mathcal{D}$ (and thus $\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]$), but not $\theta$ (and thus not $\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]$).

The result of the theorem can be restated verbally by saying that with probability at least $1 - \delta$, for all "posterior" distributions $\rho$

$$
\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)] \le \mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right] + \sqrt{\frac{2\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]\left(\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}\right)}{N}} + \frac{2\left(\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}\right)}{N}. \tag{22.2}
$$

"Prior" and "posterior" are written in quotation marks, because the semantic meaning of PAC-Bayesian priors and posteriors differs from Bayesian priors and posteriors. In PAC-Bayes a prior distribution can be any distribution that does not depend on the data $\mathcal{D}$ used in the computation of the bound. It can be used to represent a prior belief on the performance $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ of different parameters $\theta$, but the bound holds irrespective of the source of $\pi$. And the bound holds for all posterior distributions $\rho$. In particular, it allows for the selection of $\rho$ based on the data $\mathcal{D}$. The bound also holds for the Bayes posterior, but is not restricted to it.

Since Theorem 22.1 holds for all posterior distributions $\rho$, it can be used to find a distribution $\rho$ that yields the best guarantee on the expected loss $\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]$ according to the bound in equation (22.2). A distribution $\rho$ that minimizes the bound should balance between placing a high mass on the parameters $\theta$ with a low empirical risk $\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})$ in order to minimize the $\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]$ term, and staying close to the prior distribution $\pi$ in the $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ sense to keep $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)$ under control. The $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)$ term measures the complexity of a distribution $\rho$. If $\rho = \pi$, then $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) = 0$, meaning that if the posterior takes no information from the sample and remains the same as the prior, then the complexity is zero. While the bound holds for the Bayes posterior, it is typically *not* the Bayes posterior that minimizes the right hand side of equation (22.2).

The prior distribution $\pi$ is a tool to steer learning before revealing the data. If the prior allocates high probability mass to a subset of the parameter space that happens to work well on the data (happens to achieve low $\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})$ after the data is revealed), the posterior $\rho$ minimizing the right hand side of equation (22.2) will not need to deviate much from $\pi$, leading to a small $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)$ term and a tight bound. Otherwise the bound might be loose, but it always remains a valid bound.

The bound in Theorem 22.1 provides a "fast convergence rate". Specifically, if for some $\rho$ the empirical risk $\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]$ happens to be of order $O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)$, the last two terms in equation (22.2) scale with $\frac{1}{N}$, which is known as "fast rate", as opposed to scaling of the middle term with $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$ for large $\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]$, which is known as "slow rate". In other words, when $\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]$ is small, with high probability it is even closer to $\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]$ than when it is large. In the context of deep learning, where reaching zero empirical risk is often possible, it provides an extra benefit for allocating higher posterior mass to well-performing parameters and bringing $\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]$ close to zero.

Theorem 22.1 is convenient for explaining the nature of PAC-Bayes, but it is not the most convenient for optimization, because the bound is not convex in $\rho$. The following result by Thiemann et al. provides an optimization-friendly version of the bound. Similar to Theorem 22.1, it is a relaxation of Theorem 22.3 that is discussed later, and it is at least as tight as Theorem 22.1 (see Tolstikhin and Seldin).

**Theorem 22.2** (Optimization-friendly PAC-Bayesian inequality). *Assume that the loss function* $\ell$ *is bounded in the* $[0, 1]$ *interval. For any prior distribution* $\pi$ *over* $\Theta$ *that is independent of* $\mathcal{D}$ *and any* $\delta \in (0, 1]$

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(\forall \rho : \mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)] \le \min_{\lambda \in (0,2)} \left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]}{1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}} + \frac{\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}}{\lambda\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}\right)N}\right)\right) \ge 1 - \delta. \tag{22.3}
$$

In other words, with probability at least $1 - \delta$ over the draw of a sample $\mathcal{D}$, for all distributions $\rho$ over $\Theta$ and all $\lambda \in (0, 2)$

$$
\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)] \le \frac{\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]}{1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}} + \frac{\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}}{\lambda\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}\right)N}. \tag{22.4}
$$

For a fixed $\lambda$ the right hand side of the inequality (22.4) is convex in $\rho$ (because $\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]$ is linear in $\rho$ and $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)$ is convex in $\rho$), and for a fixed $\rho$ it is convex in $\lambda$ (see Tolstikhin and Seldin). Therefore, the bound in Theorem 22.2 is amenable to alternating minimization. For a fixed $\lambda$ the bound is minimized by

$$
\rho^*_\lambda(\theta) = \frac{\pi(\theta)e^{-\lambda N \hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})}}{\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[e^{-\lambda N \hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta', \mathcal{D})}\right]} \tag{22.5}
$$

(the derivation is left as an exercise) and for a fixed $\rho$ the bound is minimized by

$$
\lambda^*_\rho = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\frac{2N\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]}{\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}} + 1} + 1} \tag{22.6}
$$

(see Tolstikhin and Seldin). Alternating application of the update rules (22.5) and (22.6) leads to a local minimum of the bound (22.4). Note that the bound (22.4) is not jointly convex in $\rho$ and $\lambda$, which would have been a sufficient, but not a necessary condition for convergence to the global minimum. Thiemann et al. provide sufficient conditions under which the bound (22.4) is strongly quasi-convex in $\rho$ and $\lambda$, which guarantees convergence of alternating minimization to the global minimum, as well as examples of cases where the strong quasi-convexity conditions are violated and the procedure only converges to a local minimum.

### 1.1 Similarities and differences between PAC-Bayesian analysis and Bayesian learning

Theorems 22.1 and 22.2 provide sufficient background to compare PAC-Bayesian analysis to Bayesian learning. The similarities come first, followed by the differences.

#### Similarities to Bayesian learning

1. Similar to Bayesian learning, PAC-Bayesian analysis provides an explicit way to incorporate prior knowledge via a prior distribution $\pi$.
2. Similar to Bayesian learning, it also allows sequential updates of the prior knowledge $\pi$ as more data become available. See the work of Wu et al. on Recursive PAC-Bayes, which is a procedure for sequential prior knowledge update without loss of confidence information.

#### Differences with Bayesian learning

1. PAC-Bayesian analysis is a frequentist learning framework. The random variables in PAC-Bayesian analysis are the observations (the data $\mathcal{D}$), but not the parameters $\theta$. The prior $\pi$ in PAC-Bayesian analysis is just an auxiliary construction for deriving the bound (as will be formally shown in the next section). It is not a prior belief in the Bayesian sense on the distribution from which the parameters of a "true" model generating the data were sampled. PAC-Bayesian bounds are valid for any choice of the prior. The choice of a prior impacts the tightness of the bound, but never compromises its validity, as long as the prior is independent of the data on which the bound is computed.
2. PAC-Bayesian posteriors are averaging distributions $\rho(\theta)$ and the bounds hold for all possible posterior distributions $\rho(\theta)$. Bayesian posteriors are conditional distributions $\rho(\theta|\mathcal{D})$. Given a Bayesian prior $\pi(\theta)$ and a data set $\mathcal{D}$, the Bayesian posterior is uniquely determined. Thus, the semantics of the PAC-Bayesian and Bayesian posteriors are different.
3. The loss function $\ell$ is a central element in PAC-Bayesian analysis, because the PAC-Bayesian objective is to bound the expected risk $\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]$. To work with a PAC-Bayesian bound, one has to define a loss function, and a change of the loss function changes the minimizer $\rho$ of the bound. In contrast, the definition of the Bayes rule, $\rho(\theta|\mathcal{D}) = \pi(\theta)\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D}|\theta)/\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D})$, is not based on a loss function.
4. To draw further parallels and distinctions between Bayesian and PAC-Bayesian learning, assume that $f(x, \theta)$ returns a probability distribution on $\mathcal{Y}$ and that the loss function $\ell$ is the negative log-likelihood. Then the conditional data probability $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D}|\theta)$ can be written as $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D}|\theta) = e^{-N\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})}$ and the Bayesian posterior can be written as $\rho(\theta) = \frac{\pi(\theta)e^{-N\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})}}{\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[e^{-N\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta', \mathcal{D})}\right]}$, which corresponds to taking $\lambda = 1$ in equation (22.5). This connection is useful to underline two important differences.
   - (a) PAC-Bayesian analysis relies on convergence of empirical estimates of the risk $\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})$ to their expectations $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$. The convergence holds for bounded loss functions and, therefore, the assumption that the loss function is bounded in the $[0, 1]$ interval is common in the PAC-Bayesian literature. Unbounded loss functions, including the negative log-likelihood, do not guarantee such convergence by default and require extra care. For example, smoothing of the posterior distribution, as done by Seldin and Tishby, or introduction of assumptions on the data generating distribution that would guarantee convergence of empirical means to their expectations. Therefore, in case of unbounded loss functions application of PAC-Bayesian bounds to provide generalization guarantees for Bayesian posteriors may demand extra analysis, assumptions on the data generating distribution, and/or posterior adjustments. Since Bayesian inference is not directly concerned with concentration of empirical estimates of the losses (and loss functions in general), unboundedness of the loss function is not a direct concern for it.
   - (b) The second distinction is that the optimal value of $\lambda$ in PAC-Bayesian bounds is typically smaller than 1, meaning that they weigh prior belief $\pi(\theta)$ and evidence differently from Bayesian learning, and if the loss is not the negative log-likelihood, then the shape of the optimal posterior might be altogether completely different.
5. The Bayes rule requires access to the prior $\pi(\theta)$ to compute the posterior $\rho(\theta|\mathcal{D})$. In PAC-Bayesian bounds $\pi$ appears only in $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)$ and, therefore, it is sufficient to have an upper bound on $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)$ to work with the bounds. For example, Lever et al. define $\pi(\theta)$ in terms of the true data generating distribution. Such $\pi(\theta)$ is valid, because it does not depend on the data, but it is not accessible to the learner. Nevertheless, Lever et al. show that if both the prior and the posterior are Gibbs distributions, it is possible to bound $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)$ using a self-bounding technique and work with the bound without direct access to $\pi(\theta)$.

### 1.2 PAC-Bayes-kl inequality

This section presents the PAC-Bayes-kl inequality due to Seeger (with a slight improvement by Maurer). The inequality is at the basis of Theorems 22.1 and 22.2 discussed earlier, and will be followed by a proof. In order to proceed, the binary kl-divergence needs to be defined, which is a shorthand for the standard KL divergence (see [Chapter 12, Section 1](/blog/introduction-to-variational-inference)) between two Bernoulli distributions.

**Definition 22.2** (Binary kl-divergence). *Let* $p$ *and* $q$ *be biases of two Bernoulli random variables. The binary* $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}$ *divergence is defined as:*

$$
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}(p\|q) = \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}([1 - p, p]\|[1 - q, q]) = p\ln\frac{p}{q} + (1 - p)\ln\frac{1 - p}{1 - q}. \tag{22.7}
$$

Pay attention that the parameters of $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot\|\cdot)$ are two probability distributions, whereas the parameters of $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}(\cdot\|\cdot)$ are two numbers in the $[0, 1]$ interval. PAC-Bayes-kl inequality presented next involves binary kl divergence between the empirical and the expected loss of a randomized classifier $\rho$ (which are two numbers in $[0, 1]$) and the standard KL divergence between the posterior and the prior (which are two distributions).

**Theorem 22.3** (PAC-Bayes-kl inequality). *Assume that the loss function* $\ell$ *is bounded in the* $[0, 1]$ *interval. For any prior distribution* $\pi$ *over* $\Theta$ *that is independent of* $\mathcal{D}$ *and any* $\delta \in (0, 1]$:

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(\exists \rho : \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}\left(\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]\middle\|\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]\right) \ge \frac{\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}}{N}\right) \le \delta. \tag{22.8}
$$

Theorem 22.1 follows from Theorem 22.3 by the refined Pinsker's inequality. The proof of this statement is left as an exercise.

**Lemma 22.1** (Refined Pinsker's inequality). *If* $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}(p\|q) \le \varepsilon$ *then*

$$
q \le p + \sqrt{2p\varepsilon} + 2\varepsilon. \tag{22.9}
$$

Theorem 22.2 also follows from Theorem 22.3 by another form of the refined Pinsker's inequality.

**Lemma 22.2** (Variational form of the refined Pinsker's inequality). *If* $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}(p\|q) \le \varepsilon$ *then*

$$
q \le \min_{\lambda \in (0,2)} \left(\frac{p}{1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}} + \frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}\right)}\right). \tag{22.10}
$$

The proof of PAC-Bayes-kl inequality is based on change of measure inequality, which acts as a replacement of the union bound in continuous spaces. The change of measure inequality uses Jensen's inequality, which is presented first.

**Theorem 22.4** (Jensen's inequality). *If* $g$ *is a convex function and* $Z$ *a random variable, then*

$$
\mathbb{E}[g(Z)] \ge g(\mathbb{E}[Z]). \tag{22.11}
$$

The reader is referred to Cover and Thomas for a proof. The change of measure inequality comes next.

**Theorem 22.5** (Change of measure inequality). *For any measurable function* $g(\theta)$ *on* $\Theta$ *and any distributions* $\rho$ *and* $\pi$:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\rho}[g(\theta)] \le \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi}\left[e^{g(\theta)}\right]. \tag{22.12}
$$

*Proof.*

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\rho}[g(\theta)] &= \mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\rho}\left[\ln\left(\frac{\rho(\theta)}{\pi(\theta)} \times e^{g(\theta)} \times \frac{\pi(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)}\right)\right] && (22.13) \\
&= \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\rho}\left[\ln\left(e^{g(\theta)} \times \frac{\pi(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)}\right)\right] && (22.14) \\
&\le \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\rho}\left[e^{g(\theta)} \times \frac{\pi(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)}\right] && (22.15) \\
&= \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi}\left[e^{g(\theta)}\right], && (22.16)
\end{aligned}
$$

where the inequality in the third line is by Jensen's inequality. Note that there is nothing probabilistic in the statement of the theorem: it is a deterministic result. $\square$

The change of measure inequality can also be obtained from the variational formula by Donsker and Varadhan, which states that

$$
\ln\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi}\left[e^{g(\theta)}\right] = \sup_{\rho \ll \pi}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\rho}[g(\theta)] - \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)\right), \tag{22.17}
$$

where $\rho \ll \pi$ is used to denote absolute continuity of $\rho$ with respect to $\pi$, and $\ln\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi}\left[e^{g(\theta)}\right]$ is assumed to be finite. Note that taking $g(\theta) = \ln p(\mathcal{D}|\theta)$ in the change of measure inequality recovers the ELBO (Evidence Lower BOund) discussed in the earlier chapters.

The change of measure inequality is used in the PAC-Bayes lemma, which is the foundation of most PAC-Bayesian bounds. The PAC-Bayes lemma also uses Markov's inequality, so it comes first.

**Theorem 22.6** (Markov's inequality). *For any non-negative random variable* $Z$ *and* $\varepsilon > 0$

$$
\mathbb{P}(Z \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]}{\varepsilon}. \tag{22.18}
$$

*Equivalently (taking* $\delta = \frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]}{\varepsilon}$*), for any* $\delta \in (0, 1]$:

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(Z \ge \frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]}{\delta}\right) \le \delta. \tag{22.19}
$$

The reader is referred to Seldin for a proof; the PAC-Bayes lemma follows.

**Lemma 22.3** (PAC-Bayes lemma). *For any measurable function* $g : \Theta \times (\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y})^N \to \mathbb{R}$ *and any distribution* $\pi$ *on* $\Theta$ *that is independent of the sample* $\mathcal{D}$

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(\exists \rho : \mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\rho}[g(\theta, \mathcal{D})] \ge \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right]\right]}{\delta}\right) \le \delta, \tag{22.20}
$$

*where the probability is with respect to the draw of the sample* $\mathcal{D}$ *and* $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\cdot]$ *is the expectation with respect to the draw of* $\mathcal{D}$.

*Proof.*

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb{P}\left(\exists \rho : \mathbb{E}_\rho[g(\theta, \mathcal{D})] \ge \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right]\right]}{\delta}\right) && (22.21) \\
&\quad\le \mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right] \ge \frac{\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right]\right]}{\delta}\right) && (22.22) \\
&\quad= \mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right] \ge \frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right]\right]}{\delta}\right) && (22.23) \\
&\quad\le \delta, && (22.24)
\end{aligned}
$$

where equation (22.22) follows by the change of measure inequality (for a fixed $\pi$, $\mathbb{E}_\rho[g(h, S)] - \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) \le \ln\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right]$ for all $\rho$); in equation (22.23) the order of expectations can be exchanged, because $\pi$ is independent of $\mathcal{D}$; and in the last step Markov's inequality is applied to the random variable $Z = \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right]$.

An important point is that the change-of-measure inequality bounds $\mathbb{E}_\rho[g(h, S)] - \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)$ for all posterior distributions $\rho$ by a single quantity $\ln\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right]$ in a deterministic way, and the probabilistic argument is applied to a single random variable $\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right]$, avoiding the need to take a union bound. In this way, the change of measure inequality acts as a replacement of the union bound. $\square$

Various PAC-Bayesian inequalities are obtained from the PAC-Bayes lemma by making suitable choices of the function $g(\theta, \mathcal{D})$. A key consideration in the choice of $g(\theta, \mathcal{D})$ is the possibility to bound $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right]$. For example, Seldin et al. have used Hoeffding's lemma and Bernstein's lemma to derive PAC-Bayes-Hoeffding and PAC-Bayes-Bernstein inequalities, respectively. In PAC-Bayes-kl the choice is $g(\theta, \mathcal{D}) = N\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}\left(\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\middle\|\mathcal{L}(\theta)\right)$. The following lemma is used to control the expectation of the exponent of $g$.

**Lemma 22.4** (kl Lemma). *Let* $Z_1, \dots, Z_N$ *be independent identically distributed random variables with* $Z_1 \in [0, 1]$, $\mathbb{E}[Z_1] = p$, *and* $\hat{p} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i$. *Then*

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{N\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}(\hat{p}\|p)}\right] \le 2\sqrt{N}. \tag{22.25}
$$

The reader is referred to Maurer and to Seldin for a proof and to Seldin for examples of other choices of $g$.

Taking $Z_i = \ell(f(x_i, \theta), y_i)$, assuming that the loss function $\ell$ is bounded in $[0, 1]$, and applying Lemma 22.4, to the above choice of $g$, gives $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right] \le 2\sqrt{N}$, which is the last bit needed to present a proof of Theorem 22.3.

*Proof of Theorem 22.3.* Take $g(\theta, \mathcal{D}) = N\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}\left(\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\middle\|\mathcal{L}(\theta)\right)$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb{P}\left(\exists \rho : \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}\left(\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]\middle\|\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]\right) \ge \frac{\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}}{N}\right) \\
&\quad\le \mathbb{P}\left(\exists \rho : \mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}\left(\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\middle\|\mathcal{L}(\theta)\right)\right] \ge \frac{\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}}{N}\right) && (22.26) \\
&\quad= \mathbb{P}\left(\exists \rho : \mathbb{E}_\rho\left[N\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}\left(\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\middle\|\mathcal{L}(\theta)\right)\right] \ge \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}\right) && (22.27) \\
&\quad\le \mathbb{P}\left(\exists \rho : \mathbb{E}_\rho[g(\theta, \mathcal{D})] \ge \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right]\right]}{\delta}\right) && (22.28) \\
&\quad\le \delta. && (22.29)
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line of the derivation exploits convexity of $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}$ and Jensen's inequality, which allows the expectation to be taken outside of $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}$ on the left side of the inequality inside the probability clause. The second line multiplies both sides of the inequality by $N$. The third line applies the kl lemma (22.4), by which $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right] \le 2\sqrt{N}$, and so $\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[e^{g(\theta, \mathcal{D})}\right]\right] \le 2\sqrt{N}$. The last line is by the PAC-Bayes lemma (22.3).

Note that the requirement of boundedness of $\ell$ in the $[0, 1]$ interval is required in Lemma 22.4 and the requirement on independence of $\pi$ and $\mathcal{D}$ is required in Lemma 22.3. $\square$

## 2. Ensemble classifiers and weighted majority vote

An alternative to randomized classification from Definition 22.1 is to take a $\rho$-weighted majority vote of the predictions given by parameters $\theta \in \Theta$. This approach has two advantages. One is that it transforms a randomized prediction into a deterministic one. (Imagine hearing that a doctor has sampled parameters of a model to predict your diagnosis. While it may be a statistically sound approach, psychologically it may be unsettling to some people.) The second, more important one, is the *cancellation of errors effect*. Informally, if in a set of classification models the individual models err with probability smaller than $\frac{1}{2}$ and their errors are independent, the errors tend to cancel out. For example, consider binary classification and imagine that there is a finite set $\Theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_M)$ of size $M$, and that for every $\theta_i \in \Theta$ the probability of making an error on a random sample $(x, y)$ is $\frac{1}{2} - \varepsilon$ for a small $\varepsilon > 0$, which implies that $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{2} - \varepsilon$. Also, assume that for $i \neq j$ the predictions $f(x, \theta_i)$ and $f(x, \theta_j)$ are independent. Then a uniformly weighted majority vote of $f(x, \theta_1), \dots, f(x, \theta_M)$ will err only if at least half of the predictions are incorrect. By Hoeffding's inequality the probability of this happening is $\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^M \mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta_i) \neq y) \ge \frac{1}{2}M\right) \le e^{-2M\varepsilon^2}$, meaning that even for a small $M$ the error rate of a uniformly weighted majority vote will be much smaller than the $\frac{1}{2} - \varepsilon$ error rate of the individual classifiers.

In practice, the true error rates are unknown and are not guaranteed to be below $\frac{1}{2}$, and the errors are not necessarily independent. If the error rates of individual models are above $\frac{1}{2}$, taking a majority vote may deteriorate the prediction accuracy. It is possible to use PAC-Bayesian analysis to provide generalization bounds for weighted majority votes, as well as to optimize the weighting of classifiers $\rho$, as demonstrated next.

From this point on the discussion is restricted to multiclass classification, i.e., $\mathcal{Y}$ is finite. It is also assumed that $f(x, \theta) \in \mathcal{Y}$. Under these assumptions, prediction of $\rho$-weighted majority vote $\mathrm{MV}_\rho(x)$ is defined as

$$
\mathrm{MV}_\rho(x) = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\rho}[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) = y)], \tag{22.30}
$$

where ties are resolved arbitrarily. In words, $\mathrm{MV}_\rho(x)$ predicts the label that received the most $\rho$-weighted votes from the models $\theta \in \Theta$. In binary classification with $\mathcal{Y} = \{\pm 1\}$ and $f(x, \theta) \in \{\pm 1\}$, equation (22.30) can be simplified to

$$
\mathrm{MV}_\rho(x) = \operatorname{sign}(\mathbb{E}_\rho[f(x, \theta)]). \tag{22.31}
$$

## 3. Oracle bounds for the weighted majority vote

A key observation is that if a $\rho$-weighted majority vote made an error, then at least a $\rho$-weighted half of the predictors $\theta$ have erred. For the zero-one loss it implies

$$
\ell(\mathrm{MV}_\rho(x), y) \le \mathbf{1}(\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)] \ge 0.5). \tag{22.32}
$$

The observation leads to the following basic bound on the expected loss of the weighted majority vote.

**Lemma 22.5** (A basic bound on the expected loss of a weighted majority vote).

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) \le \mathbb{P}(\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)] \ge 0.5). \tag{22.33}
$$

*Proof.*

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) &= \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p(x,y)}[\ell(\mathrm{MV}_\rho(x), y)] && (22.34) \\
&\le \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p(x,y)}[\mathbf{1}(\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)] \ge 0.5)] && (22.35) \\
&= \mathbb{P}(\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)] \ge 0.5), && (22.36)
\end{aligned}
$$

where the first line is by the definition of $\mathcal{L}(\cdot)$, the second line is by equation (22.32), and the last holds since $\mathbf{1}(\cdot)$ is a Bernoulli random variable. $\square$

The basic bound leads to a variety of oracle bounds, which are all based on taking a random variable $Z = \mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)]$ and bounding the probability $\mathbb{P}(Z \ge 0.5)$ on the right hand side of equation (22.33). At large, there is a "first order oracle bound", which is based on the first moment of $Z$, and several variations of "second order oracle bounds", which are based on the second moment of $Z$. It is possible, but uncommon to use higher moments of $Z$ due to the statistical and computational difficulty of empirical estimation of higher moments.

### First order oracle bound

The first bound is so basic that it was called "a folk theorem".

**Theorem 22.7** (First order oracle bound).

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) \le 2\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]. \tag{22.37}
$$

*Proof.* By Lemma 22.5 and Markov's inequality (22.6)

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) &\le \mathbb{P}(\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)] \ge 0.5) && (22.38) \\
&\le 2\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p(x,y)}[\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)]] && (22.39) \\
&= 2\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p(x,y)}[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)]\right] && (22.40) \\
&= 2\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]. && (22.41)
\end{aligned}
$$

$\square$

The bound is called an *oracle* bound, because it relates the expected loss of the majority vote to the expected losses of individual classifiers, $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$, which are unknown quantities. The following section uses PAC-Bayes to convert oracle bounds into empirical bounds. However, a shortcoming of the first order bound is evident already at the oracle level. The first order oracle bound takes no interactions between predictions of different classifiers into account, whereas cancellation of errors is the major source of power of the majority vote. The minimum of the first order oracle bound is achieved by placing all the mass $\rho(\theta)$ on the best performing classifier $\theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)$, which "kills" the majority vote. Even though in practice the first order empirical bound presented in the next section is often tighter than higher order bounds, it has been shown that optimization of the weighting $\rho$ through minimization of the first order empirical bound typically degrades the expected loss $\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho)$ of the majority vote.

### Second order oracle bound

A line of works has sought to overcome the shortcoming of the first order oracle bound by applying the Chebyshev-Cantelli inequality instead of Markov's inequality to bound $\mathbb{P}(Z \ge 0.5)$, but they have suffered from statistical and computational inefficiency due to oracle quantities appearing in the denominator of the Chebyshev-Cantelli inequality. A breakthrough was achieved by Masegosa et al., who used a second order Markov's inequality.

**Theorem 22.8** (Second order Markov's inequality). *For a random variable* $Z$ *and* $\varepsilon > 0$

$$
\mathbb{P}(Z \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[Z^2\right]}{\varepsilon^2}. \tag{22.42}
$$

*Proof.*

$$
\mathbb{P}(Z \ge \varepsilon) \le \mathbb{P}\left(Z^2 \ge \varepsilon^2\right) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[Z^2\right]}{\varepsilon^2}, \tag{22.43}
$$

where the last step is by Markov's inequality. $\square$

The second order oracle bound is defined in terms of tandem loss, which is defined next.

**Definition 22.3** (Tandem loss). *The* tandem loss *of* $\theta$ *and* $\theta'$ *on* $(x, y)$ *is defined by*

$$
\ell(f(x, \theta), f(x, \theta'), y) = \mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta') \neq y). \tag{22.44}
$$

*The tandem loss counts an error if both* $\theta$ *and* $\theta'$ *erred on* $(x, y)$. *The* expected tandem loss *is defined by*

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta') = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p(x,y)}[\ell(f(x, \theta), f(x, \theta'), y)]. \tag{22.45}
$$

*And the* empirical tandem loss *is defined by*

$$
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \theta', \mathcal{D}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N \ell(f(x_n, \theta), f(x_n, \theta'), y_n). \tag{22.46}
$$

The tandem loss has the following property.

**Lemma 22.6.**

$$
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p(x,y)}\left[(\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\rho}[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)])^2\right] = \mathbb{E}_{(\theta,\theta')\sim\rho^2}[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta')], \tag{22.47}
$$

*where* $\rho^2$ *is a product distribution on* $\Theta \times \Theta$.

The property relates the tandem loss to $\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)]$, which appears in the basic bound on the expected loss of weighted majority vote in Lemma 22.5 and will be used shortly. But before it is used, a proof of the lemma is provided.

*Proof.*

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\left[\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)]^2\right] &= \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}[\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)]\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)]] && (22.48) \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta') \neq y)]\right] && (22.49) \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta') \neq y)]\right] && (22.50) \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta')]. && (22.51)
\end{aligned}
$$

$\square$

Combining Lemma 22.5, Theorem 22.8, and Lemma 22.6 leads to the following result.

**Theorem 22.9** (Second order oracle bound).

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) \le 4\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta')] \tag{22.52}
$$

*Proof.* Take a random variable $Z = \mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)]$ in Lemma 22.5, plug it into Theorem 22.8, and apply Lemma 22.6 to $\mathbb{E}\left[Z^2\right]$. $\square$

A major advantage of the second order oracle bound over the first order oracle bound is that it takes correlations of errors into account through the tandem loss. Therefore, the weighting distribution $\rho$ obtained by minimization of the corresponding second order empirical bound typically does not deteriorate the expected loss of the resulting majority vote $\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho)$. A disadvantage is that in many cases the factor 4 in the bound is typically loose (typically $\mathrm{MV}_\rho(x)$ performs better than the corresponding tandem classifier rather than within factor 4 worse) and as a bound (rather than as an optimization objective) it is typically weaker than the first order bound.

Wu et al. proposed further refinement of the bound by replacing second order Markov's inequality with variational Chebyshev-Cantelli inequality.

**Theorem 22.10** (Variational Chebyshev-Cantelli oracle bound). *For all* $\rho$:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) \le \min_{\mu < 0.5}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta')] - 2\mu\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)] + \mu^2}{(0.5 - \mu)^2}\right). \tag{22.53}
$$

The reader is referred to their paper for further details and an additional alternative form of the bound based on tandem losses with an offset. Additional improvements were proposed by Wu and Seldin and Wu et al.

For binary classification Germain et al. and Masegosa et al. provide an alternative way of writing $\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta')]$, as a difference between $\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]$ and average disagreements between predictions. Since in binary classification disagreements can be estimated using unlabeled data only, this approach can benefit from large amounts of unlabeled data. However, for a noticeable advantage, the amount of unlabeled data should be more than square the amount of labeled data. See Masegosa et al. for more details.

### Comparison of the oracle bounds

A brief comparison of the first and second order oracle bounds follows.

**A positively correlated errors case.** Since $\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta')] \le \mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]$, the second order oracle bound is at most twice worse than the first order oracle bound. The worst case occurs, for example, if all parameters $\theta$ in $\Theta$ give identical predictions. Then $\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta')] = \mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)] = \mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho)$ for all $\rho$ and the first order bound has a factor 2 slack, whereas the second order bound has a factor 4 slack.

**A negatively correlated errors case.** Imagine that $|\Theta| = M$ is finite of size $M \ge 3$, such that each set of parameters $\theta \in \Theta$ errs on $1/M$ of the sample space (according to the distribution $p(x, y)$) and that the error regions are disjoint. Then $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta) = 1/M$ for all $\theta$ and $\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta') = 0$ for all $\theta \neq \theta'$. For a uniform distribution $\rho$ on $\Theta$ the first order bound is $2\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)] = 2/M$ and the second order bound is $4\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta')] = 4/M^2$ and $\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) = 0$. In this case the second order bound is an order of magnitude tighter than the first order.

**An uncorrelated errors case.** Assume that all parameter sets $\theta \in \Theta$ produce independent errors and have the same error rate, $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}(\theta')$ for all $\theta$ and $\theta'$. Then for $\theta \neq \theta'$, $\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta') = \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta') \neq y)] = \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta) \neq y)]\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}[\mathbf{1}(f(x, \theta') \neq y)] = \mathcal{L}(\theta)^2$ and $\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta) = \mathcal{L}(\theta)$. For a uniform distribution $\rho$ the second order bound is $4\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta')] = 4\left(\mathcal{L}(\theta)^2 + \frac{1}{M}\mathcal{L}(\theta)(1 - \mathcal{L}(\theta))\right)$ and the first order bound is $2\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)] = 2\mathcal{L}(\theta)$. Assuming that $M$ is large, so that the second term in the second order bound can be ignored, it follows that it is tighter for $\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)] < 1/2$ and looser otherwise. The former is the interesting regime.

## 4. Empirical bounds for the weighted majority vote

Empirical bounds for the weighted majority vote are obtained from the oracle bounds in a straightforward manner by applying any applicable PAC-Bayes bound to $\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]$ or $\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta')]$, depending on the order. Below is an example based on the optimization-friendly form of the PAC-Bayesian bound in Theorem 22.2.

**Theorem 22.11** (First order empirical bound). *Assume that* $\ell$ *is the zero-one loss function. For any prior distribution* $\pi$ *over* $\Theta$ *that is independent of* $\mathcal{D}$ *and any* $\delta \in (0, 1]$

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(\forall \rho : \mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) \le 2\min_{\lambda \in (0,2)}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]}{1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}} + \frac{\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}}{\lambda\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}\right)N}\right)\right) \ge 1 - \delta. \tag{22.54}
$$

**Theorem 22.12** (Second order empirical bound). *Assume that* $\ell$ *is the zero-one loss function. For any prior distribution* $\pi$ *over* $\Theta$ *that is independent of* $\mathcal{D}$ *and any* $\delta \in (0, 1]$

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(\forall \rho : \mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) \le 4\min_{\lambda \in (0,2)}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \theta', \mathcal{D})\right]}{1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}} + \frac{2\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\delta}}{\lambda\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}\right)N}\right)\right) \ge 1 - \delta. \tag{22.55}
$$

Theorem 22.11 is obtained from Theorem 22.7 by applying Theorem 22.2 to $\mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathcal{L}(\theta)]$. Theorem 22.12 is obtained from Theorem 22.9 by applying Theorem 22.2 to $\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \theta')]$ and using the fact that $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho^2\middle\|\pi^2\right) = 2\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)$ (see Germain et al.).

Note that it is also possible to use Theorem 22.3 instead of Theorem 22.2 to obtain tighter bounds, but, as already mentioned, $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}$ is less convenient for optimization. A common practice is to use Theorem 22.11 or 22.12 to optimize $\rho$, and then to plug the optimized $\rho$ into a $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{kl}}$-based bound to obtain a tighter generalization certificate (which is a valid procedure, because Theorem 22.3 holds for all $\rho$, including $\rho$ obtained by optimization of any other bound).

### Comparison of the empirical bounds

On top of the differences of the underlying oracle bounds discussed earlier, empirical bounds add some practical considerations. In general, $\mathbb{E}_{\rho^2}\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \theta', \mathcal{D})\right] \le \mathbb{E}_\rho\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right]$, thus the second order bound benefits from lower empirical risk and faster convergence rates (refer to the discussion of "fast convergence rates" in Theorem 22.1, where it was mentioned that low empirical risk reduces the impact of the square root term). However, the second order bound has $2\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)$ in the complexity term compared to $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi)$ in the first order bound. Additionally, in cross-validation and bagging-style applications it is data-efficient to use out-of-bag samples for the computation of the bounds. But since the tandem losses must be estimated on overlaps of the corresponding out-of-bag samples, there are less data for their estimation compared to out-of-bag samples available for estimation of plain losses. Therefore, the effective sample size $\mathcal{D}$ in Theorem 22.12 is smaller than in Theorem 22.11. This way of ensemble and bound construction is discussed in more detail in the next section. Masegosa et al. show that empirically as an objective for optimizing $\rho$ the second order bound most often produces majority votes with lower expected risk $\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho)$ than the first order bound. However, the first order bound often produces tighter generalization guarantees. A practical possibility is to use the second order bound to find a good $\rho$ and then plug it into the first order bound to get a generalization guarantee, because the first order bound holds for all posteriors, including those produced by other methods.

## 5. PAC-Bayesian deep ensembles

There are numerous ways to construct prediction ensembles and various options for the choice of ensemble members, such as decision stumps, decision trees, or even ensembles of heterogeneous classifiers. This book focuses on ensembles of deep neural networks, see [Chapter 19](/blog/ensembles-and-approximate-bayesian-inference) for an introduction.

Combining deep neural networks into an ensemble model is a common and old approach to improving generalization performance. The most basic way to create an ensemble of $M$ neural networks is to repeat the training process $M$ times using all training data and to combine the resulting networks uniformly. This procedure can be viewed as a simple ensembling, where random weight initializations and stochastic training (randomly composed mini-batches, randomized augmentations) are assumed to generate sufficiently diverse ensemble members. Although this approach is often explicitly referred to as non-Bayesian (see, e.g., Lakshminarayanan et al., Ovadia et al., Wenzel et al.), the resulting ensemble may also be interpreted as a Bayesian model average from an approximate posterior, see [Chapter 19](/blog/ensembles-and-approximate-bayesian-inference).

Uniform weighting is an easy, but not necessarily the best way to combine predictions. Optimization of the weights has the potential to yield higher accuracy, as already pointed out in early works on ensembles. The weights can be optimized using prior information and empirical performance of ensemble members.

The idea of using PAC-Bayesian bounds as optimization objectives for optimizing the weighting $\rho$ has been floating around for a long time, however, as discussed earlier and demonstrated empirically in Masegosa et al., the first order bounds are unsuitable for this purpose, because they completely ignore (un)correlation of predictions, which is the main source of power for the majority vote. Therefore, meaningful optimization of $\rho$ is only possible based on second or higher-order bounds.

### 5.1 Construction of an ensemble

While the bounds presented above apply equally to finite and infinite sets $\Theta$, computationally it is easier to work with finite sets. Infinite sets require approximations or highly restricted parametric models allowing closed-form calculation of the relevant integrals. A finite set $\Theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_M)$ of prediction models is chosen here, and the weighting of the models in an ensemble is then optimized by minimizing a PAC-Bayes bound. Thiemann et al. have noted as long as the empirical risk estimates are unbiased, $\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \mathcal{D})\right] = \mathcal{L}(\theta)$, it is possible to use different (sub)sets of the data to compute the empirical risk of different models $\theta_i$ in PAC-Bayesian bounds. For example, in cross-validation or bagging a data point $(x, y) \in \mathcal{D}$ can be used in training some models in $\Theta$ and computing the empirical risk of the models that have not used it in training. (Seldin provides several exercises playing around with this approach.) To formalize this idea, let $\mathcal{D}_i$ denote the set of data that were *not* used for training model $\theta_i$. Then $\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_i, \mathcal{D}_i)\right] = \mathcal{L}(\theta_i)$. Further, $\mathcal{D}_{i,j} = \mathcal{D}_i \cap \mathcal{D}_j$ denotes the set of data that were *not* used for training of neither $\theta_i$ nor $\theta_j$. Then $\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_i, \theta_j, \mathcal{D}_{i,j})\right] = \mathcal{L}(\theta_i, \theta_j)$. Let $K = \min_{i,j}|\mathcal{D}_i \cap \mathcal{D}_j|$ denote the minimal overlap size and let $\hat{\mathcal{L}} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times M}$ denote the matrix of empirical tandem losses with entries $\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j} = \hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_i, \theta_j, \mathcal{D}_{i,j})$. Theorem 22.12 undergoes a slight adjustment to accommodate the use of individualized sets of data for computation of empirical risks.

**Theorem 22.13** (Second order empirical bound with individualized empirical risk estimators). *Assume that* $\ell$ *is the zero-one loss function. Let* $\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_M)^\top$ *and* $\rho = (\rho_1, \dots, \rho_M)^\top$ *be prior and posterior distributions on* $\{1, \dots, M\}$, *where* $\pi$ *is independent of* $\{\mathcal{D}_1, \dots, \mathcal{D}_M\}$. *Assume that* $\theta_i$ *is independent of* $\mathcal{D}_i$. *Then for any* $\delta \in (0, 1]$

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(\forall \rho : \mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) \le 4\min_{\lambda \in (0,2)}\left(\frac{\rho^\top\hat{\mathcal{L}}\rho}{1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}} + \frac{2\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{K}}{\delta}}{\lambda\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}\right)K}\right)\right) \ge 1 - \delta, \tag{22.56}
$$

*where* $K$ *and* $\hat{\mathcal{L}}$ *are defined above.*

In other words, with probability at least $1 - \delta$, for all $\rho$ and all $\lambda \in (0, 2)$

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{MV}_\rho) \le 4\left(\frac{\rho^\top\hat{\mathcal{L}}\rho}{1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}} + \frac{2\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{K}}{\delta}}{\lambda\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}\right)K}\right). \tag{22.57}
$$

### 5.2 Optimizing ensemble weighting through minimization of a second-order PAC-Bayesian bound

Similar to Theorem 22.2, the bound in Theorem 22.13 (the right hand side of equation (22.57)) is convex in $\lambda$ for a fixed $\rho$. If the $\mathcal{D}_{i,j}$ are identical for all $(i, j)$ pairs, then the bound is also convex in $\rho$ for a fixed $\lambda$, however, if the $\mathcal{D}_{i,j}$ differ, convexity in $\rho$ is not guaranteed (see Masegosa et al.). The weighting $\rho$ for the majority vote is optimized by alternating minimization of the right hand side of the bound in equation (22.57) with respect to $\lambda$ and with respect to $\rho$. For $\lambda$ the exact minimization step given earlier in equation (22.6) is used. Namely, for a fixed $\rho$ take

$$
\lambda_\rho = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\frac{2K\rho^\top\hat{\mathcal{L}}\rho}{2\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\pi) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{K}}{\delta}} + 1} + 1}. \tag{22.58}
$$

For a fixed $\lambda$, $\rho$ is updated by taking a gradient descent step, where the gradient $\nabla$ of the right hand side of equation (22.57) is given by

$$
\nabla = \frac{4}{1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}}\left(2\hat{\mathcal{L}}\rho + \frac{2}{\lambda K}\left(1 + \ln\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)\right), \tag{22.59}
$$

where $\ln\frac{\rho}{\pi} = \left(\ln\frac{\rho_1}{\pi_1}, \dots, \ln\frac{\rho_M}{\pi_M}\right)^\top$.

In practice, $\rho$ is represented by unconstrained parameters $\rho'$ using a differentiable mapping from $\mathbb{R}^M$ to the probability simplex $\Delta_M$, and the derivative of the objective w.r.t. $\rho'$ is considered. This allows unconstrained optimization to be applied. For example, in the experiments below, the softmax function and an Rprop variant are used for gradient-based unconstrained optimization.

Algorithm 25 summarizes the minimization procedure. Since the optimization problem is low-dimensional ($\rho \in \mathbb{R}^M$), the algorithm quickly converges to a local minimum. Theorem 22.13 provides a generalization guarantee for the ensemble produced by Algorithm 25.

**Algorithm 25** Alternating minimization of the bound in Theorem 22.13

```text
Input:  Matrix of empirical tandem losses L̂, prior π, size K of the smallest set 𝒟ᵢ,ⱼ, stopping criterion
        parameter T_ρ
Output: optimized ρ and λ
ρ ← (1/M) 1_M                                        // 1_M is the M-dimensional vector of all ones
// Update λ using current ρ
λ ← 2 ( √( 2Kρᵀ L̂ ρ / (2 D_KL(ρ‖π) + ln(2√K/δ)) + 1 ) + 1 )⁻¹
while the objective ρᵀ L̂ ρ / (1 − λ/2) + (2 D_KL(ρ‖π) + ln(2√K/δ)) / (λ(1 − λ/2)K) decreases do
    // Update ρ using current λ
    repeat
        ∇ = 4/(1 − λ/2) · ( 2 L̂ ρ + 2/(λK) · (1 + ln(ρ/π)) )
        Update ρ by an iteration of gradient descent using ∇
    until the objective ρᵀ L̂ ρ / (1 − λ/2) + (2 D_KL(ρ‖π) + ln(2√K/δ)) / (λ(1 − λ/2)K) does not improve for T_ρ steps
    // Update λ using current ρ
    λ ← 2 ( √( 2Kρᵀ L̂ ρ / (2 D_KL(ρ‖π) + ln(2√K/δ)) + 1 ) + 1 )⁻¹
```

### 5.3 Empirical example

To illustrate optimization of ensemble weighting via minimization of the bound in Theorem 22.13, the approach is applied to stacking CNN LSTMs (models combining Convolutional Neural Networks and Long Short-Term Memory) to solve the IMDb binary classification benchmark.

The IMDb data are commonly used to evaluate deep ensembles. An experimental setup similar to Wenzel et al. was adopted. The standard training protocol for the IMDb data set includes the use of a validation data set to mitigate overfitting. Neural networks are trained on the training data set, and the model parameters that yield the lowest error on the validation data set are selected as the final model. The experiments used 20,000 training samples and 5,000 random validation samples. The test set included all 25,000 samples from the original test set.

Three ensembling strategies were compared. For that, $N_{\text{trials}}$ networks were trained for $N_{\text{epochs}}$ epochs and the validation error was computed after each epoch using the model parameters at that point of the training process, also referred to as a model checkpoint. In the first method, referred to as $f^{\text{val}}_{\text{uniform}}$, the model checkpoint from the epoch with the lowest validation error was taken for each of the $N_{\text{trials}}$ training trials, ending up with an ensemble of $M = N_{\text{trials}}$ networks. These were uniformly combined into a deep ensemble. In the second method, $f^{\text{all}}_{\text{uniform}}$, all the model checkpoints from all trials were taken, resulting in $M = N_{\text{trials}} \times N_{\text{epochs}}$ models in the ensemble. They were combined uniformly, disregarding the validation set. In the third method, $f^{\text{all}}_\rho$, the weighting of the ensemble from the previous method was optimized using Algorithm 25 and the validation set. The results, averaged over five repetitions, are shown in Figure 22.1. The uniform ensemble of the best checkpoints according to the validation error ($f^{\text{val}}_{\text{uniform}}$) performed better than taking all checkpoints from each trial ($f^{\text{all}}_{\text{uniform}}$), as expected when using validation data for model selection. However, after optimizing the weights of the uniform ensemble using Algorithm 25 ($f^{\text{all}}_\rho$), the resulting ensemble classifier showed the lowest test error, even lower than the uniform ensemble of the best checkpoints ($f^{\text{val}}_{\text{uniform}}$).

The example shows that using validation data for optimizing a second-order PAC-Bayesian bound does not only provide a performance guarantee, but can also lead to a better performing ensemble.

### 5.4 Ensemble pruning

The PAC-Bayesian bounds can further be used for ensemble pruning. After optimization of $\rho$ using a second order bound, pruning can be easily done by zeroing the lowest values of the optimized $\rho(\theta)$ and renormalizing $\rho$. Since PAC-Bayesian bounds hold for all posterior distributions $\rho$, including those obtained by pruning, they provide a rigorous data-dependent trade-off between sparsity and accuracy of the pruned majority votes.
